DEAD SOULS

VOLUME ONE

Chapter One

A rather handsome little spring carriage, the kind used by bachelors: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners with about a hundred peasant souls, in a word, all those known as middling gentlemen, drove through the gates of the inn in the provincial town of NN. In the carriage sat a gentleman who was neither handsome nor bad-looking, neither too fat nor too thin; one could not say he was old, but he was not exactly young either. His arrival caused absolutely no stir in the town and was not accompanied by anything special; only two Russian peasants standing by the doors of a tavern opposite the inn made a few remarks, which, however, referred more to the carriage than to the person sitting in it. "Look at that," said one to the other, "what a wheel! What do you think, would that wheel make it to Moscow if it had to, or not?" "It would," answered the other. "And to Kazan, I think it wouldn't make it?" "To Kazan it wouldn't make it," answered the other. That was the end of the conversation. And also, when the carriage drove up to the inn, a young man was encountered wearing white twill trousers, very narrow and short, and a frock coat with pretensions to fashion, beneath which was visible a shirtfront fastened with a Tula pin bearing a bronze pistol. The young man turned around, looked at the carriage, held his cap with his hand, which had almost flown off from the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage drove into the yard, the gentleman was met by a tavern servant, or a waiter, as they are called in Russian taverns, so lively and fidgety that it was even impossible to make out what his face looked like. He ran out briskly, with a napkin in his hand, all long and wearing a long demicotoon frock coat with its back almost at the very nape of his neck, shook his hair and briskly led the gentleman up the entire wooden gallery to show him the room sent to him by God. The room was of a known sort, for the inn was also of a known sort, that is, exactly such as inns are in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day travelers receive a peaceful room with cockroaches peeking out like prunes from all the corners, and a door into the adjoining room, always blocked by a chest of drawers, where a neighbor settles in, a silent and calm person, but extremely curious, interested in knowing all the details about the traveler. The exterior facade of the inn corresponded to its interior: it was very long, two stories high; the lower one was not stuccoed and remained in dark red bricks, darkened still more by fierce weather changes and already dirty in themselves; the upper one was painted with eternal yellow paint; below were little shops with horse collars, ropes and bagels. In the corner shop of these, or, better, in the window, sat a sbiten seller with a samovar of red copper and a face as red as the samovar itself, so that from a distance one might think there were two samovars standing in the window, if one samovar did not have a beard as black as pitch.

While the visiting gentleman was inspecting his room, his belongings were brought in: first of all a suitcase of white leather, somewhat worn, showing that it was not the first time it had been on the road. The suitcase was brought in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a little sheepskin coat, and the lackey Petrushka, a fellow of about thirty, in a roomy secondhand frock coat, evidently from a master's shoulder, a fellow somewhat stern in appearance, with very large lips and nose. After the suitcase was brought in a small mahogany box with inlaid pieces of Karelian birch, shoe lasts and a roast chicken wrapped in blue paper. When all this had been brought in, the coachman Selifan went to the stable to fuss over the horses, and the lackey Petrushka began to settle in a small front room, a very dark little cubby, where he had already managed to drag his overcoat and along with it a certain smell of his own,which had been announced and the bag with various lackey's toiletries brought after it. In this cubbyhole he fitted a narrow three-legged bed against the wall, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, beaten and flat as a pancake, and perhaps just as greasy as a pancake, which he had managed to demand from the innkeeper.

While the servants were managing and bustling about, the gentleman went off to the common room. What these common rooms are like — every traveler knows very well: the same walls, painted with oil paint, darkened at the top from pipe smoke and polished below by the backs of various travelers, and even more so by the local merchants, for on trading days merchants came here in groups of six and seven to drink their famous pot of tea; the same soot-blackened ceiling; the same smoky chandelier with a multitude of hanging glass pieces that jumped and jingled every time the waiter ran across the worn oilcloths, briskly waving his tray, on which sat as many tea cups as birds on a seashore; the same pictures covering the entire wall, painted in oils — in a word, everything the same as everywhere else; the only difference being that in one picture a nymph was depicted with such enormous breasts as the reader has surely never seen. Such a play of nature, however, does happen in various historical paintings, brought to us in Russia no one knows when, from where, or by whom, sometimes even by our noblemen, lovers of art, who bought them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who brought them. The gentleman took off his cap and unwound from his neck a woolen scarf of rainbow colors, such as a wife prepares with her own hands for her married husband, supplying him with proper instructions on how to wrap himself, but for bachelors — I certainly cannot say who makes them, God knows, I have never worn such scarves. Having unwound the scarf, the gentleman ordered dinner to be served. While various dishes usual in taverns were being served to him, such as: cabbage soup with a puff pastry pie, specially saved for travelers for several weeks, brains with green peas, sausages with cabbage, roast chicken, pickled cucumber, and the eternal sweet puff pastry pie, always ready to serve; while all this was being served to him, both reheated and simply cold, he made the servant, or waiter, tell all sorts of nonsense — about who had formerly kept the tavern and who kept it now, and how much income it brought in, and whether their master was a great scoundrel; to which the waiter, as usual, replied: "Oh, a great one, sir, a swindler." As in enlightened Europe, so in enlightened Russia there are now very many respectable people who cannot eat in a tavern without talking to the servant, and sometimes even amusingly joking at his expense. However, the visitor did not ask all idle questions; he inquired with extreme precision who in the town was the governor, who was the chairman of the chamber, who was the prosecutor — in a word, he did not skip a single significant official; but with even greater precision, if not with participation, he inquired about all the significant landowners: how many souls of peasants each had, how far each lived from the town, what even their character was and how often they came to town; he inquired attentively about the state of the region: whether there had been any illnesses in their province — epidemic fevers, deadly fevers of some sort, smallpox, and the like, and all so thoroughly and with such precision that showed more than mere simple curiosity. In his manners the gentleman had something solid and blew his nose extremely loudly. It is unknown how he did it, but only his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, completely innocent merit gained him, however, much respect on the part of the tavern servant, so that every time he heard this sound, he shook his hair, straightened up more respectfully, and, bending his head down from on high, asked: did he need anything? After dinner the gentleman drank a cup of coffee and sat down on the sofa, putting a cushion behind his back, which in Russian taverns is stuffed, instead of with elastic wool, with something extremely similar to brick and cobblestone. Then he began to yawn and ordered himself to be taken to his room, where, having lain down, he slept for two hours. Having rested,he wrote on a scrap of paper, at the request of the tavern servant, his rank, first name and surname, for reporting to the proper authorities, to the police. On the paper the waiter, coming down the stairs, read syllable by syllable the following: "Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, on his own business." While the waiter was still deciphering the note syllable by syllable, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself set off to look over the town, with which he seemed to be satisfied, for he found that the town was in no way inferior to other provincial towns: the yellow paint on the stone houses struck the eye strongly, and the gray on the wooden ones modestly darkened. The houses were of one, two, and one-and-a-half stories, with the eternal mezzanine, very beautiful, in the opinion of the provincial architects. In places these houses seemed lost amid a street wide as a field and endless wooden fences; in places they huddled together, and here there was noticeably more movement of people and liveliness. There came across signboards, almost washed away by rain, with pretzels and boots, here and there with painted blue trousers and the signature of some tailor from Warsaw; where a shop with caps, peaked caps and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fedorov"; where a billiard table was painted with two players in tailcoats, of the kind worn in our theaters by guests entering the stage in the last act. The players were depicted with cues aimed, arms somewhat turned back and crooked legs, having just performed an entrechat in the air. Under all this was written: "And here is the establishment." In places simple tables stood right on the street with nuts, soap and gingerbread resembling soap; where a cookshop with a painted fat fish and a fork stuck into it. Most often of all, darkened double-headed state eagles were noticeable, which have now already been replaced by the laconic inscription: "Drinking House." The pavement was poor everywhere. He also looked into the town garden, which consisted of thin little trees that had taken root badly, with supports at the bottom in the form of triangles, very prettily painted with green oil paint. However, although these little trees were no taller than a reed, it had been said of them in the newspapers, in describing an illumination, that "our town has been adorned, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, wide-branching trees, giving coolness on a hot day," and that at the same time "it was very touching to see how the hearts of the citizens trembled in an excess of gratitude and streamed forth floods of tears as a sign of thankfulness to his honor the town governor." Having questioned the watchman in detail as to where one could go more directly, if need be, to the cathedral, to the government offices, to the governor, he set off to look at the river flowing through the middle of the town, on the way tore off a poster nailed to a post, so that, on coming home, he might read it carefully, looked intently at a lady of not bad appearance passing along the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in a military livery with a little bundle in his hand, and, once more casting his eyes over everything, as if to remember well the position of the place, he went home straight to his room, lightly supported on the stairs by the tavern servant. Having drunk his fill of tea, he sat down before the table, ordered a candle to be brought to him, took the poster out of his pocket, held it up to the candle and began to read, screwing up his right eye a little. However, there was not much that was remarkable in the little poster: a drama by Mr. Kotzebue was being given, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplevin, Cora by Miss Zyablova, the other characters were even less remarkable; however, he read them all, got even to the price of the pit and learned that the poster had been printed in the typography of the provincial government, then turned it over to the other side: to find out whether there was anything there too, but, having found nothing, he wiped his eyes, rolled it up neatly and put it in his little box, where he had the habit of putting everything that came his way. The day, it seems, was concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour cabbage soup and a sound sleep in full snoring swing, as they say in certain parts of the vast Russian state.

The whole of the following day was devoted to visits; the newcomer set off to pay visits to all the town dignitaries. He paid his respects to the governor, who, as it turned out, like Chichikov, was neither fat,He was not particularly handsome, but he had an Anna on his neck, and it was even rumored that he had been nominated for a star;[ A star — the Order of St. Stanislav.

] however, he was a great good-natured fellow and even sometimes embroidered on tulle himself. Then he went to the vice-governor, then he was at the prosecutor's, at the chairman of the chamber's, at the police chief's, at the tax farmer's, at the head of the state factories… it is a pity that it is somewhat difficult to remember all the powers that be; but it is enough to say that the visitor displayed extraordinary activity with regard to visits: he even appeared to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical board and the city architect. And then he still sat for a long time in the britzka, thinking to whom else he might pay a visit, but there were no more officials to be found in the city. In conversations with these potentates he was very skillfully able to flatter each one. To the governor he hinted somehow in passing that when you enter his province it is like entering paradise, the roads everywhere are like velvet, and that those governments which appoint wise dignitaries deserve great praise. To the police chief he said something very flattering about the city's watchmen; and in conversations with the vice-governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were still only state councilors, he even said twice by mistake: "Your Excellency," which pleased them very much. The consequence of this was that the governor extended him an invitation to come to his home party that same day, and the other officials likewise, each in his own way, some to dinner, some to a little game of boston, some for a cup of tea.

About himself the visitor, it seemed, avoided speaking much; if he did speak, it was in some sort of commonplaces, with noticeable modesty, and his conversation in such cases took on somewhat bookish turns: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and did not deserve to have much concern made over him, that he had experienced much in his lifetime, endured much in service for the truth, had many enemies who had even attempted his life, and that now, wishing to be at peace, he was seeking finally to choose a place of residence, and that, having arrived in this city, he considered it an indispensable duty to pay his respects to its first dignitaries. That was all that was learned in the city about this new person, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor's party. The preparation for this party took up more than two hours, and here the visitor displayed such attention to his toilet as is not to be seen everywhere. After a brief after-dinner nap he ordered water to wash with and for an extremely long time rubbed soap on both cheeks, bracing them from inside with his tongue; then, taking a towel from the shoulder of the tavern servant, he wiped his full face with it from all sides, beginning from behind the ears and snorting a couple of times right into the tavern servant's face. Then he put on his shirtfront before the mirror, plucked out two hairs that had come out of his nose, and immediately after that found himself in a cranberry-colored frock coat with a sparkle. Having dressed himself thus, he rolled off in his own carriage along the endlessly wide streets, lit by the meager illumination of lamps flickering here and there. However, the governor's house was so brightly lit that it might have been for a ball; a carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes before the entrance, the outrider's shouts in the distance — in a word, everything as it should be. Entering the hall, Chichikov had to shut his eyes for a moment, because the brilliance from the candles, the lamps, and the ladies' dresses was terrific. Everything was flooded with light. Black frock coats flickered and darted about separately and in clusters here and there, like flies darting about on a white gleaming sugar loaf in the heat of July summer, when an old housekeeper chops and divides it into sparkling chunks before an open window; the children all gaze, gathered around, curiously following the movements of her tough hands as they raise the hammer, while the aerial squadrons of flies, lifted by the light air, fly in boldly, like full masters, and, taking advantage of the old woman's poor eyesight and the sun that bothers her eyes, sprinkle themselves over the tasty chunks, here scattered, there in thick clusters. Sated with rich summer, which even without that sets out tasty dishes at every step, they flew in not at all to eat, but only to show themselves, to stroll back and forth over the sugar heap, to rub one another's hind or front legs,or scratch themselves under their little wings with them, or, stretching out both front paws, rub them over their heads, turn around and fly away again, and fly back again with new annoying squadrons. Chichikov had not managed to look around before he was already seized by the arm by the governor, who immediately presented him to the governor's wife. The visiting guest did not disgrace himself here either: he said some compliment, quite proper for a middle-aged man of a rank neither too high nor too low. When the established pairs of dancers pressed everyone against the wall, he, having put his hands behind his back, looked at them for a couple of minutes very attentively. Many ladies were well dressed and in fashion, others had dressed in whatever God had sent to a provincial town. The men here, as everywhere, were of two kinds: some thin, who were all hovering around the ladies; some of them were of a sort that could with difficulty be distinguished from Petersburg ones, they also had sideburns very deliberately and tastefully combed, or simply handsome, very smoothly shaven ovals of faces, also carelessly drew close to the ladies, also spoke French and made the ladies laugh just as in Petersburg. The other kind of men consisted of fat ones or the same as Chichikov, that is, not so as to be too fat, yet not thin either. These, on the contrary, squinted and backed away from the ladies and looked only to the sides, whether the governor's servant was setting up a green table for whist somewhere. Their faces were full and round, some even had warts, some were also pockmarked, they did not wear their hair on their heads either in forelocks, or in curls, or in the manner of "devil take me," as the French say - their hair was either cut low or slicked down, and their facial features were more rounded and firm. These were the honored officials in the town. Alas! fat ones know better how to manage their affairs in this world than thin ones. Thin ones serve more on special commissions or merely are listed and dodge hither and thither; their existence is somehow too easy, airy, and completely unreliable. Fat ones, however, never occupy indirect places, but all direct ones, and if they do sit down somewhere, they will sit reliably and firmly, so that the place will sooner crack and sag under them, but they will not fly off. They do not love outward brilliance; their tailcoat is not so deftly cut as on thin ones, but in their caskets there is the grace of God. A thin one in three years does not have a single soul left not mortgaged to the loan bank; a fat one is calm, and lo, somewhere at the end of town a house has appeared, bought in his wife's name, then at the other end another house, then near the town a little village, then also a village with all its lands. Finally the fat one, having served God and the sovereign, having earned universal respect, leaves the service, moves and becomes a landowner, a glorious Russian gentleman, a hospitable host, and lives, and lives well. And after him again thin heirs squander, according to Russian custom, on post horses all their father's goods. It cannot be concealed that almost this kind of reflection occupied Chichikov at the time when he was examining society, and the consequence of this was that he finally joined the fat ones, where he met almost all familiar faces: the prosecutor with very black thick eyebrows and a left eye that blinked somewhat, as if to say: "Let's go, brother, to the other room, there I'll tell you something," - a man, however, serious and silent; the postmaster, a short man, but a wit and philosopher; the chairman of the chamber, a very sensible and amiable man, - who all greeted him as an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov bowed somewhat sideways, though not without pleasantness. Right there he also became acquainted with the very courteous and polite landowner Manilov and the somewhat awkward-looking Sobakevich, who at the first moment stepped on his foot, saying: "I beg your pardon." Right there they also thrust a card for whist at him, which he accepted with the same polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not get up until supper. All conversations completely ceased, as always happens when people finally give themselves over to a businesslike occupation. Although the postmaster was very talkative, even he, having taken the cards in his hands,At the same hour, he expressed a thoughtful physiognomy on his face,

The next day Chichikov went to dinner and an evening party at the police chief's, where from three o'clock after dinner they sat down to whist and played until two o'clock at night. There, among others, he made the acquaintance of the landowner Nozdryov, a man of about thirty, a lively fellow, who after three or four words began addressing him as "thou." With the police chief and the prosecutor Nozdryov was also on "thou" terms and addressed them in a friendly manner; but when they sat down to play a big game, the police chief and the prosecutor examined his bids with extreme attention and followed almost every card he played. The next day Chichikov spent the evening at the chairman of the chamber's, who received his guests in a dressing gown, somewhat greasy, and among them two ladies of some sort. Then he was at an evening party at the vice-governor's, at a big dinner at the tax farmer's, at a small dinner at the prosecutor's, which, incidentally, cost as much as a big one; at a luncheon after the liturgy given by the city head, which also cost as much as a dinner. In a word, he did not have a single hour to remain at home, and he came to the hotel only to sleep. The newcomer somehow knew how to find himself in everything and showed himself to be an experienced man of the world. Whatever the conversation was about, he always knew how to support it: whether the talk was about a horse farm, he also spoke about a horse farm; whether they spoke of good dogs, and here too he made very pertinent remarks; whether they discussed an investigation conducted by the government chamber - he showed that he was not unfamiliar with judicial tricks either; whether there was a discussion about billiards - and in billiards he did not miss his mark; whether they spoke of virtue, and of virtue he reasoned very well, even with tears in his eyes; about the distilling of hot wine, and in hot wine he knew his business; about customs inspectors and officials, and of them he judged as though he himself had been both an official and an inspector. But what was remarkable was that he knew how to clothe all this in a certain sedateness, knew how to carry himself well. He spoke neither loudly nor softly, but exactly as one should. In a word, whichever way you turned, he was a very decent man. All the officials were pleased with the arrival of the new person. The governor explained about him that he was a well-intentioned man; the prosecutor - that he was a capable man; the gendarme colonel said that he was a learned man; the chairman of the chamber - that he was a knowledgeable and respectable man; the police chief - that he was a respectable and amiable man; the police chief's wife - that he was the most amiable and courteous man. Even Sobakevich himself, who rarely spoke well of anyone, having arrived rather late from town and already completely undressed and lain down on the bed beside his thin wife, said to her: "I, my dear, was at the governor's evening party, and dined at the police chief's, and made the acquaintance of collegiate counselor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov: a most agreeable man!" To which his spouse replied: "Hm!" - and pushed him with her foot.

Such an opinion, very flattering for the guest, was formed about him in the city, and it held until such time as one strange property of the guest and an enterprise, or, as they say in the provinces, a passage, about which the reader will soon learn, brought almost the entire city into complete bewilderment.

Chapter Two

For more than a week the visiting gentleman had been living in the city, driving about to evening parties and dinners and thus spending, as they say, a very pleasant time. Finally he decided to transfer his visits beyond the city and call on the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps he was prompted to this by another, more substantial reason, a matter more serious, closer to his heart... But the reader will learn about all this gradually and in due time, if only he has the patience to read the proposed tale, which is very long and is destined afterward to expand wider and more spaciously as it approaches the end that crowns the matter. The coachman Selifan was given the order early in the morning to harness the horses to the familiar britzka; Petrushka was ordered to remain at home, to look after the room and the suitcase. It will not be superfluous for the reader to make the acquaintance of these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not such noticeable persons, and that,What is called secondary or even tertiary, although the main moves and springs of the poem are not established on them and only here and there touch and lightly hook them, - but the author loves exceedingly to be circumstantial in everything and in this respect, despite the fact that he himself is a Russian man, wants to be neat like a German. This will take, however, not much time and space, because not much needs to be added to what the reader already knows, that is, that Petrushka wore a somewhat wide brown frock coat from the master's shoulder and had, according to the custom of people of his rank, a large nose and lips. He was of a more silent than talkative character; he even had a noble impulse toward enlightenment, that is, reading books, the content of which did not trouble him: it was completely all the same to him whether it was the adventures of a hero in love, simply a primer, or a prayer book, - he read everything with equal attention; if chemistry had come his way, he would not have refused it either. He liked not what he read, but more the reading itself, or, better to say, the very process of reading, that somehow words always come out of letters, which sometimes mean the devil knows what. This reading was done more in a lying position in the front hall, on the bed and on the mattress, which from such circumstances had become beaten down and thin as a pancake. Besides his passion for reading, he had two more habits that constituted two other characteristic traits: to sleep without undressing, just as he was, in the same frock coat, and always to carry about with him a certain special air, his own particular smell, which smacked somewhat of a lived-in room, so that it was enough for him simply to set up his bed somewhere, even in a room hitherto uninhabited, and drag his overcoat and belongings there, and it already seemed that people had lived in that room for ten years. Chichikov, being a very squeamish man and even in certain cases fastidious, having drawn the air toward himself onto his fresh nose in the morning, would only wrinkle up and shake his head, saying: "You, brother, the devil only knows, do you sweat or something. You should go to the bathhouse at least." To which Petrushka answered nothing and tried right there to occupy himself with some task; or he would go with a whip to the master's tailcoat hanging there, or simply put something in order. What he thought at that time when he was silent - perhaps he was saying to himself: "And yet you are good, too, aren't you tired of repeating the same thing forty times," - God knows, it is hard to know what a serf thinks at the time his master gives him instruction. So, this is what can be said about Petrushka for the first time. The coachman Selifan was a completely different person... But the author is very ashamed to occupy readers so long with people of low class, knowing from experience how unwillingly they become acquainted with low estates. Such is already the Russian man: a strong passion to get familiar with someone who would be at least one rank higher than him, and a hat acquaintance with a count or prince is better for him than any close friendly relations. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a collegiate councilor. Court councilors, perhaps, will become acquainted with him, but those who have already climbed to general ranks, those, God knows, perhaps will even throw one of those contemptuous glances that are thrown proudly by a man at everything that crawls at his feet, or, what is still worse, perhaps will pass by with a disregard deadly for the author. But however regrettable both the one and the other, still, however, it is necessary to return to the hero. So, having given the necessary orders already in the evening, having woken up in the morning very early, having washed, having wiped himself from head to foot with a wet sponge, which was done only on Sundays, - and that day happened to be Sunday, - having shaved in such a way that his cheeks became genuine satin in regard to smoothness and gloss, having put on a frock coat of lingonberry color with a sparkle and then an overcoat on large bears, he went down the stairs, supported under the arm now from one side, now from the other by a tavern servant, and sat in the britzka. With a thunder the britzka drove out from under the gates of the inn onto the street. A passing priest took off his hat, several boys in soiled shirts stretched out their hands, saying:"Sir, give a little something to an orphan!" The coachman, noticing that one of them was a great lover of hanging on to the back of the carriage, lashed him with his whip, and the britska went bouncing over the stones. It was not without joy that he beheld in the distance the striped toll barrier, which announced that the cobblestones, like every other torment, would soon come to an end; and after hitting his head quite hard against the body of the carriage a few more times, Chichikov finally sped along on soft earth. Barely had the town retreated behind them when they began to write, according to our custom, nonsense and wild scenes on both sides of the road: hummocks, spruce forest, low sparse bushes of young pines, charred trunks of old ones, wild heather and similar rubbish. Villages stretched out in a straight line came into view, their construction resembling old stacked firewood, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations beneath them in the form of hanging embroidered towels. A few peasants, as usual, were yawning, sitting on benches before the gates in their sheepskin coats. Women with fat faces and tightly bound breasts looked out of the upper windows; from the lower ones a calf peered out or a pig thrust forth its blind snout. In a word, the usual sights. After passing the fifteenth verst, he remembered that here, according to Manilov, his village should be, but the sixteenth verst also flew by, and the village was still nowhere to be seen, and if it had not been for two peasants who happened to come their way, they would hardly have managed to get on the right track.

To the question of how far it was to the village of Zamanilovka, the peasants took off their hats, and one of them, who was cleverer and wore a wedge-shaped beard, answered:

"Manilovka, maybe, and not Zamanilovka?"

"Well yes, Manilovka."

"Manilovka! And when you drive another verst, then there you are, that is, straight to the right."

"To the right?" responded the coachman.

"To the right," said the peasant. "That will be your road to Manilovka; but Zamanilovka there is none. It is called so, that is, its name is Manilovka, and Zamanilovka is not here at all. There, straight on the hill, you will see a house, made of stone, two stories, a gentleman's house, in which, that is, the gentleman himself lives. That is Manilovka for you, and Zamanilovka there is none at all here and never was."

They set off to find Manilovka. After driving two versts, they came upon a turn onto a country road, but they had already gone two, and three, and four versts, it seemed, and the stone house of two stories was still nowhere to be seen. Here Chichikov recalled that if a friend invites you to his village fifteen versts away, it means there are a good thirty to it. The village of Manilovka could hardly lure anyone with its location. The gentleman's house stood alone on a rise, that is, on an elevation open to all the winds that might choose to blow; the slope of the hill on which it stood was covered with trimmed turf. On it were scattered, in the English manner, two or three flower beds with bushes of lilac and yellow acacia; five or six birches in small clumps here and there raised their small-leaved, sparse tops. Under two of them was visible a gazebo with a flat green cupola, wooden blue columns and the inscription: "Temple of Solitary Reflection"; below it a pond, covered with greenery, which, incidentally, is not unusual in the English gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this elevation, and partly along the very slope, gray log huts darkened lengthwise and crosswise, which our hero, for reasons unknown, immediately began to count and counted more than two hundred; among them there was not a single growing tree or any greenery anywhere; everywhere only bare logs met the eye. Finally he went into the room, sat down on a chair, and gave himself up to reflection, rejoicing inwardly that he had given his guest a small pleasure. Then his thoughts were carried imperceptibly to other subjects and finally bore him off God knows where. He thought about the well-being of friendly life, about how good it would be to live with a friend on the bank of some river, then across that river he began to build a bridge, then a huge house with such a high belvedere that one could see even Moscow from there and drink tea there in the evening in the open air and discuss some pleasant subjects. Then, that together with Chichikov they arrived in some society in good carriages, where everyone is charmed by the pleasantness of their manners, and that supposedly the sovereign, having learned of such friendship between them, granted them generals, and further, finally, God knows what, which he himself could no longer make out. Chichikov's strange request suddenly interrupted all his reveries. The thought of it somehow especially did not digest in his head: no matter how he turned it, he simply could not explain it to himself, and all the time he sat and smoked his pipe, which lasted until supper itself.

Chapter Three

And Chichikov, in a contented state of mind, sat in his britzka, which had long been rolling along the highroad. From the previous chapter it is already evident what constituted the chief object of his taste and inclinations, and therefore it is no wonder that he soon plunged entirely into it, both body and soul. The suppositions, calculations, and considerations wandering over his face were evidently very pleasant, for every minute they left behind traces of a satisfied smirk. Occupied by them,He paid no attention at all to how his coachman, pleased with the reception from Manilov's house serfs, was making very sensible remarks to the piebald trace horse harnessed on the right side. This piebald horse was greatly cunning and only pretended to be pulling, while the root bay horse and the trace horse of the dun color, called Assessor because he had been acquired from a certain assessor, labored with all their hearts, so that even in their eyes one could notice the pleasure they derived from it. "Be crafty, be crafty! I'll outfox you yet!" said Selifan, rising up and cracking his whip at the lazy one. "You know your business, you German pantaloon! The bay is an honorable horse, he does his duty, I'll gladly give him an extra measure, because he's an honorable horse, and Assessor is a good horse too... Now, now! what are you twitching your ears for? You fool, listen when you're spoken to! I won't teach you anything bad, you ignoramus. Look where he's crawling!" Here he cracked his whip at him again, adding, "Ugh, you barbarian! You cursed Bonaparte!" Then he shouted at all of them: "Hey there, my dears!" and lashed all three, no longer as punishment but to show that he was pleased with them. Having given such pleasure, he again turned his speech to the piebald: "You think you'll hide your behavior. No, live by the truth if you want to be respected. There at the landowner's where we were, there are good people. I'll gladly have a talk if a man is good; with a good man we're always true friends, bosom companions; whether it's drinking tea or having a bite, I'm willing, if he's a good man. Every respect is given to a good man. Our master is respected by everyone because, don't you hear, he served the state, he's a collegiate councilor..."

Thus reasoning, Selifan finally wandered into the most remote abstractions. If Chichikov had listened, he would have learned many details relating personally to himself; but his thoughts were so occupied with his own subject that only a strong clap of thunder made him come to his senses and look around; the whole sky was completely overcast with clouds, and the dusty post road was sprinkled with raindrops. Finally a thunderclap sounded a second time, louder and closer, and the rain suddenly poured down as if from a bucket. At first, taking a slanting direction, it lashed against one side of the carriage body, then the other, then, changing its manner of attack and becoming completely straight, it drummed directly on the top of the carriage body; the splashes finally began to reach his face. This forced him to draw the leather curtains with two round windows intended for viewing roadside scenery, and to order Selifan to drive faster. Selifan, also interrupted in the very middle of his speech, realized that there was indeed no time to dawdle, pulled out from under the driver's seat some trifle made of gray cloth, put it on with sleeves, grabbed the reins, and shouted at his troika, which was barely moving its legs, for it felt a pleasant relaxation from the edifying speeches. But Selifan could not for the life of him remember whether he had passed two or three turns. Having thought it over and recalling the road somewhat, he guessed that there had been many turns, all of which he had missed. Since a Russian man in decisive moments will find what to do without indulging in lengthy deliberation, he turned to the right onto the first crossroad, shouted, "Hey there, my honored friends!" and broke into a gallop, little thinking about where the road he had taken would lead.

The rain, however, seemed to have settled in for a long time. The dust lying on the road quickly turned to mud, and it became harder for the horses to drag the britska with every passing minute. Chichikov was already beginning to worry greatly, not seeing Sobakevich's village for so long. By his calculation, it should have been time to arrive long ago. He peered in all directions, but the darkness was such that you could not see your hand in front of your face.

"Selifan!" he said at last, leaning out of the britska.

"What is it, master?" answered Selifan.

"Look, can you see the village?"

"No, master, it's nowhere to be seen!" After which Selifan, waving his whip, began humming something that was not quite a song, but something so long that it had no end. Everything went into it: all the encouraging and urging cries,with which horses are fed throughout all of Russia from one end to the other; adjectives of all genders without further sorting, as whatever first came to tongue. Thus it reached the point that he finally began to call them secretaries.

Meanwhile Chichikov began to notice that the britchka was swaying in all directions and giving him very strong jolts; this made him feel that they had turned off the road and were probably dragging along a harrowed field. Selifan, it seemed, had himself realized it but said not a word.

"What, you rascal, what road are you driving on?" said Chichikov.

"But what's to be done, master, the time's like that; you can't see the whip, such darkness!" Having said this, he tilted the britchka so sharply that Chichikov was forced to hold on with both hands. Only then did he notice that Selifan was tipsy.

"Hold it, hold it, you'll overturn us!" he shouted at him.

"No, master, how could I ever overturn it," said Selifan. "It's no good to overturn, I know that myself; I'll never overturn it." Then he began to turn the britchka slightly, turned and turned, and finally flipped it completely onto its side. Chichikov went sprawling into the mud, hands and feet both. Selifan, however, stopped the horses, though they would have stopped on their own anyway, because they were thoroughly exhausted. Such an unforeseen occurrence utterly amazed him. Climbing down from the box, he stood before the britchka, propped his hands on his hips, while the master was floundering in the mud, struggling to get out of it, and said after some reflection: "Look at that, and it's tipped over!"

"You're drunk as a cobbler!" said Chichikov.

"No, master, how could I possibly be drunk! I know that being drunk is a bad business. I had a talk with a friend, because with a good man one can have a talk, there's no harm in that; and we had a bite together. A bite is no offense; with a good man one can have a bite.

"And what did I tell you the last time when you got drunk? Eh? forgotten?" said Chichikov.

"No, your honor, how could I forget? I already know my business. I know that being drunk is not good. I had a talk with a good man, because...

"I'll give you a thrashing, and then you'll know what it means to talk with a good man!"

"As your mercy pleases," answered the all-agreeable Selifan, "if you thrash, then thrash; I'm not at all against it. Why not thrash, if it's for a good reason, that's the master's will. It needs a thrashing, because a peasant gets up to mischief, order must be observed. If it's for a good reason, then thrash; why not thrash?"

At such reasoning the master was completely at a loss what to reply. But at that moment it seemed as though fate itself had decided to take pity on him. From afar came the sound of a dog's bark. Delighted, Chichikov gave orders to urge the horses on. A Russian driver has good instinct instead of eyes; that is why it happens that he, with eyes shut, sometimes drives at full speed and always ends up somewhere. Selifan, seeing not a thing, directed the horses so straight toward the village that he stopped only when the britchka struck its shafts against a fence and when there was decidedly nowhere left to go. Chichikov only noticed through the thick veil of pouring rain something resembling a roof. He sent Selifan to look for the gate, which would undoubtedly have gone on for a long time if in Russia there had not been, instead of doormen, spirited dogs, which announced him so loudly that he put his fingers to his ears. A light flashed in one little window and reached the fence in a misty stream, pointing out the gate to our travelers. Selifan began to knock, and soon, opening the wicket, some figure thrust itself out, covered with a homespun coat, and the master and the servant heard a hoarse woman's voice:

"Who's knocking? what's all this racket?"

"Travelers, mother, let us stay the night," said Chichikov.

"Look at you, what a spry one," said the old woman, "arriving at such an hour! This is no inn: a landowner lives here."

"What's to be done, mother: see, we've lost our way. We can't spend the night out on the steppe at such a time."

"Yes, the time is dark, a bad time," added Selifan.

"Be quiet, fool," said Chichikov.

"But who are you?" said the old woman.

"A gentleman, mother."

The word "gentleman" made the old woman seem to ponder a little.

"Wait, I'll tell the mistress,"— she said, and about two minutes later she returned with a lantern in her hand.

The gate was unlocked. A light flashed in another window too. The britzka, having driven into the yard, stopped before a small house, which was hard to make out in the darkness. Only one half of it was lit by the light coming from the windows; a puddle could also be seen in front of the house, struck directly by that same light. The rain beat noisily on the wooden roof and ran down in gurgling streams into a barrel placed underneath. Meanwhile the dogs struck up in every possible voice: one, throwing its head back, drawled so long and with such effort, as if it were receiving heaven knows what salary for it; another snapped it off hastily, like a parish clerk; between them rang out, like a post bell, the restless treble of what was probably a young puppy, and all of this was finally capped by a bass, perhaps an old dog endowed with a sturdy canine nature, because it rasped as a singing contrabass rasps when the concert is in full swing: the tenors rise on tiptoe from a strong desire to hit a high note, and everything strains upward, throwing back its head, while it alone, having thrust its unshaven chin into its necktie, crouching and sinking almost to the ground, lets out from there its note, at which the windows shake and rattle. Just from the dogs' barking alone, composed of such musicians, one could suppose that the little village was a decent one; but our soaked and chilled hero thought of nothing but bed. Before the britzka had quite stopped, he had already leaped onto the porch, staggered, and nearly fell. A woman came out onto the porch again, younger than the previous one, but very much like her. She led him into a room. Chichikov cast two cursory glances: the room was hung with old striped wallpaper; pictures of some sort of birds; between the windows, old little mirrors with dark frames in the shape of curled leaves; behind each mirror had been tucked either a letter, or an old deck of cards, or a stocking; a wall clock with painted flowers on its dial… there was no strength to notice anything more. He felt that his eyes were sticking together, as if someone had smeared them with honey. A minute later the hostess came in, a woman of elderly years, in some sort of sleeping cap put on hastily, with flannel around her neck, one of those little mothers, small landowners, who complain about crop failures, losses, and hold their heads somewhat to one side, while meanwhile they gradually accumulate money in little patterned bags placed in the drawers of a chest. In one bag they put all the rubles, in another the half-rubles, in a third the quarter-rubles, though outwardly it seems as if there were nothing in the chest but linen, and night jackets, and skeins of thread, and a ripped-open coat which is later to be turned into a dress if the old one somehow burns up during the baking of holiday pastries with all sorts of fritters, or wears out by itself. But the dress will not burn up and will not wear out by itself: the thrifty old woman is careful, and the coat is destined to lie for a long time in its ripped-open state, and then go by testament to the niece of a second cousin along with all sorts of other junk.

Chichikov apologized for troubling her with an unexpected arrival.

— It is nothing, nothing, — said the hostess. — At what time has God brought you! Such confusion and snowstorm… You ought to have something to eat after the road, but it is nighttime now, nothing can be prepared.

The hostess's words were interrupted by a strange hissing, so that the guest was frightened; the noise was like the whole room filling with snakes; but, glancing up, he calmed down, for he realized that the wall clock had taken a notion to strike. The hissing was immediately followed by a wheezing, and at last, straining with all their might, they struck two o'clock with a sound as if someone were pounding a stick on a broken pot, after which the pendulum went back to quietly clicking to the right and to the left.

Chichikov thanked the hostess, saying that he needed nothing, that she should not trouble herself about anything, that besides a bed he required nothing, and merely inquired with curiosity as to what parts he had arrived in and how far the road from there was to the landowner Sobakevich, to which the old woman said,that she had never even heard of such a name and that there was no such landowner at all.

"At least you know Manilov?" said Chichikov.

"And who is Manilov?"

"A landowner, little mother."

"No, I haven't heard of him, there's no such landowner."

"What ones are there, then?"

"Bobrov, Svinin, Kanapatyev, Kharpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov."

"Are they rich people or not?"

"No, father, there are no rich ones at all. One has twenty souls, another thirty, but there are none with a hundred."

Chichikov observed that he had driven into a proper backwater.

"Is it far at least to the town?"

"It'll be about sixty versts. How sorry I am that you have nothing to eat! Wouldn't you like, father, to have some tea?"

"Thank you, little mother. I need nothing but a bed."

"True, after such a journey you very much need rest. Here, settle yourself right here, father, on this sofa. Hey, Fetinya, bring a featherbed, pillows, and a sheet. What a time God has sent us: such thunder - my candle burned before the icon all night. Eh, my father, your back and side are covered in mud like a boar's! Where did you manage to get so filthy?"

"Still, thank God that I only got filthy, I ought to be grateful that I didn't break my sides entirely."

"Holy saints, what horrors! But don't you need something to rub your back with?"

"Thank you, thank you. Don't trouble yourself, just tell your girl to dry and clean my clothes."

"Do you hear, Fetinya!" said the hostess, turning to the woman who came out onto the porch with a candle, who had already managed to drag in the featherbed and, fluffing it up on both sides with her hands, sent up a whole flood of feathers all over the room. "Take their caftan along with the underthings and first dry them before the fire, the way we used to do for the late master, and after that rub them down and beat them well."

"Yes, madam!" said Fetinya, spreading a sheet over the featherbed and placing the pillows.

"Well, here's your bed ready," said the hostess. "Farewell, father, I wish you a peaceful night. But don't you need anything else? Maybe you're used, my father, to having someone scratch your heels at night? My late husband could never fall asleep without that."

But the guest declined the heel-scratching as well. The hostess went out, and he at once hastened to undress, giving Fetinya all the gear he had taken off, both outer and inner, and Fetinya, having also wished him a peaceful night on her part, dragged away these wet accoutrements. Left alone, he looked not without pleasure at his bed, which was almost up to the ceiling. Fetinya, as was evident, was a master at fluffing featherbeds. When, placing a chair, he climbed onto the bed, it sank beneath him almost to the very floor, and the feathers, displaced by him beyond their limits, flew scattered into all corners of the room. Having put out the candle, he covered himself with a calico blanket and, curling up under it like a pretzel, fell asleep that very moment. He woke the next day already at a rather late morning hour. The sun through the window was shining straight into his eyes, and the flies, which yesterday had slept peacefully on the walls and ceiling, all turned to him: one sat on his lip, another on his ear, a third tried to settle on his very eye, and the one that had carelessly perched close to his nostril, he pulled in his drowsiness right into his nose, which made him sneeze hard, -

the circumstance that had been the cause of his awakening. Casting a glance around the room, he now noticed that not all the paintings were of birds: among them hung a portrait of Kutuzov and an oil painting of some old man with red cuffs on his uniform, of the kind worn under Pavel Petrovich. The clock again emitted a hissing sound and struck ten; a woman's face peeked through the door and at once hid again, for Chichikov, wishing to sleep better, had thrown off absolutely everything. The face that had peeked in seemed to him somehow familiar. He began trying to recall who it might be, and at last remembered that it was the landlady. He put on his shirt; his clothes, already dried and cleaned, lay beside him. Having dressed, he went up to the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that an Indian rooster that had just come up to the window—the window being very close to the ground—suddenly gobbled something at him very rapidly in its strange language, probably "I wish you good health," to which Chichikov called it a fool. Going up to the window, he began to examine the views before him: the window looked out on what was scarcely not a chicken coop; at any rate, the narrow little yard before him was entirely filled with birds and every sort of domestic creature. There was no counting the turkeys and chickens; among them a rooster strutted with measured steps, shaking its comb and turning its head to one side, as if listening to something; a pig with its family turned up right there too; right there, while raking through a heap of refuse, it casually ate a chick and, not noticing this, continued to gobble down watermelon rinds in its own fashion. This small yard, or chicken coop, was blocked off by a plank fence, beyond which stretched spacious kitchen gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, beets, and other household vegetables. Here and there among the garden were scattered apple trees and other fruit trees, covered with nets for protection from magpies and sparrows, the latter of which moved from one place to another in whole slanting clouds. For this same reason, several scarecrows had been set up on long poles with outspread arms; on one of them was placed the landlady's own nightcap. Beyond the gardens came the peasant cottages, which, though built scattered about and not arranged in regular streets, yet, according to the observation made by Chichikov, showed the contentment of the inhabitants, for they were properly maintained: the worn-out boards on the roofs had everywhere been replaced with new ones; no gate was askew anywhere, and in the peasant covered sheds facing him he noticed here a spare cart, almost new, and there even two. "Yes, her little village is not a small one," he said, and right there decided to have a talk and get better acquainted with the landlady. He peeked through the crack of the door from which she had stuck out her head, and, seeing her sitting at the tea table, went in to her with a cheerful and affectionate look.

"Hello, father. How did you sleep?" said the landlady, rising from her place. She was dressed better than yesterday—in a dark dress and no longer in a sleeping cap, but around her neck there was still something tied in the same way.

"Very well, very well," said Chichikov, sitting down in an armchair. "And how are you, mother?"

"Poorly, my father."

"How so?"

"Insomnia. My lower back aches all the time, and my leg, just above the bone, simply throbs."

"It will pass, it will pass, mother. There's no point paying attention to that."

"God grant it passes. I rubbed it with pork fat and also moistened it with turpentine. And what will you take with your tea? There's fruit vodka in the flask."

"Not bad, mother, let's have some fruit vodka too."

The reader, I think, has already noticed that Chichikov, despite his affectionate look, nevertheless spoke with greater freedom than he had with Manilov, and stood on no ceremony at all. It must be said that if we in Rus have not yet caught up with foreigners in certain things, we have far surpassed them in the art of dealing with people. One cannot count all the shades and subtleties of our manner of address. A Frenchman or a German will never in a century grasp or understand all its peculiarities and distinctions; he will speak in almost the same voice and the same language both to a millionaire and to a petty tobacco dealer, though of course in his soul he will fawn upon the former to a certain degree. With us it is not so: with us there are such wise men who, with a landowner possessing two hundred souls,will speak in a completely different way than with someone who has three hundred, and someone who has three hundred will again speak differently than with someone who has five hundred, and with someone who has five hundred, again differently than with someone who has eight hundred, — in a word, even if you go up to a million, they will still find shades of difference. Suppose, for example, there exists an office, not here, but in a kingdom far away, and in the office, suppose, there exists a head of the office. I ask you to look at him when he sits among his subordinates, — you simply cannot utter a word from fear! pride and nobility, and what does his face not express? just take a brush and paint: Prometheus, a decisive Prometheus! He looks about like an eagle, steps smoothly, measuredly. That same eagle, as soon as he leaves the room and approaches the office of his superior, hurries along like a partridge with papers under his arm, so that it is unbearable. In society and at a party, if everyone is of low rank, Prometheus will remain Prometheus, but as soon as someone is a little higher than he, with Prometheus there will occur such a transformation as even Ovid could not invent: a fly, even smaller than a fly, he is annihilated into a grain of sand! "But this is not Ivan Petrovich," you say, looking at him. "Ivan Petrovich is taller, but this one is short and thin; that one speaks loudly, in a bass voice, and never laughs, but this one, the devil knows what: he squeaks like a bird and laughs all the time." You come closer, you look — it is exactly Ivan Petrovich! "Heh-heh," you think to yourself… But, however, let us return to the acting characters. Chichikov, as we have already seen, decided not to stand on ceremony at all, and therefore, having taken a cup of tea in his hands and poured fruit liqueur into it, he launched into the following speech:

— You have a nice little village, mother. How many souls are in it?

— Souls in it, my father, close to eighty, — said the hostess, — but the trouble is, times are bad, and last year there was such a crop failure that God preserve us.

— However, the peasants look sturdy, the huts are solid. But allow me to inquire your surname. I got so carried away… I arrived at nighttime..

— Korobochka, collegiate secretary.

— Most humbly grateful. And your name and patronymic?

— Nastasya Petrovna.

— Nastasya Petrovna? a good name, Nastasya Petrovna. I have an aunt by blood, my mother's sister, Nastasya Petrovna.

— And what is your name? — asked the landowner. — Surely you are the assessor?

— No, mother, — answered Chichikov, grinning, — surely not the assessor, but I just travel about on my own little affairs.

— Ah, so you are a buyer! How very unfortunate, truly, that I sold my honey to the merchants so cheaply, but you, my father, would surely have bought it from me.

— But honey I would not have bought.

— What else then? Hemp, perhaps? But I have little hemp now: only half a pood in all.

— No, mother, a different kind of goods: tell me, have any of your peasants died?

— Oh, my father, eighteen people — said the old woman, sighing. — And such fine folk died, all workers. Afterwards, it is true, some were born, but what are they: all such small fry; and the assessor came round — pay, he says, the tax from each soul. The people are dead, but you pay as for the living. Last week my blacksmith burned up, such a skilled blacksmith, and he knew locksmithing too.

— Was there a fire at your place, mother?

— God spared us from such a disaster, a fire would have been even worse; he burned up himself, my father. Something caught fire inside him, he drank too much, only a blue flame came from him, he smoldered all away, smoldered away and turned black as coal, and he was such a most skilled blacksmith! and now I have nothing to drive out with: there is no one to shoe the horses.

— On all things is the will of God, mother! — said Chichikov, sighing, — against the wisdom of God one cannot say anything… Let me have them, Nastasya Petrovna?

— Whom, my father?

— Why, all these here, the ones that died.

— But how can I let you have them?

— Why, just like that. Or, if you like, sell them. I will give you money for them.

— But how is that? I really cannot make sense of it. Surely you do not want to dig them up out of the ground?

Chichikov saw that the old woman had gone far indeed and that it was absolutely necessary to explain to her what the matter was. In a few words he explained to her that the transfer or purchase would be recorded only on paper and the souls would be registered as if living.

— But what do you need them for? — said the old woman, staring at him wide-eyed.

— That is my business.

— But they are dead, after all.— But who says they are alive? That's why you're at a loss — because they're dead: you pay for them, and now I'll save you from the hassle and the payment. Understand? And not only will I save you from it, but on top of that I'll give you fifteen rubles. Well, is it clear now?

— Really, I don't know, — the hostess said deliberately. — You see, I've never sold dead ones before.

— Of course not! It would be more of a wonder if you had sold them to someone. Or do you think there's actually some use in them?

— No, that I don't think. What use could there be in them, no use at all. The only thing that troubles me is that they're already dead.

"Well, the woman seems thick-headed!" — Chichikov thought to himself.

— Listen, mother. Just think it over carefully: — you're ruining yourself, paying the tax for him as if he were alive…

— Oh, father mine, don't even speak of it! — the landowner caught up. — Only three weeks ago I paid out more than a hundred and fifty. And I greased the assessor's palm too.

— Well, you see, mother. And now just consider this: you won't need to grease the assessor's palm anymore, because now I'll be paying for them; I, not you; I take upon myself all the obligations. I'll even complete the deed of sale at my own expense, do you understand that?

The old woman fell to thinking. She saw that the matter did indeed seem profitable, only it was too new and unprecedented; and therefore she began to be very much afraid that this buyer might somehow cheat her; he had come from God knows where, and at nighttime too.

— So then, mother, is it a deal? — said Chichikov.

— Really, father mine, I've never happened to sell dead ones. The live ones I've let go — just the year before last I gave the priest two girls, a hundred rubles each, and he was very grateful, they turned out to be splendid workers: they weave napkins themselves.

— Well, but it's not about the live ones; God be with them. I'm asking about the dead ones.

— Really, I'm afraid at first that I might somehow suffer a loss. Maybe you, father mine, are deceiving me, and they… they're worth more somehow.

— Listen, mother… oh, what are you like! What could they be worth? Consider: it's dust. Do you understand? It's simply dust. Take any worthless, last thing, for example even a simple rag, and even a rag has a price: at least it'll be bought for a paper factory, but this is no good for anything. Well, tell me yourself, what is it good for?

— That's truly true. It's absolutely no good for anything; the only thing that stops me is that they're already dead.

"What a woman, what a blockhead!" — Chichikov said to himself, already beginning to lose patience. "Go try and come to terms with her! She's got me in a sweat, the cursed old woman!" Here he took a handkerchief out of his pocket and began wiping away the sweat that had actually appeared on his forehead. However, Chichikov was angry in vain: there are people who are respectable, even statesmen, but in practice turn out to be a perfect Korobochka. Once they get something into their head, you can't overcome it with anything; no matter how many arguments you present, clear as day, everything bounces off them like a rubber ball bouncing off a wall. Having wiped off the sweat, Chichikov decided to try whether he could lead her onto the right path from some other angle.

— You, mother, — he said, — either don't want to understand my words, or you speak that way on purpose just to say something… I'm giving you money: fifteen rubles in assignats. Do you understand? It's money. You won't find it lying in the street. Well, admit it, how much did you sell your honey for?

— Twelve rubles a pood.

— You've taken a bit of sin upon your soul, mother. You didn't sell it for twelve.

— By God, I did sell it.

— Well, do you see? But that's honey for you. You gathered it, maybe for about a year, with cares, with effort, with troubles; you traveled, smoked the bees,[ Smoking the bees — stupefying them with smoke in order to take out the honeycombs.

] fed them in the cellar all winter; but dead souls are a matter not of this world. Here you applied no effort on your part, it was God's will that they should leave this world, causing damage to your household. There you received for your labor, for your effort, twelve rubles, but here you take for nothing, for free, and not twelve, but fifteen, and not in silver, but all in blue assignats.After such strong convictions Chichikov almost no longer doubted that the old woman would finally give in.

"Right," answered the landowner, "such is my inexperienced widow's business! better if I wait a little, perhaps merchants will come, and I'll compare prices."

"Shame, shame, mother! simply shame! Well, what are you saying, think for yourself! Who would buy them? Well, what use could he make of them?"

"But maybe in the household somehow on occasion they might be needed..." objected the old woman, and did not finish her speech, opened her mouth and looked at him almost with fear, wishing to know what he would say to that.

"Dead ones in the household! My, where have you gone! To scare sparrows at night in your garden, is that it?"

"You're lying, you're lying, and never thought of combing; I think, fool, you even let your own loose on it. Here look, Chichikov, look, what ears, go on, feel with your hand."

"Why should I, I can see as it is: good breed!" answered Chichikov.

"No, take it on purpose, feel the ears!"

Chichikov, to please him, felt the ears, adding:

"Yes, it will be a good dog."

"And the nose, feel how cold it is? Take it with your hand."

Not wishing to offend him, Chichikov took it by the nose too, saying:

"Good scent."

"A real pug," continued Nozdryov, "ah, I confess, I've long been sharpening my teeth for a pug. Here, Porfiry, take him back!"

Porfiry, taking the puppy under the belly, carried it away to the britzka.

"Listen, Chichikov, you must absolutely come to my place now, it's only five versts, we'll dash there in no time, and there, perhaps, you can go on to Sobakevich."

'And what if,' thought Chichikov to himself, 'I do stop by Nozdryov's. What makes him worse than others, he's the same kind of person, and he's even lost at cards. He's capable of anything, as can be seen, so I can beg something off him for nothing.'

"Very well, let's go," he said, "but mind you don't delay me, my time is precious."

"Well, soul, that's the way! That's good, wait then, I'll kiss you for that. - Here Nozdryov and Chichikov kissed. - And splendid: the three of us will roll along!"

"No, please, let me go," said the fair-haired one, "I need to go home."

"Trifles, trifles, brother, I won't let you."

"Really, my wife will be angry; you can now get into their britzka."

"No, no, no! Don't think of it."

The fair-haired one was one of those people,"in whose character at first glance there is some kind of stubbornness. Before you can even open your mouth, they are already prepared to argue and, it seems, will never agree to what is clearly contrary to their way of thinking, that they will never call a fool smart and that especially they will not agree to dance to someone else's tune; but it always ends with their character turning out to have a softness, that they will agree to precisely what they rejected, will call a fool smart and will then go and dance as nicely as can be to someone else's tune, - in a word, they begin smoothly and end foully.

"Nonsense!" said Nozdryov in response to some sort of remark from the fair-haired man, put a cap on his head, and - the fair-haired man set off after them.

"You didn't pay for the vodka, master..." said the old woman.

"Ah, fine, fine, mother. Listen, son-in-law! pay, please. I don't have a single kopek in my pocket."

"How much do you owe?" said the son-in-law.

"Why, father, just a dvugrivennik," said the old woman.

"You're lying, you're lying. Give her a poltina,[ A dvugrivennik (in silver) - eighty kopeks in assignats. A poltina - fifty kopeks in assignats.

] that's plenty for her.

"That's a bit little, master," said the old woman, but she took the money with gratitude and even ran in haste to open the door for them. She was not at a loss, because she had asked four times what the vodka was worth.

The visitors seated themselves. Chichikov's britchka rode alongside the britchka in which Nozdryov and his son-in-law sat, and therefore all three of them could freely converse among themselves during the journey. Behind them followed, constantly falling behind, Nozdryov's small carriage on thin peasant horses. In it sat Porfiry with a puppy.

Since the conversation that the travelers conducted among themselves was not very interesting for the reader, we would do better to say something about Nozdryov himself, who, perhaps, is destined to play not entirely the last role in our poem.

Nozdryov's face is surely already somewhat familiar to the reader. Everyone has had occasion to meet no few such people. They are called lively fellows, are known even in childhood and at school as good comrades and with all that are very painfully beaten. In their faces there is always something open, direct, daring. They quickly make acquaintances, and before you can look around, they are already addressing you as "thou." They will strike up a friendship, it seems, forever: but almost always it happens that the one who has become friends will fight with them that very evening at a friendly feast. They are always talkers, revelers, daredevils, conspicuous folk. Nozdryov at thirty-five was exactly the same as he had been at eighteen and twenty: a lover of merrymaking. His marriage had not changed him in the least, the more so since his wife soon departed to the next world, leaving two little children who were decidedly of no use to him. The children, however, were looked after by a pretty nurse. At home he could not sit still for more than a day. His keen nose could smell from several dozen versts away where there was a fair with all sorts of gatherings and balls; he was already there in the twinkling of an eye, arguing and creating a commotion at the green table, for he had, like all such people, a passion for cards. At cards, as we have already seen from the first chapter, he did not play quite without sin and cleanly, knowing many different sleights and other subtleties, and therefore the game very often ended in another game: either they beat him with boots, or they gave a tug to his thick and very good side-whiskers, so that sometimes he returned home with only one side-whisker, and that a rather thin one. But his healthy and full cheeks were so well made and contained so much plant force that the side-whiskers soon grew back again, even better than before. And what is strangest of all, which can only happen in Russia, he would after some time meet again those same friends who had pummeled him, and would meet as if nothing had happened, and he, as they say, was nothing, and they were nothing.

Nozdryov was in a certain respect a historical person. Not a single gathering where he was present was complete without a story. Some kind of story would invariably occur: either the gendarmes would lead him out of the hall under the arms, or his own friends would be compelled to push him out.If this does not happen, then something will still happen that will never happen to anyone else: either he will cut himself in the buffet in such a way that he just laughs, or he will lie in the most cruel way, so that in the end he himself will become ashamed. And he will lie completely without any need: suddenly he will tell you that he had a horse of some sort of blue or pink wool, and similar nonsense, so that the listeners finally all walk away, saying: "Well, brother, you seem to have already started pouring bullets." There are people who have a little passion for fouling their neighbor, sometimes for no reason at all. Another, for example, even a man in ranks, with a noble appearance, with a star on his chest, will shake your hand, talk with you about deep subjects that invite reflection, and then, you see, right there, before your eyes, he will foul you. And he will foul you just like a simple collegiate registrar, and not at all like a man with a star on his chest talking about subjects that invite reflection, so that you just stand there and marvel, shrugging your shoulders, and nothing more. Nozdrev had the same strange passion. The closer anyone became with him, the sooner he salted everyone: he would spread a tall tale, stupider than which it is hard to invent, he would upset a wedding, a trade deal, and did not consider himself your enemy at all; on the contrary, if chance brought him to meet you again, he would treat you in a friendly way again and even say: "But you're such a scoundrel, you never come to see me." Nozdrev in many respects was a many-sided man, that is, a man handy at anything. At that very moment he would offer to go with you anywhere, even to the end of the world, to enter into any enterprise you like, to exchange anything there is for anything you want. A gun, a dog, a horse — everything was an object of exchange, but not at all in order to win: this happened simply from some restless nimbleness and liveliness of character. If at a fair he was lucky enough to come upon a simpleton and beat him at cards, he would buy up a heap of everything that had previously caught his eye in the shops: horse collars, smoking candles, handkerchiefs for a nanny, a stallion, raisins, a silver washstand, Dutch linen, fine flour, tobacco, pistols, herrings, pictures, a sharpening tool, pots, boots, faience dishes — as far as the money went. However, it rarely happened that this was brought home; almost on the same day it was all let go to another, more fortunate gambler, sometimes even his own pipe with pouch and mouthpiece was added, and another time also the whole team of four with everything: with the carriage and the coachman, so that the owner himself set off in a short frock coat or an archaluk to look for some friend in order to make use of his carriage. That was the kind of man Nozdrev was! Perhaps they will call his character hackneyed, they will begin to say that now there is no more Nozdrev. Alas! those who begin to say so will be unjust. Nozdrev will not soon disappear from the world. He is everywhere among us and, perhaps, only walks in a different caftan; but people are frivolously imperceptive, and a man in a different caftan seems to them a different man.

Meanwhile the three carriages had already rolled up to the porch of Nozdrev's house. There was no preparation at all in the house for their reception. In the middle of the dining room stood wooden trestles, and two peasants, standing on them, were whitewashing the walls, dragging out some endless song; the floor was all splashed with whitewash. Nozdrev ordered the peasants and the trestles out at once and ran into another room to give orders. The guests heard how he was ordering dinner from the cook; realizing this, Chichikov, who had already begun to feel somewhat hungry, saw that they would not sit down at the table before five o'clock. Nozdrev, returning, led the guests to inspect everything there was in his village, and in a little over two hours showed absolutely everything, so that there was nothing left to show. First of all they went to inspect the stable, where they saw two mares, one gray dappled, the other dark bay, then a bay stallion, unprepossessing in appearance, but for which Nozdrev swore he had paid ten thousand.

"You did not give ten thousand for him," remarked the son-in-law. "He is not worth one."

"By God, I gave ten thousand,"— said Nozdrev.

— You can swear as much as you like, — answered the son-in-law.

— Well, if you like, let's have a bet! — said Nozdrev.

The son-in-law did not want to make a bet.

Then Nozdrev showed them the empty stalls, where there had also been good horses before. In the same stable they saw a goat, which, according to an old belief, was considered necessary to keep with horses, and which, as it seemed, was on good terms with them, strolling under their bellies as if at home. Then Nozdrev led them to see a wolf cub that was on a leash. "Here's a wolf cub!" he said. "I feed it raw meat on purpose. I want it to be a perfect beast!" They went to look at the pond, in which, according to Nozdrev, there were fish of such a size that two men could hardly pull one out, though the relative did not fail to doubt this. "I'll show you, Chichikov," said Nozdrev, "a most excellent pair of dogs: the firmness of the black meat simply inspires amazement, the shield is a needle!" — and he led them to a very beautifully built little house, surrounded by a large courtyard fenced on all sides. Entering the courtyard, they saw all sorts of dogs there, both thick-coated and smooth-coated, of all possible colors and markings: brownish-red, black with tan markings, half-piebald, brown-piebald, red-piebald, black-eared, gray-eared... There were all sorts of names here, all imperative moods: Shoot, Scold, Flutter, Fire, Dandy, Scribble, Torment, Scorch, Severga, Darling, Reward, Patroness. Nozdrev was among them exactly like a father among his family; they all, immediately putting up their tails, called "rules" by dog handlers, flew straight toward the guests and began greeting them. About ten of them put their paws on Nozdrev's shoulders. Scold showed the same friendship to Chichikov and, rising on its hind legs, licked him right on the lips with its tongue, so that Chichikov immediately spat. They inspected the dogs, which inspired amazement by the firmness of their black meat — they were good dogs. Then they went to inspect a Crimean bitch, which was already blind and, according to Nozdrev, should soon die, but two years ago had been a very good bitch; they inspected the bitch too — the bitch was, indeed, blind. Then they went to inspect a water mill, where there was no spindle to hold the upper millstone, which spins rapidly on a spindle — "fluttering," in the wonderful expression of the Russian peasant.

— And here soon there will be a forge too! — said Nozdrev. After walking a little further, they saw, indeed, a forge, and inspected the forge too.

— In this field, — said Nozdrev, pointing with his finger at the field, — there are so many hares that you can't see the ground; I caught one myself with my own hands by its hind legs.

— Well, you won't catch a hare with your hand! — remarked the son-in-law.

— But I did catch one, caught it on purpose! — answered Nozdrev — Now I'll take you to see, — he continued, turning to Chichikov, — the boundary where my land ends.

Nozdrev led his guests across the field, which in many places consisted of hummocks. The guests had to make their way between fallow land and harrowed fields. Chichikov was beginning to feel tired. In many places their feet squeezed water out from under them, the ground was so low. At first they tried to be careful and stepped cautiously, but then, seeing that this was of no use, they trudged straight on, not distinguishing where there was more and where there was less mud. After walking a considerable distance, they saw, indeed, a boundary consisting of a wooden post and a narrow ditch.

— Here's the boundary! — said Nozdrev. — Everything you see on this side, all this is mine, and even on that side, all that forest that shows blue over there, and everything beyond the forest, all is mine.

— But when did that forest become yours? — asked the son-in-law. — Did you buy it recently? It wasn't yours, was it.

— Yes, I bought it recently, — answered Nozdrev.

— When did you manage to buy it so quickly?

— Why, I bought it the day before yesterday, and paid a lot, damn it.

— But you were at the fair at that time.

— Eh, you Sofron! Can't one be at the fair and buy land at the same time? Well, I was at the fair, and my clerk bought it here without me.

— Yes, well, a clerk! — said the son-in-law, but even here he doubted and shook his head.The guests returned home by the same nasty road. Nozdryov led them into his study, in which, however, no traces were noticeable of what usually is found in studies, that is, books or papers; only sabers and two guns hung there - one worth three hundred, and the other eight hundred rubles. The son-in-law, having examined them, merely shook his head. Then Turkish daggers were shown, on one of which, by mistake, was engraved: "Master Savely Sibiryakov." Following that, a hurdy-gurdy appeared to the guests. Nozdryov immediately turned something on it before them. The hurdy-gurdy played not without pleasantness, but in the middle of it something seemed to have happened, for the mazurka ended with the song: "Malbrug went on a campaign," and "Malbrug went on a campaign" unexpectedly concluded with some long-familiar waltz. Nozdryov had long since stopped turning it, but in the hurdy-gurdy there was one very lively pipe that absolutely refused to calm down, and for a long time afterward it kept whistling all by itself. Then pipes were shown - wooden, clay, meerschaum, smoked and unsmoked, covered with chamois and not covered, a pipe stem with an amber mouthpiece, recently won, a tobacco pouch embroidered by some countess who had fallen head over heels in love with him somewhere at a posting station, whose little hands, according to him, were of the most subdelicate superflue - a word probably meaning in his vocabulary the highest point of perfection. After having a bite of balyk, they sat down to the table close to five o'clock. Dinner, evidently, did not constitute the main thing in Nozdryov's life; the dishes did not play a big role: some things were burnt, some things were not cooked at all. It was evident that the cook had been guided more by some sort of inspiration and put in the first thing that came to hand: if pepper stood near him - he sprinkled pepper, if cabbage turned up - he shoved in cabbage, stuffed in milk, ham, peas - in a word, slapdash, as long as it was hot, and some sort of taste would surely come out. But Nozdryov leaned on the wines: before the soup was even served, he had already poured the guests a large glass of port and another of haut-sauterne, because in provincial and district towns there is no plain sauterne. Therefore Nozdryov ordered a bottle of madeira to be brought, better than which the field marshal himself had never drunk. The madeira, indeed, even burned in the mouth, for the merchants, already knowing the taste of landowners who loved good madeira, doctored it mercilessly with rum, and sometimes even poured in aqua regia, in the hope that Russian stomachs would withstand anything. Then Nozdryov ordered yet another special bottle to be brought, which, according to him, was both burgundy and champagne together. He poured very diligently into both glasses, and to the right and to the left, both for the son-in-law and for Chichikov; Chichikov noticed, however, somehow in passing, that he did not add much to his own. This made him be cautious, and as soon as Nozdryov somehow got carried away talking or poured for his son-in-law, he would immediately tip his glass into his plate. In a short time rowanberry vodka was brought to the table, which, according to Nozdryov, had the perfect taste of cream, but in which, to one's amazement, one could hear fusel oil in all its power. Then they drank some sort of balsam bearing such a name that it was even difficult to remember, and which the host himself the next time called by a different name. Dinner had long since ended, and the wines had been tried, but the guests were still sitting at the table. Chichikov by no means wanted to speak with Nozdryov in front of his son-in-law about the main subject. After all, the son-in-law was an outsider, and the subject required a private and friendly conversation. However, the son-in-law was hardly likely to be a dangerous person, because he had loaded himself, it seemed, to his heart's content and, sitting on his chair, was nodding off every minute. Noticing himself that he was not in a reliable state, he finally began to ask to go home, but in such a lazy and listless voice, as if, according to the Russian expression, he was dragging a horse collar onto a horse with pincers.

"And no-no! I won't let you go!" said Nozdryov.

— No, don't offend me, my friend, really, I'm going, said the son-in-law, — you will offend me very much.

— Trifles, trifles! we'll set up a little bank here this minute.

— No, you set it up yourself, brother, but I can't, my wife will be very put out, really, I must tell her about the fair. I must, brother, really, I must give her that pleasure. No, don't hold me back!

— Hang her, the wife, to...! A really important matter, you're going to do together!

— No, brother! she's so respectable and faithful! She renders such services... would you believe it, I have tears in my eyes. No, don't hold me back; as an honest man, I'm going. I assure you of this on my true conscience.

— Let him go, what's the use of him! — Chichikov said quietly to Nozdryov.

— And that's the truth! — said Nozdryov. — I simply can't stand such slobs! — and he added aloud: — Well, the devil take you, go and be a woman with your wife, you fetiuk![ Fetiuk — a word insulting to a man, derived from Fita — a letter considered by some to be an indecent letter. (Note by N. V. Gogol.)

]

— No, brother, don't call me fetiuk, — answered the son-in-law, — I owe her my life. She's so kind, really, dear, she shows such affection... it moves me to tears; she'll ask what I saw at the fair — I must tell her everything, she's so dear, really.

— Well, go on, tell her nonsense! Here's your cap.

— No, brother, you really shouldn't speak of her like that; by that, one might say, you offend me myself, she's so dear.

— Well, then get out of here to her quicker!

— Yes, brother, I'm going, forgive me that I cannot stay. With all my soul I'd be glad to, but I cannot.

The son-in-law kept repeating his apologies for a long time, not noticing that he had long since been sitting in the britzka, had long since driven out of the gate, and before him there had long since been nothing but empty fields. One must suppose that his wife did not hear many details about the fair.

— What a piece of trash! — said Nozdryov, standing before the window and looking at the departing carriage. — Look how he's dragged himself off! The off-side horse of the troika isn't bad, I've long wanted to get hold of it. But there's no way to get along with him. Fetiuk, simply a fetiuk!

After that they went into the room. Porfiry brought candles, and Chichikov noticed in the host's hands a deck of cards that had appeared from who knows where.

— And what about it, brother, — said Nozdryov, pressing the sides of the deck with his fingers and bending it somewhat, so that it cracked and a slip of paper sprang out. — Well, to pass the time, I'll hold a bank of three hundred rubles!

But Chichikov pretended as though he had not heard what it was about, and said, as if suddenly remembering:

— Ah! so as not to forget: I have a request for you.

— What is it?

— First give your word that you'll fulfill it.

— But what is the request?

— Well, just give your word!

— Very well.

— Your honest word?

— My honest word.

— Here's the request: you have, I dare say, many dead peasants who haven't yet been struck off the census?

— Well, I have, so what?

— Transfer them to me, in my name.

— But what do you need them for?

— Well, I need them.

— But what for?

— Well, I just need them... that's my business, — in short, I need them.

— Well, you've certainly thought up something. Confess, what is it?

— But what have I thought up? You can't think up anything from such a trifle.

— But why do you need them?

— Oh, what a curious fellow! He'd like to feel every bit of trash with his hand, and even smell it!

— But why don't you want to say?

— But what profit is it to you to know? Well, just like that, a whim came to me.

— So here's the thing: until you tell me, I won't do it!

— Well, you see, that's already dishonest on your part: you gave your word, and now you're backing out.

— Well, as you like, but I won't do it until you tell me what for.

"What should I tell him?" — thought Chichikov, and after a moment's reflection he announced that dead souls were needed for acquiring weight in society, that he had no large estates, so until then at least some little souls.

— You're lying, you're lying! — said Nozdryov, not letting him finish. — You're lying, brother!

Chichikov himself noticed that he had thought it up not very cleverly and the pretext was rather weak.

— Well, then I'll tell you more directly, — he said, correcting himself, — only, please, don't blab to anyone. I've conceived the idea of getting married; but you must know that the father and mother of the bride are extremely ambitious people. Such a nuisance, really: I'm not glad I got involved, they absolutely want the groom to have no fewer than three hundred souls, and since I have nearly a hundred and fifty peasants short...

— Well, you're lying! you're lying!— Nozdrev shouted again.

— Well, now here,— said Chichikov,— I haven't lied by even this much,— and he showed with his thumb on his little finger the very smallest part.

— I'll bet my head that you're lying!

— But that's insulting! What am I really! Why should I necessarily lie?

— Well, but I know you: you're a big swindler, allow me to tell you that in friendship! If I were your superior, I'd hang you on the first tree.

Chichikov was offended by such a remark. Any expression, however slightly rude or offensive to propriety, was unpleasant to him. He did not even like to permit familiar treatment of himself in any case, unless the person was of too high a rank. And therefore now he was completely offended.

— By God, I'd hang you,— repeated Nozdrev,— I'm telling you this frankly, not to offend you, but simply speaking in friendship.

— There are limits to everything,— said Chichikov with a sense of dignity.— If you want to show off with such speeches, then go to the barracks,— and then he added: — If you don't want to give them as a gift, then sell them.

— Sell! But I know you, you're a scoundrel, you won't pay much for them?

— Eh, but you're a fine one too! look at you! are they diamond, or what?

— Well, that's just it. I knew you already.

— Good heavens, brother, what kind of Jewish motive is that? You ought to simply give them to me.

— Well, listen, to prove to you that I'm not some kind of miser, I won't take anything for them. Buy a stallion from me, and I'll give them to you thrown in.

— Good heavens, what do I need a stallion for?— said Chichikov, genuinely amazed by such an offer.

— What do you mean, what for? But I paid ten thousand for him, and I'm giving him to you for four.

— But what do I need a stallion for? I don't keep a stud farm.

— But listen, you don't understand: I'll take only three thousand from you now, and the remaining thousand you can pay me later.

— But I don't need a stallion, God be with him!

— Well, buy a dun mare.

— And I don't need a mare.

— For the mare and the gray horse that you saw at my place, I'll take only two thousand from you.

— But I don't need horses.

— You'll sell them, at the first fair they'll give you three times as much for them.

— Then it's better if you sell them yourself, since you're sure you'll gain three times as much.

— I know I'll gain, but I want you to profit too.

Chichikov thanked him for the goodwill and flatly refused both the gray horse and the dun mare.

— Well then, buy some dogs. I'll sell you such a pair, it simply gives you the shivers! broad-muzzled, with mustaches, hair standing up like bristles. The barrel-chested ribs are beyond comprehension, the paw is all compact, it won't touch the ground.

— But what do I need dogs for? I'm not a hunter.

— But I want you to have dogs. Listen, if you don't want dogs, then buy my hurdy-gurdy, a wonderful hurdy-gurdy; as an honest man, it cost me fifteen hundred. I'm giving it to you for nine hundred rubles.

— But what do I need a hurdy-gurdy for? I'm not a German, to drag it along the roads and beg for money.

— But this isn't the kind of hurdy-gurdy that Germans carry around. It's an organ; look at it specially: it's all made of mahogany. Here, I'll show it to you right now!— Here Nozdrev, seizing Chichikov by the arm, began to drag him into the other room, and however much the latter dug his feet into the floor and assured him that he already knew what kind of hurdy-gurdy it was, he still had to hear once more how Malbrough went off on campaign.— Since you don't want it for money, then here's what, listen: I'll give you the hurdy-gurdy and all the dead souls I have, and you give me your britzka and three hundred rubles thrown in.

— Well, what about that, and what will I ride in?

— I'll give you another britzka. Let's go to the shed, I'll show it to you! You'll just repaint it, and it'll be a wonder of a britzka.

"What an restless devil has possessed him!" — Chichikov thought to himself and resolved to get rid of all britzkas, hurdy-gurdies, and all possible dogs at any cost, despite the ribs' barrel-chestedness beyond comprehension and the compactness of their paws.

— But the britzka, the hurdy-gurdy, and the dead souls, all together!

— I don't want to,— said Chichikov once again.

— Why don't you want to?

— Because I simply don't want to, and that's that.

— What a fellow you really are! with you, as I see it, one can't,As is customary between good friends and comrades, such a one, truly!.. Now one can see that you are a two-faced man!

— But am I a fool, or what? judge for yourself: why should I acquire a thing that is decidedly unnecessary to me?

— Well, please don't say that. Now I know you very well. Such a rascal, truly! Well, listen, would you like to set up a little bank? I'll put all the dead souls on the card, and the hurdy-gurdy too.

— Well, to go into the bank means to expose oneself to uncertainty, — said Chichikov, and meanwhile he glanced sideways at the cards in his hands. Both deals seemed to him very much like artificial ones, and the very marking looked highly suspicious.

— Why uncertainty? — said Nozdryov. — No uncertainty at all! if only luck is on your side, you can win a devil of a lot. There it is! what luck! — he said, beginning to deal to stir up excitement. — What luck! what luck! there: it just hammers away! there's that cursed nine, on which I lost everything! I felt it would sell, but already, shutting my eyes, I think to myself: "The devil take you, sell, cursed one!"

When Nozdryov was saying this, Porfiry brought a bottle. But Chichikov refused decidedly both to play and to drink.

— Why don't you want to play? — said Nozdryov.

— Well, because I'm not in the mood. And, to tell the truth, I'm not at all fond of playing.

— Why not fond?

Chichikov shrugged his shoulders and added:

— Because I'm not fond.

— You're a worthless fellow then!

— What's to be done? that's how God made me.

— You're simply a Fetyuk! I used to think before that you were at least somewhat a decent man, but you don't understand any manners at all. There's no way to talk with you as with a close person… no straightforwardness, no sincerity! a perfect Sobakevich, such a scoundrel!

— But why are you scolding me? Am I to blame that I don't play? Sell me some souls alone, if you're such a man that you tremble over this nonsense.

— You'll get a bald devil! I wanted to, I wanted to give them for nothing, but now you won't get them! Even if you offer three kingdoms, I won't give them up. Such a sharpster, a nasty stove-maker! From now on I don't want to have any business with you. Porfiry, go tell the groom not to give oats to his horses, let them eat only hay.

Chichikov had not expected this last conclusion at all.

— You'd better simply not show yourself before my eyes! — said Nozdryov.

Despite, however, such a quarrel, the guest and the host had supper together, although this time there stood on the table no wines with fancy names. There was only one bottle of some kind of Cyprus wine, which was what is called sour stuff in every respect. After supper Nozdryov said to Chichikov, taking him aside into a side room where a bed had been prepared for him:

— Here's your bed! I don't even want to wish you good night!

Chichikov remained after Nozdryov's departure in the most unpleasant state of mind. He was inwardly vexed with himself, scolded himself for having stopped at his place and wasted time for nothing. But he scolded himself even more for having spoken to him about the matter, having acted carelessly, like a child, like a fool: for the matter was not at all of such a sort as to be entrusted to Nozdryov… Nozdryov was a worthless man, Nozdryov might lie, add, spread the devil knows what, and some sort of gossip might come out — that was not good, not good. "I'm simply a fool," — he said to himself. He slept very badly that night. Some very impudent little insects bit him unbearably painfully, so that he scratched the wounded spot with his whole handful, saying: "Ah, may the devil take you together with Nozdryov!" He woke up early in the morning. His first business was, having put on his dressing gown and boots, to go across the yard to the stable and order Selifan to harness the britzka that very moment. Returning across the yard, he met Nozdryov, who was also in a dressing gown, with a pipe in his teeth.

Nozdryov greeted him in a friendly way and asked how he had slept.

— So-so, — answered Chichikov very dryly.

— And I, brother, — said Nozdryov, — such filth kept creeping in all night that it's disgusting to tell, and after yesterday my mouth feels as if a squadron had spent the night in it. Imagine: I dreamed that I was flogged, upon my word! and, imagine, by whom? You'll never guess for anything: Staff-Captain Potseluyev together with Kuvshinnikov.

"Yes,"— thought Chichikov to himself, — it would be good if you were thrashed in reality."

— By God! it hurts a lot! I woke up: damn it, something is really itching, — must be witch fleas. Well, you go and get dressed now, I'll come to you right away. I just need to curse out that scoundrel of a clerk.

Chichikov went to his room to get dressed and wash up. When he came out to the dining room after that, a tea set with a bottle of rum was already standing on the table. There were traces of yesterday's dinner and supper in the room; it seemed the floor brush had not touched it at all. Bread crumbs were scattered on the floor, and tobacco ash was even visible on the tablecloth. The host himself, who did not delay in entering soon, had nothing on under his dressing gown except an open chest, on which some kind of beard had grown. Holding a pipe in his hand and sipping from a cup, he was very good for a painter who does not like to paint sleek, curled gentlemen, like barbershop signs, or those cropped under a comb.

— Well, so what do you think? — said Nozdryov, after a brief silence. — You don't want to play for souls?

— I already told you, brother, that I don't play; buy — if you please, I'll buy.

— I don't want to sell, that wouldn't be friendly. I won't peel the skin off God knows what. At cards — that's another matter. Let's at least play a hand!

— I already said no.

— And you don't want to trade?

— I don't want to.

— Well, listen, let's play checkers, if you win — they're all yours. I have many of them that need to be crossed off the census. Hey, Porfiry, bring the checkerboard here.

— It's a wasted effort, I won't play.

— But this isn't banking; there can be no luck or cheating here: it's all skill; I even warn you that I don't know how to play at all, unless you give me something in advance.

"Let me, — Chichikov thought to himself, — play checkers with him! I used to play checkers not badly, and it's hard for him to pull tricks here."

— If you please, so be it, I'll play checkers.

— The souls go at a hundred rubles!

— Why so much? Fifty is enough if they go at fifty.

— No, what kind of pot is fifty? I'd better include in that sum some middling puppy or a gold seal for your watch.

— Well, if you please! — said Chichikov.

— How many moves will you give me in advance? — said Nozdryov.

— On what grounds? Of course, none.

— At least let me have two moves.

— I don't want to, I play poorly myself.

— We know how poorly you play! — said Nozdryov, moving a checker.

— It's been a while since I've picked up checkers! — said Chichikov, also moving a checker.

— We know how poorly you play! — said Nozdryov, moving a checker.

— It's been a while since I've picked up checkers! — said Chichikov, moving a checker.

— We know how poorly you play! — said Nozdryov, moving a checker, and at the same time with his cuff he moved another checker as well.

— It's been a while since I've picked them up!.. Eh, eh! what's this, brother? move it back! — said Chichikov.

— Move what?

— The checker, — said Chichikov, and at the same time he saw right before his nose another one, which seemed to be making its way to become a king; where it came from, only God knew. — No, — said Chichikov, getting up from the table, — there's no way to play with you! That's not how you play, three checkers at once!

— Why three? That's by mistake. One moved accidentally, I'll move it back, if you please.

— And where did the other one come from?

— What other one?

— That one there, that's making its way to become a king?

— Come on, as if you don't remember!

— No, brother, I counted all the moves and remember them all; you only placed it there just now. Its place is over there!

— How, where's its place? — said Nozdryov, blushing. — Yes, brother, as I see it, you're a fabricator!

— No, brother, it seems it's you who's the fabricator, and an unsuccessful one at that.

— Who do you take me for? — said Nozdryov. — Would I really cheat?

— I don't take you for anyone, but I will never play from now on.

— No, you can't refuse, — said Nozdryov, getting heated, — the game has begun!

— I have the right to refuse, because you don't play as befits an honest man.

— No, you're lying, you can't say that!

— No, brother, you're the one who's lying!

— I didn't cheat, and you can't refuse, you must finish the game!

— You won't make me do that, — said Chichikov coolly and,"Having approached the board, he mixed up the checkers.

Nozdryov flared up and came up to Chichikov so close that the latter stepped back two paces.

"I'll make you play! It's nothing that you mixed up the checkers, I remember all the moves. We'll set them up again just as they were."

"No, brother, the matter is finished, I will not play with you."

"So you don't want to play?"

"You yourself see that it's impossible to play with you."

"No, tell me straight out, you don't want to play?" said Nozdryov, coming still closer.

"I don't want to!" said Chichikov and, however, brought both hands closer to his face, just in case, for the matter was becoming really hot.

This precaution was very much in place, because Nozdryov swung his arm... and it could very well have happened that one of the pleasant and plump cheeks of our hero would have been covered with indelible dishonor; but, happily deflecting the blow, he seized Nozdryov by both his defiant arms and held him firmly.

"Porfiry, Pavlushka!" shouted Nozdryov in a frenzy, struggling to break free.

Hearing these words, Chichikov, so as not to make the household serfs witnesses of a seductive scene and at the same time feeling that holding Nozdryov was useless, released his arms. At that very moment Porfiry entered and with him Pavlushka, a stalwart fellow with whom it was quite unprofitable to have dealings.

"So you don't want to finish the game?" said Nozdryov. "Answer me straight out!"

"There's no possibility of finishing the game," said Chichikov and glanced out the window. He saw his britzka, which stood all ready, and Selifan seemed to be waiting for a wave of the hand to roll it up to the porch, but there was no possibility of getting out of the room: in the doorway stood two stalwart serf fools.

"So you don't want to finish the game?" repeated Nozdryov with a face burning as if on fire.

"If you played as befits an honest man. But now I cannot."

"Ah! so you cannot, you scoundrel! When you saw that it wasn't your side winning, so you cannot! Beat him!" he shouted in frenzy, turning to Porfiry and Pavlushka, and himself seized a cherry-wood chibouk in his hand. Chichikov turned pale as a sheet. He wanted to say something, but felt that his lips were moving without sound.
