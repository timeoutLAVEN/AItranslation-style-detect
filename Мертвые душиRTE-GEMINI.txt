DEAD SOULS

Text printed according to the edition: 

N. V. Gogol , Collected Works in seven volumes, vol. 5,

IKHL, M. 1967

VOLUME ONE

Chapter one

Through the gates of an inn in the provincial town of NN drove a rather handsome, small sprung britzka, the kind in which bachelors travel: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners possessing about a hundred peasant souls, in short, all those who are called gentlemen of the middle sort. In the britzka sat a gentleman, not a beauty, but not of bad appearance either, neither too fat nor too thin; it could not be said that he was old, yet he was not so very young either. His entry produced absolutely no noise in the town and was not accompanied by anything special; only two Russian peasants, standing at the door of a tavern opposite the inn, made a few remarks, which, however, related more to the carriage than to the person sitting in it. "Look at that," said one to the other, "look at that wheel! what do you think, would that wheel make it, if it happened, to Moscow or not?" — "It would make it," answered the other. "But to Kazan, I think, it would not make it?" — "To Kazan it would not make it," answered the other. With this the conversation ended. And also, when the britzka approached the inn, a young man was encountered in white dimity pantaloons, very narrow and short, in a frock coat with attempts at fashion, from under which was visible a shirtfront fastened with a Tula pin with a bronze pistol. The young man turned back, looked at the carriage, held his cap with his hand as it almost flew off from the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage drove into the yard, the gentleman was met by a tavern servant, or a "polovoy" as they are called in Russian taverns, lively and restless to such a degree that it was impossible even to see what kind of face he had. He ran out nimbly, with a napkin in hand, all long and in a long fustian surcoat with the back almost at the very back of his head, shook his hair and quickly led the gentleman up along the entire wooden gallery to show the room sent to him by God. The room was of a known kind, for the inn was also of a known kind, that is, exactly like the inns in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day travelers get a quiet room with cockroaches looking out like prunes from all corners, and a door to the next room, always blocked by a chest of drawers, where a neighbor settles, a silent and calm person, but extremely curious, interested in knowing all the details of the traveler. The external facade of the inn matched its interior: it was very long, two stories high; the lower one was not plastered and remained in dark red bricks, darkened even more by harsh weather changes and somewhat dirty by themselves; the upper one was painted with the eternal yellow paint; below were small shops with horse collars, ropes and pretzels. In the corner one of these shops, or rather, in the window, was stationed a sbiten-seller with a samovar made of red copper and a face as red as the samovar, so that from a distance one might think two samovars stood in the window, if one samovar did not have a beard as black as pitch.

While the newly arrived gentleman inspected his room, his belongings were brought in: first of all, a suitcase made of white leather, somewhat worn, showing that it was not for the first time on the road. The suitcase was brought in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a short sheepskin coat, and the valet Petrushka, a fellow about thirty years old, in a spacious second-hand surcoat, evidently from his master's shoulder, a fellow a bit sullen in look, with very large lips and a nose. Following the suitcase, a small mahogany casket was brought in with decorative inlays of Karelian birch, boot lasts and a roasted chicken wrapped in blue paper. When all this was brought in, the coachman Selifan went to the stable to busy himself around the horses, and the valet Petrushka began to settle in the small front room, a very dark little hole, where he had already managed to drag his overcoat and along with it some own smell of his,which was reported and followed by a bag containing various valet attire. In this cubbyhole, he fixed a narrow three-legged bed to the wall, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, as flat and beaten down as a pancake, and perhaps as greasy as the pancake he had managed to demand from the innkeeper.

While the servants were managing and bustling about, the gentleman headed to the common room. Any traveler knows very well what these common rooms are like: the same walls painted with oil paint, darkened at the top by pipe smoke and made glossy at the bottom by the backs of various travelers, and even more so by those of local merchants, for on market days merchants would come here in groups of 6 or 7 to drink their well-known pair of tea; the same soot-stained ceiling; the same smoky chandelier with a multitude of hanging glass pieces that jumped and jingled every time the waiter ran across the worn oilcloths, briskly waving a tray on which sat an abyss of teacups as numerous as birds on a seashore; the same wall-to-wall oil paintings — in short, everything the same as everywhere else; the only difference was that one painting depicted a nymph with breasts so enormous that the reader has surely never seen the like. Such a freak of nature, however, occurs in various historical paintings, brought to us in Russia at unknown times, from unknown places, and by unknown persons, sometimes even by our grandees, lovers of the arts, who bought them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who transported them. The gentleman took off his cap and unwound from his neck a woolen, rainbow-colored scarf, the kind a wife prepares with her own hands for a married man, providing proper instructions on how to wrap up, but for single men — I cannot say for certain who makes them, God knows, I have never worn such scarves. Having unwound the scarf, the gentleman ordered dinner. While the various dishes common in taverns were being served to him, such as: cabbage soup with a puff pastry, specially kept for travelers for several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, roasted pullet, pickled cucumber, and the eternal sweet puff pastry, always ready for service; while all this was being served to him, both reheated and simply cold, he made the servant, or waiter, tell all sorts of nonsense — about who kept the tavern before and who does now, and whether it yields much profit, and whether their master is a big scoundrel; to which the waiter, as usual, replied: "Oh, a big one, sir, a rogue." In enlightened Europe, as in enlightened Russia, there are now many respectable people who cannot eat in a tavern without talking to the servant, and sometimes even making a funny joke at their expense. However, the newcomer did not ask only empty questions; he inquired with extraordinary precision who the governor of the town was, who the presiding judge was, who the prosecutor was — in short, he did not miss a single significant official; but with even greater precision, if not with personal interest, he inquired about all the significant landowners: how many peasant souls each owned, how far they lived from the town, even what their character was and how often they came to town; he inquired carefully about the state of the region: whether there had been any diseases in their province — widespread fevers, any murderous agues, smallpox, and the like, and all so thoroughly and with such precision as to show more than mere curiosity. In his manners, the gentleman had something solid, and he blew his nose extremely loudly. It is unknown how he did it, but his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, completely innocent merit, nevertheless, won him much respect from the tavern servant, so that every time he heard that sound, he shook his hair, straightened up more respectfully, and, bowing his head from above, asked: is anything needed? After dinner, the gentleman drank a cup of coffee and sat on the sofa, placing a cushion behind his back, which in Russian taverns, instead of elastic wool, is stuffed with something extremely similar to brick and cobblestone. Then he began to yawn and ordered to be taken to his room, where, lying down, he slept for 2 hours. Having rested,at the request of the inn servant, he wrote on a scrap of paper his rank, first name, and last name for reporting to the proper authorities, the police. On the piece of paper, the waiter, descending the stairs, read out syllable by syllable the following: "Collegiate Councilor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, traveling on his own business". While the waiter was still deciphering the note syllable by syllable, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself went to see the town, with which he was, it seemed, satisfied, for he found that the town was in no way inferior to other provincial capitals: the yellow paint on the stone houses struck the eye forcefully, and the gray on the wooden ones darkened modestly. The houses were one, two, and one and a half stories high, with the eternal mezzanine, very beautiful in the opinion of provincial architects. In places, these houses seemed lost in the middle of a street as wide as a field and endless wooden fences; in places, they huddled together, and here more movement of people and liveliness were noticeable. There were signs, almost washed away by the rain, featuring pretzels and boots, here and there with painted blue trousers and the signature of some Arshavsky tailor; here a shop with caps, forage caps, and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fedorov"; there a billiard table was painted with two players in tailcoats, the kind that guests wear in our theaters when entering the stage in the final act. The players were depicted with aimed cues, arms somewhat twisted back, and crooked legs having just performed an entrechat in mid-air. Under all this was written: "And here is the establishment". Here and there, right on the street, stood tables with nuts, soap, and gingerbread that looked like soap; there an eatery with a painted fat fish with a fork stuck into it. Most often, one noticed darkened double-headed state eagles, which have now been replaced by the laconic inscription: "Drinking House". The pavement was rather poor everywhere. He also looked into the town garden, which consisted of thin trees that had taken root poorly, with supports at the bottom in the form of triangles, very beautifully painted with green oil paint. However, although these little trees were no taller than reeds, the newspapers had said of them, when describing the illumination, that "our town has been adorned, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, wide-branched trees giving coolness on a sultry day", and that "it was very touching to see how the hearts of the citizens fluttered in an excess of gratitude and poured forth streams of tears as a sign of appreciation to the governor". After asking the sentry in detail where one could pass most directly, if necessary, to the cathedral, to the administrative offices, to the governor, he went to look at the river flowing through the middle of the town, on the way tore off a poster nailed to a post so that, having come home, he could read it properly, looked intently at a lady of decent appearance passing along the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in military livery with a small bundle in his hand, and, casting his eyes over everything once more as if to remember the layout of the place well, went home straight to his room, supported slightly on the stairs by the inn servant. Having enjoyed some tea, he sat down at the table, ordered a candle, took the poster out of his pocket, held it to the candle, and began to read, squinting his right eye a little. However, there was little remarkable in the small poster: a drama by Mr. Kotzebue was being given, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplevin, Cora by Miss Zyablova, the other characters were even less remarkable; nevertheless, he read them all, reached even the price of the parterre, and learned that the poster had been printed at the typography of the provincial administration, then turned it over to the other side: to see if there was anything there too, but, finding nothing, rubbed his eyes, folded it neatly, and put it in his small casket, where he was in the habit of putting everything that came his way. The day, it seems, was concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour schi, and sound sleep to the full capacity of his pump, as they say in certain parts of the vast Russian state.

The entire next day was dedicated to visits; the newcomer set out to pay visits to all the town dignitaries. He paid a respectful visit to the governor, who, as it turned out, like Chichikov, was neither fat,neither thin in build, he wore the Anna around his neck, and it was even rumored that he had been recommended for a star;[ Star — the Order of St. Stanislaus.

] moreover, he was a very good-natured fellow and even embroidered on tulle himself sometimes. Then he set off to the vice-governor, then he was at the prosecutor's, at the chairman of the chamber's, at the police chief's, at the tax farmer's, at the head of the state factories… it is a pity that it is somewhat difficult to remember all the powerful of this world; but suffice it to say that the newcomer showed extraordinary activity regarding visits: he even appeared to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical board and the city architect. And then for a long time he sat in the chaise, thinking of whom else to pay a visit to, but there were no more officials to be found in the city. In conversations with these rulers, he knew very skillfully how to flatter each one. To the governor he hinted somehow in passing that entering his province was like entering paradise, the roads everywhere were like velvet, and that those governments that appoint wise dignitaries are worthy of great praise. To the police chief he said something very flattering about the city sentries; and in conversations with the vice-governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were still only state counselors, he even said by mistake twice: "your excellency", which they liked very much. The consequence of this was that the governor invited him to come to his home that same day for a small evening party, and the other officials likewise, on their part, some for dinner, some for a game of boston, some for a cup of tea.

About himself the newcomer, as it seemed, avoided speaking much; if he did speak, it was in some generalities, with noticeable modesty, and his conversation in such cases took on somewhat bookish turns: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and not worthy of much care being taken over him, that he had experienced much in his time, suffered in service for the truth, had many enemies who had even attempted his life, and that now, wishing to settle down, he was seeking to finally choose a place for residence, and that, having arrived in this city, he considered it an indispensable duty to pay his respects to its first dignitaries. This was all that was learned in the city about this new person, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor's party. Preparation for this party took over 2 hours of time, and here the newcomer showed such attention to his toilet as is not even seen everywhere. After a short post-dinner nap, he ordered washing water and for an extremely long time rubbed both cheeks with soap, propping them from the inside with his tongue; then, taking a towel from the shoulder of the tavern servant, he wiped his full face from all sides with it, starting from behind the ears and snorting twice first right into the face of the tavern servant. Then he put on a shirt-front before the mirror, plucked out two hairs protruding from his nose, and immediately after that found himself in a frock coat of bilberry color with a spark. Having dressed in this manner, he rolled in his own carriage along the endlessly wide streets, lit by the meager illumination from the windows flickering here and there. However, the governor's house was lit as if for a ball; a carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes before the entrance, postilion cries in the distance — in short, everything just as it should be. Upon entering the hall, Chichikov had to squint his eyes for a minute, because the glare from the candles, lamps, and ladies' dresses was terrible. Everything was flooded with light. Black frock coats flickered and rushed about separately and in clusters here and there, as flies rush about on white shining refined sugar in the time of a hot July summer, when an old housekeeper chops and divides it into sparkling fragments before an open window; the children all look on, gathered around, following curiously the movements of her rough hands raising the hammer, and the aerial squadrons of flies, lifted by the light air, fly in boldly, like full masters, and, taking advantage of the old woman's poor sight and the sun troubling her eyes, cover the tasty pieces here scattered, there in dense clusters. Satiated by the rich summer, which even without that sets out tasty dishes at every step, they flew in not at all to eat, but only to show themselves, to walk back and forth over the sugar heap, to rub their back or front legs against one another,or scratch under their wings with them, or, stretching out both front paws, rub them over their head, turn around and fly away again, and again fly back with new tiresome squadrons. Chichikov had not even had time to look around before he was already taken by the arm by the governor, who introduced him right then and there to the governor's spouse. The visiting guest did not lose his composure here either: he offered some compliment, quite appropriate for a man of middle age, holding a rank neither too large nor too small. When the established pairs of dancers pressed everyone against the wall, he, putting his hands behind his back, watched them for about two minutes very attentively. Many ladies were well-dressed and in fashion, others dressed in whatever God had sent to the provincial town. The men here, as everywhere else, were of two kinds: some were thin ones, who were always hovering around the ladies; some of them were of such a sort that it was difficult to distinguish them from those in Petersburg, they also had very thoughtfully and tastefully combed sideburns or simply comely, very smoothly shaven ovals of faces, they sat down just as carelessly next to the ladies, spoke French just the same and made the ladies laugh just as they did in Petersburg. The other kind of men consisted of fat ones or those like Chichikov, that is, not so much too fat, yet not thin either. These, on the contrary, looked askance and backed away from the ladies and only looked around to see if the governor's servant was setting out a green table for whist anywhere. Their faces were full and round, some even had warts, someone was also pockmarked, they wore the hair on their heads neither in tufts, nor in curls, nor in the "devil take me" manner, as the French say, - their hair was either cut short, or slicked down, and their facial features were more rounded and sturdy. These were the honored officials in the city. Alas! the fat ones know better how to manage their affairs in this world than the thin ones. The thin ones serve mostly on special assignments or are merely on the rolls and wiggle here and there; their existence is somehow too light, airy and completely unreliable. The fat ones, however, never occupy indirect positions, but always direct ones, and if they sit down somewhere, they sit down reliably and firmly, so that the seat is more likely to crack and bend under them than they are to fly off. They do not like outward brilliance; on them the frock coat is not so cleverly tailored as on the thin ones, but instead there is God's grace in their caskets. Within three years, a thin one does not have a single soul left that is not pledged to the pawnshop; with a fat one it is peaceful, look - and a house has appeared somewhere at the end of the city, bought in the wife's name, then at the other end another house, then a small village near the city, then a whole village with all its lands. Finally the fat one, having served God and the sovereign, having earned universal respect, leaves the service, moves and becomes a landowner, a glorious Russian master, a hospitable host, and lives, and lives well. And after him again the thin heirs squander, according to the Russian custom, all the father's property on couriers. It cannot be hidden that almost such reflections occupied Chichikov at the time when he was examining the society, and the consequence of this was that he finally joined the fat ones, where he met almost all familiar faces: the prosecutor with very black thick eyebrows and a somewhat twitching left eye as if he were saying: "Let's go, brother, to another room, I'll tell you something there," - a person, however, serious and silent; the postmaster, a short person, but a wit and a philosopher; the president of the chamber, a very sensible and pleasant person, - all of whom greeted him like an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov bowed somewhat to the side, however, not without pleasantness. Right there he became acquainted with a very affable and polite landowner Manilov and the somewhat clumsy-looking Sobakevich, who stepped on his foot from the first moment, saying: "I beg your pardon". Right there they thrust a card for whist at him, which he accepted with the same polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not get up again until supper. All conversations completely ceased, as always happens when people finally give themselves over to a businesslike occupation. Although the postmaster was very talkative, even he, taking the cards in his hands,at the same moment expressed a thoughtful countenance on his face,

The next day Chichikov went to lunch and an evening at the police captain's, where from 3:00 after lunch they sat down to whist and played until 2:00 in the morning. There, among other things, he met the landowner Nozdrev, a man of about 30, a dashing fellow, who after three or four words began to address him as "thou". Nozdrev was also on "thou" terms and acted friendly with the police captain and the prosecutor; but, when they sat down to a high-stakes game, the police captain and the prosecutor examined his tricks extremely carefully and watched almost every card he played. The next day Chichikov spent the evening with the presiding judge, who received his guests in a somewhat greasy dressing gown, and including two certain ladies. Then he was at an evening at the vice-governor's, at a large lunch at the tax farmer's, at a small lunch at the prosecutor's, which, however, was worth a large one; at a snack after the liturgy given by the mayor, which was also worth a lunch. In short, he did not have a single hour to stay at home, and he came to the hotel only to fall asleep. The visitor somehow knew how to manage in everything and showed himself to be an experienced man of the world. No matter what the conversation was about, he always knew how to sustain it: if the talk was about a stud farm, he spoke about the stud farm; if they spoke about good dogs, here too he provided very practical remarks; if they deliberated regarding an investigation conducted by the treasury office — he showed that he was not unaware of judicial tricks; if there was a discussion about a game of billiards — and in the game of billiards he did not miss a beat; if they spoke of virtue, he reasoned about virtue very well, even with tears in his eyes; about the production of hot wine, and in hot wine he knew his business; about customs inspectors and officials, and he judged them as if he himself had been both an official and an inspector. But it is remarkable that he knew how to clothe all this in a certain gravity, he knew how to behave well. He spoke neither loudly nor softly, but exactly as one should. In short, whichever way you turned, he was a very decent man. All the officials were pleased with the arrival of the new person. The governor expressed that he was a well-intentioned man; the prosecutor — that he was a practical man; the gendarme colonel said that he was a learned man; the presiding judge — that he was a knowledgeable and respectable man; the police captain — that he was a respectable and amiable man; the wife of the police captain — that he was the most amiable and most courteous man. Even Sobakevich himself, who rarely spoke of anyone in a good way, having arrived home from town rather late and already completely undressed and lying in bed next to his thin wife, said to her: "My dear, I was at the governor's evening, and lunched at the police captain's, and met Collegiate Councilor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov: a most pleasant man!" To which the spouse replied: "Hm!" — and nudged him with her foot.

Such an opinion, very flattering for the guest, was formed about him in the town, and it lasted until a certain strange quality of the guest and an enterprise, or, as they say in the provinces, a passage, about which the reader will soon learn, brought almost the entire town to utter perplexity.

Chapter Two

For more than a week already the visiting gentleman lived in the town, driving around to parties and lunches and thus spending his time, as they say, very pleasantly. Finally he decided to take his visits outside the town and visit the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps he was prompted to this by another, more substantial reason, a matter more serious, closer to the heart… But the reader will learn about all this gradually and in due time, if only they have the patience to read the proposed tale, a very long one, which is to expand wider and more spaciously as it approaches the end, which crowns the work. The coachman Selifan was given the order to harness the horses to the well-known chaise early in the morning; Petrushka was ordered to stay at home, to look after the room and the suitcase. It will not be superfluous for the reader to become acquainted with these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not such prominent figures, and yet,what are called secondary or even tertiary, although the main plots and springs of the poem are not fixed upon them and only here and there touch and lightly catch them, — but the author loves extremely to be circumstantial in everything and in this respect, despite being a Russian person themselves, wants to be accurate, like a German. This will occupy, however, not much time and space, because not much needs to be added to what the reader already knows, that is, that Petrushka walked in a somewhat wide brown frock coat from his master's shoulder and had, according to the custom of people of their rank, a large nose and lips. By character he was more silent than talkative; he even had a noble impulse toward enlightenment, that is, reading books, with the content of which he did not trouble himself: it was absolutely all the same to him whether it was the adventure of an enamored hero, a simple primer, or a prayer book, — he read everything with equal attention; if chemistry were shoved under his nose, he would not have refused it either. He liked not what he read, but rather the reading itself, or, better to say, the process of reading itself, that there-look, out of letters some word eternally comes out, which sometimes the devil knows what it even means. This reading was performed mostly in a lying position in the entryway, on the bed and on the mattress, which had become from such a circumstance beaten down and thin, like a pancake. Besides the passion for reading, he had two other habits, which constituted two other of his characteristic traits: to sleep without undressing, just as he was, in the same frock coat, and to always carry with him a kind of his own special air, of his own personal smell, savoring somewhat of a lived-in room, so that it was enough for him only to set up his bed somewhere, even in a hitherto uninhabited room, and drag his overcoat and belongings there, and it already seemed as if people had lived in that room for 10 years. Chichikov, being a very fastidious person and even in some cases picky, having drawn in the air with a fresh nose in the morning, only winced and shook his head, saying: "You, brother, the devil knows you, are sweating, or something. You should at least go to the bathhouse." To which Petrushka answered nothing and tried to occupy himself right then and there with some business; or he would approach the hanging master's tailcoat with a whip, or simply tidy something up. What he thought at the time when he was silent, — maybe he said to himself: "And you, though, are a fine one, aren't you tired of repeating the same thing 40 times," — God knows, it is hard to know what a domestic serf person thinks at the time a master is giving them an instruction. So, here is what for the first time can be said about Petrushka. The coachman Selifan was a completely different person… But the author is very ashamed to occupy the readers for so long with people of a low class, knowing from experience how reluctantly they get acquainted with the lower estates. Such is already the Russian person: a strong passion to get to know someone who is at least one rank higher than them, and a nodding acquaintance with a count or a prince is better for them than any close friendly relations. The author even fears for their hero, who is only a collegiate counselor. Court counselors, perhaps, will get acquainted with him, but those who have already scrambled up to general ranks, those, God knows, perhaps will even cast one of those contemptuous looks that are cast proudly by a person at everything that crawls at their feet, or, what is even worse, perhaps will pass by with an inattention murderous for the author. But however distressing both may be, it is still, however, necessary to return to the hero. So, having given the necessary orders since evening, having woken up in the morning very early, having washed, having wiped themselves from head to foot with a wet sponge, which was done only on Sundays, — and that day happened to be Sunday, — having shaved in such a way that the cheeks became real satin in terms of smoothness and gloss, having put on a tailcoat of lingonberry color with a spark and then an overcoat on large bear skins, he went down the stairs, supported by the arm now from one, now from the other side by an inn servant, and sat in the chaise. With a thunder the chaise drove out from under the gates of the hotel into the street. A passing priest took off his hat, several boys in soiled shirts stretched out their hands, saying:"Master, give to a poor orphan!" The coachman, noticing that one of them was a great lover of hanging onto the back, gave him a lash with the whip, and the chaise began to hop over the stones. It was not without joy that the striped barrier was sighted in the distance, letting it be known that the pavement, like any other torment, would soon be at an end; and after hitting his head quite hard against the body of the carriage a few more times, Chichikov finally sped along over soft earth. Scarcely had the town receded, when, according to our custom, all sorts of nonsense and rubbish began to appear on both sides of the road: hillocks, fir groves, low sparse bushes of young pines, charred trunks of old ones, wild heather, and similar junk. There appeared villages stretched out in a line, built like stacks of old firewood, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations underneath in the shape of hanging embroidered towels. A few peasants, as usual, were yawning while sitting on benches before the gates in their sheepskin coats. Women with fat faces and bound breasts looked out from the upper windows; from the lower ones a calf peered out or a pig poked its blind snout. In short, familiar views. Having passed the fifteenth verst, he remembered that here, according to Manilov, his village should be, but even the sixteenth verst flew by, and the village was still nowhere to be seen, and if it were not for two peasants who happened to be coming toward them, they would hardly have managed to find the way.

To the question of whether the village of Zamanilovka was far, the peasants took off their hats, and one of them, who was the smarter one and wore a wedge-shaped beard, replied:

— Manilovka, perhaps, and not Zamanilovka?

— Well yes, Manilovka.

— Manilovka! and when you go one more verst, there you have it, that is, right to the right.

— To the right? — the coachman echoed.

— To the right, — said the peasant. — That will be your road to Manilovka; but there is no Zamanilovka at all. It is called that, that is, its name is Manilovka, but there is no Zamanilovka here at all. There, straight on the hill, you will see a house, stone, two stories, a manor house, in which, that is, the master himself lives. That is Manilovka for you, and there is no Zamanilovka here at all and never was.

They went off to seek Manilovka. Having driven two versts, they came upon a turn onto a country road, but already two, and three, and four versts, it seemed, had been covered, and the stone two-story house was still not visible. Here Chichikov remembered that if a friend invites you to his village fifteen versts away, it means that there are a good thirty to it. The village of Manilovka could entice few people by its location. The manor house stood alone on a height, that is, on an elevation open to all winds that might take it into their heads to blow; the slope of the hill on which it stood was covered with trimmed turf. On it were scattered in the English fashion two or three flowerbeds with bushes of lilacs and yellow acacias; five or six birches in small clusters here and there raised their small-leaved, sparse tops. Under two of them was visible a summerhouse with a flat green dome, wooden blue columns, and the inscription: "Temple of Solitary Reflection"; lower down was a pond covered with greenery, which, however, is no rarity in the English gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this elevation, and partly along the slope itself, gray log huts stretched across and along, which our hero, for reasons unknown, immediately began to count and counted more than 200; nowhere between them was a growing tree or any greenery; everywhere only logs were visible. God alone perhaps could tell what Manilov's character was like. There is a kind of people known by the name: people so-so, neither this nor that, neither Bogdan in the city nor Selifan in the village, according to the proverb. Everyone has their own passion: for one, the passion turned to harriers; another thinks he is a strong lover of music and feels all the deep parts in it amazingly; a third is a master of dining dashingly; a fourth plays a role even one inch higher than the one assigned to him; a fifth, with a more limited desire, sleeps and dreams of how to take a walk with an adjutant-general, to show off to his friends, acquaintances, and even strangers; a sixth is already endowed with such a hand that feels a supernatural desire to turn up the corner of some ace of diamonds or a deuce, while the hand of a seventh just reaches out to produce order somewhere, to get closer to the person of the station master or the coachmen, — in a word, everyone has their own, but Manilov had nothing. At home he spoke very little and for the most part reflected and thought, but what he thought about was also perhaps known only to God. — Allow me, I will tell your coachman right now.

Then Manilov, with the same politeness, explained the matter to the coachman and even said "you" to him once.

The coachman, hearing that he needed to pass two turns and turn at the third, said: "We'll manage it, your honor," — and Chichikov drove off, followed for a long time by the bows and handkerchief-wavings of the hosts, who stood on their tiptoes.

Manilov stood on the porch for a long time, following with his eyes the retreating chaise, and when it had become completely invisible, he was still standing there, smoking his pipe. Finally, he entered the room, sat on a chair and gave himself up to reflection, inwardly rejoicing that he had provided his guest with a small pleasure. Then his thoughts drifted imperceptibly to other subjects and finally wandered God knows where. He thought about the well-being of a friendly life, about how good it would be to live with a friend on the bank of some river, then a bridge began to be built across this river, then a most enormous house with such a high belvedere that one could even see Moscow from there and drink tea in the open air in the evening and discuss some pleasant subjects. Then, that he and Chichikov arrived together at some society in fine carriages, where they charmed everyone with the pleasantness of their manner, and that the Tsar, having learned of such a friendship of theirs, promoted them to generals, and further, finally, God knows what, which he himself could no longer make out at all. Chichikov's strange request suddenly interrupted all his dreams. The thought of it somehow particularly failed to digest in his head: no matter how he turned it over, he could not explain it to himself, and he sat and smoked his pipe the whole time, which stretched until supper itself.

Chapter three

And Chichikov, in a contented frame of mind, sat in his chaise, which had long been rolling along the high road. From the previous chapter it is already clear what the main object of his taste and inclinations consisted of, and therefore it is no wonder that he was soon completely immersed in it, both in body and soul. The assumptions, estimates, and considerations that wandered across his face were evidently very pleasant, for every minute they left behind traces of a satisfied smirk. Occupied with them,he paid no attention to how his coachman, satisfied with the reception from Manilov's house servants, made very sensible remarks to the dappled gray off-horse harnessed on the right side. This dappled horse was extremely crafty and only made a show of pulling, while the bay shaft-horse and the chestnut off-horse, named Assessor because he had been acquired from some assessor, worked with all their hearts, so that the pleasure they derived from it was even noticeable in their eyes. "Cheat away, cheat away! I will outcheat you!" said Selifan, rising up and lashing the idler with his whip. "You mind your business, you German trouser-wearer! The bay is a respectable horse, he does his duty, I will gladly give him an extra measure because he is a respectable horse, and Assessor is a good horse too... Now, now! why are you shaking your ears? You fool, listen when you are spoken to! I will not teach you anything bad, you ignoramus. Look where he is crawling!" Here he lashed him again with the whip, adding; "Oh, you barbarian! You cursed Bonaparte!" Then he shouted at them all: "Hey there, my dears!" — and flicked all three, no longer as a punishment, but to show that he was pleased with them. Having delivered such pleasure, he turned his speech again to the dappled horse: "You think you can hide your behavior. No, you live by the truth if you want people to show you respect. Now, at the landowner's where we were, there are good people. I will gladly talk if a person is good; with a good person we are always close friends, fine companions; whether to drink tea or have a snack — gladly, if the person is good. Everyone shows respect to a good person. Now, everyone respects our master, because he, do you hear, fulfilled the state service, he is a collegiate councillor..."

Reasoning thus, Selifan finally wandered into the most remote abstractions. If Chichikov had listened, he would have learned many details relating personally to him; but his thoughts were so occupied with his subject that only a loud clap of thunder made him rouse himself and look around; the whole sky was completely overcast with clouds, and the dusty post road was sprinkled with raindrops. Finally, a thunderclap sounded a second time, louder and closer, and the rain suddenly poured as if from a bucket. At first, taking a slanting direction, it lashed one side of the carriage body, then the other, then, changing its mode of attack and becoming perfectly straight, it drummed directly onto the top of its body; the spray finally began to reach his face. This forced him to pull the leather curtains with two small round windows, intended for examining road views, and to order Selifan to drive faster. Selifan, also interrupted in the middle of a speech, realized that, indeed, there was no time to linger, pulled out some gray cloth rubbish from under the box seat, put his arms into the sleeves, grabbed the reins, and shouted at his troika, which was barely moving its legs, for it felt a pleasant relaxation from the instructive speeches. But Selifan could not remember at all whether he had passed two or three turns. Reflecting and recalling the road somewhat, he guessed that there had been many turns, all of which he had passed by. Since a Russian person in decisive moments finds what to do without entering into long deliberations, turning right onto the first crossroad, he shouted: "Hey there, my respectable friends!" — and set off at a gallop, giving little thought to where the taken road would lead.

The rain, however, seemed to have set in for a long time. The dust lying on the road quickly kneaded into mud, and it became harder for the horses to pull the chaise every minute. Chichikov was already beginning to worry greatly, not seeing Sobakevich's village for so long. By his calculation, it should have been time to arrive long ago. He looked out to the sides, but the darkness was such that you could gouge your eye out.

— Selifan! — he finally said, leaning out of the chaise.

— What, master? — Selifan replied.

— Look and see if the village is visible?

— No, master, it is not visible anywhere! — After which Selifan, waving his whip, struck up a song that was not a song, but something so long that there was no end to it. Everything went into it: all the encouraging and prompting cries,with which horses are treated all over Russia from one end to the other, adjectives of all kinds without further distinction, whatever first happened to come to the tongue. Thus it reached the point that he finally began to call them secretaries.

Meanwhile, Chichikov began to notice that the chaise was rocking in all directions and dealing him mighty jolts, this made him feel that they had turned off the road and were likely dragging along through a plowed field. Selifan, it seemed, sensed it himself, but did not say a word.

— Well, you rogue, what road are you taking? — said Chichikov.

— Why, master, what can be done, the weather being what it is, you cannot see the whip, such darkness! — Having said this, he tilted the chaise so much that Chichikov was forced to hold on with both hands. Only then did he notice that Selifan was tipsy.

— Hold on, hold on, you will overturn us! — he shouted to him.

— No, master, how could I overturn us, — said Selifan. — It is a bad thing to overturn, I know that myself, I will certainly not overturn us. — Then he began to turn the chaise slightly, he turned and turned, and finally tipped it completely on its side. Chichikov flopped into the mud with both hands and feet. Selifan, however, stopped the horses, though they would have stopped on their own, for they were greatly exhausted. Such an unforeseen event completely amazed him. Climbing down from the box, he stood before the chaise, propped his hands on his hips while his master scrambled in the mud, struggling to get out, and said after some reflection: "Look at that, it did turn over!"

— You are as drunk as a cobbler! — said Chichikov.

— No, master, how could I be drunk! I know that it is a bad thing to be drunk. I had a talk with a friend, because one can have a talk with a good person, there is no harm in that, and we had a snack together. A snack is not an offensive thing, one can have a snack with a good person.

— And what did I tell you the last time you got drunk? eh? have you forgotten? — said Chichikov.

— No, your honor, how could I forget! I know my business already. I know that it is not good to be drunk. I had a talk with a good person, because...

— If I give you a flogging, then you will know how to talk with a good person!

— As your mercy pleases, — replied the ever-agreeable Selifan, — if a flogging is due, then a flogging it is, I am not at all against it. Why not a flogging if it is for a reason, that is the master's will. It is necessary to flog, because the peasant plays the fool, order must be observed. If it is for a reason, then do flog, why not a flogging?

To such reasoning the master found absolutely no answer. But at that moment, it seemed as if fate itself decided to take pity on him. From afar, the barking of a dog was heard. Delighted, Chichikov gave the order to drive the horses on. A Russian driver has a good scent instead of eyes, because of this it happens that, with eyes shut, he sometimes gallops at full speed and always arrives somewhere. Selifan, not seeing a thing, directed the horses so straight toward the village that he stopped only when the chaise struck the fence with its shafts and when there was resolutely nowhere else to go. Chichikov only noticed through the thick veil of pouring rain something resembling a roof. He sent Selifan to find the gates, which would, without doubt, have taken a long time if there were not dashing dogs in Russia instead of porters, who announced him so loudly that he put his fingers to his ears. A light flickered in one small window and reached the fence in a misty stream, showing our travelers the gate. Selifan began to knock, and soon, opening the wicket, some figure covered in a coarse coat poked out, and the master and servant heard a raspy female voice:

— Who is knocking? why all this walking about?

— Travelers, mother, let us in for the night, — Chichikov uttered.

— Look at that, what a quick-footed one, — said the old woman, — arriving at such a time! This is no inn: a lady landowner lives here.

— What can be done, mother: you see, we have lost our way. We cannot spend the night in the steppe in such weather.

— Yes, the time is dark, a bad time, — added Selifan.

— Be quiet, you fool, — said Chichikov.

— Well, who are you? — said the old woman.

— A nobleman, mother.

The word "nobleman" seemed to make the old woman think for a moment.

— Wait, I will tell the mistress,— she uttered, and within two minutes she had already returned with a lantern in hand.

The gates opened. A light flickered in another window as well. The chaise, having driven into the yard, stopped before a small house that was difficult to discern due to the darkness. Only one half of it was lit by the light coming from the windows; a puddle was also visible in front of the house, which the same light struck directly. The rain drummed loudly on the wooden roof and flowed in gurgling streams into a placed barrel. Meanwhile, the dogs broke out in every possible voice: one, throwing back its head, howled so long and with such effort as if it were receiving God knows what salary for it; another snapped off quickly, like a sacristan; between them, an irrepressible treble, probably a young puppy, rang out like a mail bell; and all this was finally topped off by a bass, perhaps an old fellow endowed with a robust canine nature, because he wheezed as a singing contrabass wheezes when a concert is in full swing: the tenors rise on their tiptoes from a strong desire to hit a high note, and everything there is strains upward, throwing back its head, while he alone, thrusting his unshaven chin into his necktie, crouching and descending almost to the ground, releases his note from there, making the window panes shake and rattle. From the dog barking alone, composed of such musicians, one could assume that the village was decent; but our soaked and chilled hero thought of nothing but bed. Before the chaise had completely stopped, he had already jumped onto the porch, staggered, and nearly fell. Another woman came out onto the porch, younger than the previous one but very similar to her. She led him into a room. Chichikov cast two passing glances: the room was hung with old striped wallpaper; pictures with some kind of birds; between the windows, small old mirrors with dark frames in the shape of curled leaves; behind every mirror was tucked either a letter, or an old deck of cards, or a stocking; a wall clock with painted flowers on the dial… it was impossible to notice anything more. He felt that his eyes were sticking together, as if someone had smeared them with honey. A minute later, the landlady entered, a woman of elderly years, in some kind of nightcap put on in haste, with flannel around her neck, one of those mothers, small landowners, who complain of crop failures, losses, and hold their heads somewhat to one side, yet meanwhile collect a little money in linen pouches distributed among the drawers of dressers. In one pouch they set aside all the silver rubles, in another the half-rubles, in a third the quarter-rubles, although to all appearances it seems as if there is nothing in the dresser except linen, and night blouses, and thread skeins, and a ripped-up cloak destined to be turned into a dress later if the old one somehow burns during the baking of holiday cakes with all sorts of fritters or wears out by itself. But the dress will not burn and will not wear out by itself: the old woman is thrifty, and the cloak is destined to lie for a long time in a ripped-up state, and then go by a spiritual will to a niece of a second cousin along with all the other junk.

Chichikov apologized for disturbing her with an unexpected arrival.

— It is nothing, nothing, — said the landlady. — At what a time God has brought you! Such turmoil and blizzard… You ought to eat something after the road, but the time is late at night, it is impossible to prepare anything.

The landlady's words were interrupted by a strange hissing, so that the guest was startled; the noise was as if the whole room had filled with snakes; but, looking up, he calmed down, for he realized that the wall clock had taken a fancy to strike. The hissing was immediately followed by a wheezing, and finally, straining with all its might, it struck 2 o'clock with a sound as if someone were beating a stick against a broken pot, after which the pendulum went back to ticking calmly to the right and to the left.

Chichikov thanked the landlady, saying that he needed nothing, that she should not worry about anything, that he required nothing except a bed, and was curious only to know what places he had driven into and how far from here were the paths to the landowner Sobakevich, to which the old woman said,that she had never heard of such a name and that there was no such landowner at all.

— At least do you know Manilov? — said Chichikov.

— And who might Manilov be?

— A landowner, mother.

— No, I haven't heard of him, there is no such landowner.

— Which ones are there?

— Bobrov, Svinyin, Kanapatyev, Kharpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov.

— Rich people or not?

— No, father, there aren't any who are too rich. Some have 20 souls, some 30, but as for those with a 100, there are none like that.

Chichikov noticed that he had driven into quite a remote wilderness.

— How far is it at least to the town?

— It would be about 60 versts. How I regret that I have nothing for you to eat! Wouldn't you like some tea, father?

— Thank you, mother. I need nothing but a bed.

— True, after such a journey one really needs to rest. You can settle right here, father, on this sofa. Hey, Fetinya, bring a feather bed, pillows, and a sheet. What weather God has sent: such thunder — I had a candle burning before the icon all night. Oh, my father, why, your whole back and side are covered in mud like a boar's! Where did you manage to get so filthy?

— It's still a mercy to God that I only got filthy, I should be thankful I didn't break my ribs entirely.

— Saints alive, what horrors! Don't you need something to rub your back with?

— Thank you, thank you. Don't worry, just tell your girl to dry out and clean my clothes.

— Do you hear, Fetinya! — said the hostess, turning to the woman coming out onto the porch with a candle, who had already managed to drag in the feather bed and, having fluffed it from both sides with her hands, released a whole flood of feathers throughout the room. — You take his coat along with his undergarments and first dry them before the fire, as we used to do for the late master, and then rub them over and beat them out well.

— I hear you, mistress! — said Fetinya, spreading a sheet over the feather bed and placing the pillows.

— Well, there's your bed ready, — said the hostess. — Goodbye, father, I wish you a peaceful night. Do you need anything else? Perhaps, my father, you are used to someone scratching your heels at night? My late husband could never fall asleep without it.

But the guest declined even the scratching of his heels. The hostess left, and he immediately hurried to undress, handing Fetinya all the gear he had taken off, both upper and lower, and Fetinya, also wishing him a peaceful night on her part, hauled away this wet armor. Left alone, he looked with no small pleasure at his bed, which was almost up to the ceiling. Fetinya, as was evident, was a master at fluffing feather beds. When, having placed a chair, he climbed onto the bed, it sank under him almost to the very floor, and the feathers, displaced by him from their bounds, flew into all corners of the room. Having extinguished the candle, he covered himself with a cotton quilt and, curling up under it like a pretzel, fell asleep that very minute. He woke up the next day already in the fairly late morning. The sun shone through the window directly into his eyes, and the flies, which yesterday slept peacefully on the walls and ceiling, all turned to him: one sat on his lip, another on his ear, a third intended to settle on his very eye, while the one that had the imprudence to sit close to a nostril, he inhaled in his sleep right into his nose, which made him sneeze loudly —

the circumstance that had been the cause of his awakening. Glancing around the room, he now noticed that the pictures were not all birds: among them hung a portrait of Kutuzov and an oil painting of some old man with red cuffs on his uniform, such as were worn in the time of Pavel Petrovich. The clock again emitted a hiss and struck ten; a woman's face peeped into the door and vanished in the same instant, for Chichikov, wishing to sleep better, had taken off absolutely everything. The face that peeped in seemed somewhat familiar to him. He began to recall who it might be, and finally remembered that it was the hostess. He put on his shirt; his clothes, already dried and cleaned, lay beside him. Having dressed, he went to the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that a turkey cock who had approached the window at that time — the window being very close to the ground — suddenly started gobbling something very rapidly to him in its strange language, probably "I wish you health", to which Chichikov called him a fool. Going to the window, he began to examine the views before him: the window looked out almost into a poultry yard; at least, the narrow little courtyard before him was entirely filled with birds and all kinds of domestic creatures. There was no counting the turkeys and chickens; among them a rooster paraded with measured steps, shaking his comb and turning his head sideways as if listening for something; a pig with her family appeared there as well; right there, scratching a pile of rubbish, she ate a chicken in passing and, without noticing it, continued to polish off watermelon rinds in her usual way. This small courtyard, or poultry yard, was enclosed by a plank fence, beyond which stretched extensive vegetable gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, beets, and other household vegetables. Apple trees and other fruit trees were scattered here and there through the garden, covered with nets for protection against magpies and sparrows, the latter of which moved from place to place in entire slanting clouds. For this same reason, several scarecrows were erected on long poles with outstretched arms; one of them wore the hostess's own cap. Beyond the gardens followed peasant huts, which, although built at random and not enclosed in regular streets, showed, according to Chichikov's observation, the prosperity of the inhabitants, for they were properly maintained: the decayed boards on the roofs had everywhere been replaced by new ones; the gates were nowhere crooked, and in the covered peasant sheds facing him, he noticed a spare, almost new wagon here, and two there. "Why, she has a village that is not small," he said and decided then and there to have a talk and get better acquainted with the hostess. He peeped through the crack of the door from which she had stuck out her head, and, seeing her sitting at a small tea table, he went in to her with a cheerful and kindly air.

— Good morning, father. How did you sleep? — said the hostess, rising from her place. She was dressed better than yesterday, — in a dark dress and no longer in a nightcap, but something was still tied around her neck in the same way.

— Well, well, — said Chichikov, sitting down in an armchair. — How are you, mother?

— Badly, my father.

— How so?

— Sleeplessness. My lower back always aches, and my leg, above the ankle, just throbs so.

— It will pass, it will pass, mother. Don't pay any mind to that.

— God grant that it passes. I have been rubbing it with hog's lard and also moistening it with turpentine. And what will you take with your tea? There is fruit liqueur in the flask.

— Not bad, mother, we'll have a sip of the fruit liqueur too.

The reader, I think, has already noticed that Chichikov, despite his kindly air, spoke, however, with greater freedom than with Manilov, and was not at all ceremonious. It must be said that if we in Russia have not yet caught up with foreigners in some other things, we have far outstripped them in the skill of social interaction. One cannot count all the shades and subtleties of our interaction. A Frenchman or a German will never comprehend or understand all its peculiarities and differences; he will speak with almost the same voice and the same language to a millionaire and to a small tobacco dealer, although, of course, in his soul he will play the sycophant to a certain extent before the first. With us it is not so: we have such wise men who, with a landowner having 200 souls,will speak quite differently than to one who has three hundred, and to one who has three hundred, they will speak differently again than to one who has five hundred, and to one who has five hundred, differently again than to one who has eight hundred, in a word, even if you go up to a million, they will still find shades of difference. Suppose, for example, there exists an office, not here, but in some far-off kingdom, and in the office, suppose, there exists a head of the office. I ask you to look at him when he sits among his subordinates, why, you simply cannot utter a word out of fear! pride and nobility, and what does his face not express? just take a brush and paint: Prometheus, a decisive Prometheus! He looks out like an eagle, steps smoothly, measuredly. That same eagle, as soon as he leaves the room and approaches the office of his superior, hurries along like a partridge with papers under his arm so fast there is no strength to watch. In society and at a party, if everyone is of a low rank, Prometheus will remain Prometheus, but just a little bit above him, such a transformation will happen to Prometheus as even Ovid could not invent: a fly, even smaller than a fly, reduced to a grain of sand! "But that is not Ivan Petrovich," you say, looking at him. "Ivan Petrovich is taller, and this one is both short and thin; that one speaks loudly, in a bass voice and never laughs, but this one, devil knows what: squeaks like a bird and laughs all the time." You come closer, look, it really is Ivan Petrovich! "Ehe-he," you think to yourself... But, however, let us return to the characters. Chichikov, as we have already seen, decided not to stand on ceremony at all and therefore, taking a cup of tea in his hands and pouring some fruit liqueur into it, led the conversation thus:

— You have, mother, a nice little village. How many souls are in it?

— Souls in it, my dear, are nearly 80, — said the hostess, — but the trouble is, times are bad, even last year there was such a crop failure that God forbid.

— However, the peasants look sturdy, the huts are strong. And allow me to ask your surname. I was so distracted... arrived at night...:

— Korobochka, a collegiate secretary's widow.

— I thank you most humbly. And your first name and patronymic?

— Nastasya Petrovna.

— Nastasya Petrovna? a good name Nastasya Petrovna. I have a biological aunt, my mother's sister, Nastasya Petrovna.

— And what is your name? — asked the landowner. — For you are, I expect, an assessor?

— No, mother, — replied Chichikov, smiling, — I expect not an assessor, but just traveling on my own little business.

— Ah, so you are a buyer! What a pity, truly, that I sold the honey to merchants so cheaply, for you, my dear, would surely have bought it from me.

— But honey is exactly what I would not have bought.

— What else then? Perhaps hemp? But then, I have little hemp now: only half a pood.

— No, mother, a different kind of little ware: tell me, have any of your peasants died?

— Oh, dear sir, 18 people — said the old woman, sighing. — And such fine folk all died, all workers. Since then, true, some have been born, but what good are they: all such small fry; and the assessor drove up — pay, he says, the tax per soul. The folk are dead, but pay as if for the living. Last week my blacksmith burned up, such a skilled blacksmith and knew locksmithing too.

— Did you have a fire, mother?

— God preserved us from such a disaster, a fire would be even worse; he burned up by himself, my dear. Something inside him caught fire, he drank too much, only a little blue flame came from him, he smoldered all over, smoldered and turned black like coal, and such a highly skilled blacksmith he was! and now I have no way to drive out: there is no one to shoe the horses.

— It is all God's will, mother! — said Chichikov, sighing, — one can say nothing against God's wisdom... Why not cede them to me, Nastasya Petrovna?

— Whom, dear sir?

— Why, all these ones who died.

— But how can I cede them?

— Just simply. Or, if you like, sell them. I will give you money for them.

— But how? I, truly, cannot grasp the meaning. Do you perhaps want to dig them up out of the ground?

Chichikov saw that the old woman had gone too far and that it was absolutely necessary to explain to her what the matter was. In a few words he explained to her that the transfer or purchase would exist only on paper and the souls would be listed as if alive.

— But what do you want them for? — said the old woman, bulging her eyes at him.

— That is my business.

— But they are dead.— But who says they are alive? That is exactly why they are a loss to you, because they are dead: you pay for them, and now I will relieve you of the trouble and the payment. Do you understand? Not only will I relieve you, but on top of that I will give you 15 rubles. Well, is it clear now?

— Truly, I do not know, — said the landlady with hesitation. — You see, I have never sold the dead before.

— I should think not! It would rather be a wonder if you had sold them to anyone. Or do you think there is actually some use in them?

— No, I do not think that. What use could there be in them, there is no use at all. The only thing that troubles me is that they are already dead.

"Well, the woman seems to be thick-headed!" — Chichikov thought to himself.

— Listen, mother. Just consider it properly: — you are being ruined, you pay the tax for them as if they were alive...

— Oh, my father, do not even speak of it! — the landlady cut in. — Only the week before last I paid over 150. And I greased the palm of the assessor.

— Well, you see, mother. And now just take into consideration that you no longer need to grease the assessor's palm, because now I am paying for them; I, not you; I take all the obligations upon myself. I will even execute the deed of sale at my own expense, do you understand that?

The old woman grew thoughtful. She saw that the matter did indeed seem profitable, but it was just too new and unprecedented; and therefore she began to fear greatly that this buyer might somehow cheat her; he had arrived from God knows where, and at night too.

— So what do you say, mother, is it a deal then? — Chichikov said.

— Truly, my father, it has never happened to me to sell the deceased. I have parted with living ones, why, three years ago I let the archpriest have two girls, at 100 rubles each, and he was very grateful, they turned out to be such fine workers: they weave napkins themselves.

— Well, the living are not the point; God be with them. I am asking for the dead.

— Truly, I am afraid at first that I might somehow suffer a loss. Perhaps you, my father, are deceiving me, and they... they are worth something more.

— Listen, mother... oh, what a person you are! What could they be worth? Look at them: they are dust. Do you understand? They are simply dust. You take any useless, most wretched thing, for instance even a simple rag, and even a rag has a price: at least it will be bought for a paper mill, but this is needed for nothing. Well, tell me yourself, what is it needed for?

— That is certainly true. It is needed for absolutely nothing; but the only thing that stops me is that they are already dead.

"Oh, what a blockhead she is! — Chichikov said to himself, already beginning to lose his patience. — Try and deal with her! She has put me into a sweat, the cursed old woman!" Here he, taking a handkerchief from his pocket, began to wipe the sweat that had indeed appeared on his forehead. However, Chichikov was angry in vain: many a respectable, and even a statesmanlike person, turns out in practice to be a complete Korobochka. Once they get something into their head, there is no overcoming it; no matter how many arguments you present to them, clear as day, everything bounces off them like a rubber ball bounces off a wall. Having wiped the sweat, Chichikov decided to try whether he could lead her to the path from some other side.

— You, mother, — he said, — either do not want to understand my words, or you talk like that on purpose just to have something to say... I am giving you money: 15 rubles in banknotes. Do you understand? That is money. You will not find it in the street. Now, confess, how much did you sell the honey for?

— At 12 rubles a pood.

— You have taken a little sin upon your soul, mother. You did not sell it at 12.

— By God, I sold it.

— Well, you see? But then, that is honey. You gathered it, perhaps, for about a year, with cares, with diligence, with trouble; you traveled, you stifled the bees, you fed them in the cellar all winter; but dead souls are a matter not of this world. Here you have applied no diligence on your part, it was the will of God that they should leave this world, bringing loss to your household. There you received 12 rubles for labor and diligence, but here you take for nothing, for free, and not 12, but 15, and not in silver, but all in blue banknotes.— After such strong persuasions, Chichikov was almost no longer in doubt that the old woman would finally yield.

— Really, — replied the landowner, — my widow's affairs are so inexperienced! I had better wait a little, maybe merchants will come by, and I will get a sense of the prices.

— Shame, shame, mother! Just a shame! Well, what are you saying, think for yourself! Who else would buy them? Well, what use could one make of them?

Things are strangely arranged in this world: the cheerful instantly turns into the sad if only you stand before it too long, and then god knows what will wander into your head. Perhaps you will even start to think: well now, is Korobochka really so low on the infinite ladder of human perfection? Having driven up to the tavern, Chichikov ordered a stop for two reasons. On the one hand, to give the horses a rest, and on the other, to have a bit of a snack and refresh himself. The author must confess that he greatly envies the appetite and stomach of this sort of people. For him, all the high-ranking gentlemen living in St. Petersburg and Moscow, spending their time pondering what to eat tomorrow and what dinner to compose for the day after, and sitting down to that dinner only after first sending a pill into their mouths, mean absolutely nothing; swallowing oysters, sea spiders and other wonders, and then setting off for Carlsbad or the Caucasus. No, these gentlemen never excited envy in him. But the gentlemen of the middle sort, who at one station demand ham, at another a piglet, at a third a slice of sturgeon or some baked sausage with onions and then as if nothing had happened sit down at the table at whatever time you like,and sterlet soup with burbot and milt hisses and grunts between their teeth, eaten with a rasstegai or a kulebyaka with a somoviy plyos, [ Somoviy plyos — "the tail of a catfish, consisting entirely of fat." (From N. V. Gogol's notebook.)

] so that the appetite is stirred even in a bystander, — these gentlemen, truly, enjoy an enviable gift from heaven! More than one gentleman of high standing would sacrifice half of his peasant souls and half of his mortgaged estates this very moment, just to have a stomach like that of a gentleman of middle standing; but the trouble is that no amount of money, nor even estates, with or without improvements, can acquire a stomach such as a gentleman of middle standing possesses.

The dark wooden inn received Chichikov under its narrow hospitable canopy on turned wooden pillars, resembling old church candlesticks. — Give him here, put him here on the floor!

Porfiry put the puppy on the floor, who, stretching out on all four paws, sniffed the ground.

— There is a puppy! — said Nozdrev, picking him up by the scruff of his neck. The puppy let out a rather pitiful howl.

— You didn't do what I told you, though, — said Nozdrev, turning to Porfiry and examining the puppy's belly, — and didn't think to brush him?

— No, I did brush him.

— Then why are there fleas?

— I cannot know. Perhaps they crawled in somehow from the chaise.

— You lie, you lie, you didn't even think of brushing him; I think, you fool, you let your own on him. Look here, Chichikov, look what ears, here, feel them with your hand.

— Why, I can see as it is: a good breed! — Chichikov replied.

— No, take them on purpose, feel the ears!

Chichikov, to please him, felt the ears, remarking:

— Yes, it will be a good dog.

— And the nose, do you feel how cold it is? take it with your hand.

Not wishing to offend him, Chichikov took it by the nose as well, saying:

— A good scent.

— A real bulldog, — Nozdrev continued, — and, I confess, I've had my eye on a bulldog for a long time. Here, Porfiry, take him away!

Porfiry, taking the puppy under its belly, carried him to the chaise.

— Listen, Chichikov, you must absolutely come to my place now, it is only five versts, we will be there in a flash, and then, if you like, you can go to Sobakevich's.

«Well, — Chichikov thought to himself, — I will indeed stop by Nozdrev's. Why is he any worse than others, he is a person just the same, and he even lost at gambling. He seems to be good at everything, so maybe I can get something out of him for nothing».

— Very well, let's go, — he said, — but on the condition not to delay, my time is precious.

— Well, my soul, that's it! That's good, wait, I'll kiss you for that. — Here Nozdrev and Chichikov kissed. — Splendid: the three of us will roll along!

— No, please, let me go, — said the blond man, — I need to go home.

— Nonsense, nonsense, brother, I won't let you go.

— Truly, my wife will be angry; you can switch to their chaise now.

— No, no, no! Don't even think of it.

The blond man was one of those people,whose character at first glance seems to possess a certain stubbornness. Even before you have time to open your mouth, they are already prepared to argue and, it seems, will never agree to what is clearly opposite to their way of thinking, will never call a fool a wise person and, in particular, will never agree to dance to another's tune; yet it always ends with their character revealing its softness, with them agreeing to exactly what they rejected, calling the foolish wise and then going off to dance as well as can be to another's tune, — in short, they start smooth and end rough.

— Nonsense! — said Nozdrev in response to some remark by the fair-haired one, jammed a cap on his head, and — the fair-haired one followed after them.

— For the vodka, master, you did not pay... — said the old woman.

— Ah, good, good, mother. Listen, brother-in-law! Pay, please. I do not have a kopeck in my pocket.

— How much for you? — said the brother-in-law.

— Why, father, only a dvugrivenik, — said the old woman.

— You lie, you lie. Give her a poltina,[ A dvugrivenik (silver) — 80 kopecks in paper notes. A poltina — 50 kopecks in paper notes. ] that is more than enough for her.

— A bit little, master, — said the old woman, yet she took the money with gratitude and even ran in a hurry to open the door for them. She was not at a loss, because she had asked four times more than what the vodka was worth.

The arrivals took their seats. Chichikov's britzka drove alongside the britzka in which Nozdrev and his brother-in-law sat, and therefore all three of them could freely converse with one another during the journey. Following them, constantly lagging behind, was Nozdrev's small, wretched carriage on thin, local horses. Porfiry sat in it with a puppy.

Since the conversation which the travelers conducted among themselves was not very interesting for the reader, we would do better if we said something about Nozdrev himself, who, perhaps, will happen to play a role not entirely of the least importance in our poem.

Nozdrev's face is, surely, already somewhat familiar to the reader. Everyone has had to meet many such people. They are called dashing fellows, are known even in childhood and at school as good comrades and, for all that, happen to be very painfully beaten. In their faces something open, direct, and daring is always visible. They make acquaintances quickly, and before you can look around, they are already calling you "thou". They will start a friendship, it seems, forever: but it almost always happens that the one who became friends will get into a fight with them that very same evening at a friendly drinking party. They are always talkers, revelers, reckless drivers, people of notice. Nozdrev at 35 years old was exactly the same as he was at 18 and 20: a lover of carousing. Marriage did not change him at all, especially since his wife soon departed for the other world, leaving two youngsters, who were decidedly not needed by him. The children, however, were looked after by a pretty nurse. At home he could not sit for more than a day. His sensitive nose smelled from several dozen versts away where there was a fair with all sorts of gatherings and balls; in the blink of an eye he was already there, arguing and stirring up confusion at the green table, for he had, like all such people, a little passion for cards. At cards, as we have already seen from the first chapter, he played not entirely sinlessly and cleanly, knowing many different tricks and other subtleties, and therefore the game very often ended with another game: either they beat him with boots, or they gave a working over to his thick and very good sideburns, so that he returned home sometimes with only one sideburn, and even that a rather thin one. Но his healthy and full cheeks were so well created and contained within them so much vegetative power, that the sideburns soon grew again, even better than the previous ones. And what is strangest of all, what can only happen in Russia, after some time he would already meet again with those friends who had thrashed him, and meet as if nothing had happened, and he, as they say, was fine, and they were fine.

Nozdrev was in a certain respect a historical person. Not a single gathering where he was present went by without a history. Some kind of history inevitably occurred: either gendarmes would lead him out of the hall by his arms, or his own friends would be forced to shove him out.If this does not happen, then something else will occur that never happens to anyone else: either they get so drunk at the buffet that they do nothing but laugh, or they lie so outrageously that they eventually feel ashamed themselves. And they will lie for absolutely no reason: suddenly claiming they owned a horse with some kind of blue or pink fur, and similar nonsense, so that the listeners finally all walk away, saying: "Well, friend, it seems you have already started spinning yarns." There are people who have a little passion for playing dirty tricks on their neighbor, sometimes for no reason at all. Someone, for example, even a person of high rank, with a noble appearance, with a star on their chest, will shake your hand, talk with you about deep subjects that provoke reflection, and then, you see, right there, before your eyes, they will play a dirty trick on you. And they will do it like a common collegiate registrar, and not at all like a person with a star on their chest talking about subjects that provoke reflection, so that you just stand there wondering, shrugging your shoulders, and nothing more. Nozdrev had the same strange passion. The closer someone became to him, the sooner he would play a trick on them: he would spread a tall tale so stupid it would be hard to invent anything worse, break up a wedding, a business deal, and not at all consider himself your enemy; on the contrary, if chance led him to meet you again, he would behave in a friendly manner once more and even say: "You are such a scoundrel, you never come to see me." Nozdrev was in many respects a versatile person, that is, a person for all trades. In the same minute he would offer to go with you anywhere, even to the end of the world, enter into any enterprise you wanted, exchange everything he had for anything you wished. A gun, a dog, a horse — everything was an object of exchange, but not at all for the sake of profit: it happened simply from some restless nimbleness and liveliness of character. If at a fair he was lucky enough to stumble upon a simpleton and win against them, he would buy a heap of everything that had previously caught his eye in the shops: horse collars, scented candles, kerchiefs for the nanny, a stallion, raisins, a silver washbasin, Dutch linen, fine flour, tobacco, pistols, herrings, pictures, sharpening tools, pots, boots, earthenware dishes — as far as the money lasted. However, it rarely happened that this was brought home; almost on the same day it was all lost to another, luckier player, sometimes even his own pipe with its pouch and mouthpiece was added, and another time the entire team of four with everything: the carriage and the coachman, so that the owner himself went off in a short jacket or an arkhaluk to look for some friend to use their carriage. That is what Nozdrev was like! Perhaps they will call him a cliché character, they will say that there is no Nozdrev anymore. Alas! those who say so will be unjust. Nozdrev will not disappear from the world for a long time yet. He is everywhere among us and, perhaps, only walks in a different coat; but people are thoughtlessly unobservant, and a person in a different coat seems to them a different person.

Meanwhile, three carriages had already pulled up to the porch of Nozdrev's house. There was no preparation in the house for their reception. In the middle of the dining room stood wooden trestles, and two peasants, standing on them, were whitewashing the walls, droning out some endless song; the floor was all splashed with whitewash. Nozdrev ordered the peasants and the trestles out at once and ran into another room to give orders. The guests heard him ordering dinner from the cook; realizing this, Chichikov, who was already beginning to feel somewhat hungry, saw that they would not sit down at the table before five o'clock. Nozdrev, having returned, led the guests to inspect everything he had in the village, and in a little over two hours showed absolutely everything, so that there was nothing left to show. First of all they went to inspect the stable, where they saw two mares, one dappled gray, the other chestnut, then a bay stallion, unremarkable in appearance, but for which Nozdrev swore he had paid ten thousand.

— You did not give ten thousand for him, — noted the brother-in-law. — He is not worth even one.

— By God, I gave ten thousand, —— said Nozdrev.

— You can swear as much as you like, — the brother-in-law replied.

— Well, do you want to bet on it! — said Nozdrev.

The brother-in-law did not want to bet.

Then Nozdrev showed the empty stalls where there had also been good horses before. In this same stable they saw a goat, which, according to an old belief, was considered necessary to keep with horses, and which, as it seemed, was on good terms with them, walking under their bellies as if at home. Then Nozdrev led them to look at a wolf cub that was on a leash. "Here is a wolf cub! — he said. — I purposely feed him raw meat. I want him to be a perfect beast!" They went to look at the pond, in which, according to Nozdrev, fish were found of such a size that two people could hardly pull one out, a fact which, however, the relative did not fail to doubt. "I will show you, Chichikov, — said Nozdrev, — an excellent brace of dogs: the hardness of their black flesh is simply amazing, their coat is like a needle!" — and he led them to a very handsomely built small house, surrounded by a large yard fenced on all sides. Entering the yard, they saw all kinds of dogs there, both long-haired and short-haired hounds, of all possible colors and coats: brindled, black and tan, fallow-piebald, brindled-piebald, red-piebald, black-eared, grey-eared... Here were all the names, all the imperative moods: Shoot, Scold, Flutter, Fire, Swagger, Scribble, Harass, Scorcher, Severga, Swallow, Reward, Patroness. Nozdrev was among them exactly like a father among his family; all of them, immediately putting up their tails, called "rules" by dog-fanciers, flew straight toward the guests and began to greet them. About ten of them put their paws on Nozdrev's shoulders. Scold showed the same friendship to Chichikov and, rising on his hind legs, licked him with his tongue right on the lips, so that Chichikov immediately spat it out. They inspected the dogs, which inspired amazement with the hardness of their black flesh — they were good dogs. Then they went to inspect a Crimean bitch, who was already blind and, according to Nozdrev, was soon to die, but two years ago she was a very good bitch; they inspected the bitch — the bitch, indeed, was blind. Then they went to inspect the water mill, where the casing was missing in which the upper stone is fixed, rotating rapidly on the spindle — "fluttering," according to the wonderful expression of the Russian peasant.

— And right here soon there will be a forge too! — said Nozdrev. After walking a little further, they saw, indeed, a forge, and inspected the forge as well.

— On this field, — said Nozdrev, pointing his finger at the field, — there are so many hares that you cannot see the ground; I myself caught one by its hind legs with my own hands.

— Well, you won't catch a hare by hand! — remarked the brother-in-law.

— But I did catch one, I caught it on purpose! — Nozdrev replied. — Now I will lead you to see, — he continued, turning to Chichikov, — the boundary where my land ends.

Nozdrev led his guests through a field, which in many places consisted of hillocks. The guests had to make their way between fallow lands and harrowed fields. Chichikov was beginning to feel tired. In many places their feet squeezed out water beneath them, so low was the ground. At first they were careful and stepped cautiously, but then, seeing that this served no purpose, they waded straight ahead, not distinguishing where the mud was deeper or shallower. After walking a considerable distance, they saw, indeed, the boundary, consisting of a wooden post and a narrow ditch.

— Here is the boundary! — said Nozdrev. — Everything you see on this side, all this is mine, and even on that side, all this forest, which looks blue over there, and everything beyond the forest, all is mine.

— But when did this forest become yours? — asked the brother-in-law. — Did you buy it recently? It used to not be yours.

— Yes, I bought it recently, — Nozdrev replied.

— When did you manage to buy it so quickly?

— Why, I bought it only the day before yesterday, and I paid dearly, damn it.

— But you were at the fair at that time.

— Oh you, Sofron! Is it impossible to be at the fair and buy land at the same time? Well, I was at the fair, and my steward here bought it without me.

— Yes, well, if it was the steward! — said the brother-in-law, but even then he doubted and shook his head.The guests returned to the house by the same foul road. Nozdrev led them into his study, in which, however, no traces were visible of what is usually found in studies, that is, books or paper; there hung only sabres and two guns — one worth 300, and the other 800 rubles. The brother-in-law, after examining them, only shook his head. Then Turkish daggers were shown, on one of which by mistake was engraved: "Master Savely Sibiryakov". Immediately after that, a barrel organ was shown to the guests. Nozdrev right then and there turned out something before them. The barrel organ played not without some pleasantness, but in the middle of it, it seems, something happened, for the mazurka ended with the song: "Malbrough s'en va-t-en guerre", and "Malbrough s'en va-t-en guerre" unexpectedly concluded with some long-familiar waltz. Nozdrev had already long stopped turning, but in the barrel organ there was one pipe, very spirited, that simply would not quiet down, and so for a long time it whistled on alone. Then pipes appeared — wooden, clay, meerschaum, smoked and unsmoked, covered in suede and uncovered, a chibouk with an amber mouthpiece, recently won, a tobacco pouch embroidered by some countess who had fallen head over heels in love with him somewhere at a post station, whose hands, according to him, were the most "subdilte superflu" — a word that probably signified for him the highest point of perfection. Having had a snack of dried sturgeon, they sat down at the table near 5 o'clock. Dinner, evidently, did not constitute the main thing in life for Nozdrev; the dishes played no great role: some things were burnt, some were not cooked at all. It was clear that the cook was guided more by some inspiration and put in the first thing that came to hand: if pepper stood near him — he poured pepper, if cabbage came along — he shoved in cabbage, stuffed in milk, ham, peas — in a word, hit or miss, as long as it was hot, and some kind of taste would surely come out. On the other hand, Nozdrev leaned on the wines: before the soup was even served, he had already poured the guests a large glass of port and another of "haut-sauterne", because in provincial and district towns there is no such thing as simple sauterne. Then Nozdrev ordered a bottle of Madeira to be brought, better than which even the Field Marshal himself had never drunk. The Madeira, truly, even burned in the mouth, for the merchants, already knowing the taste of landowners who loved good Madeira, mercilessly doctored it with rum, and sometimes poured in aqua regia, in the hope that Russian stomachs would endure anything. Then Nozdrev ordered yet another special bottle to be brought, which, according to him, was both "bourguignon" and "champagnon" together. He poured very zealously into both glasses, to the right and to the left, for both the brother-in-law and Chichikov; Chichikov noticed, however, as if in passing, that he did not add much to his own. This made him cautious, and as soon as Nozdrev got distracted talking or poured for the brother-in-law, he would that very minute empty his glass into his plate. In a short time, a mountain-ash vodka was brought to the table, having, according to Nozdrev, the perfect taste of cream, but in which, to one's amazement, the raw fusel oil was heard in all its strength. Then they drank some kind of balsam bearing a name that was even difficult to remember, and the host himself the next time called it by a different name. Dinner had long since ended, and the wines had been sampled, but the guests still sat at the table. Chichikov by no means wanted to talk to Nozdrev about the main subject in front of the brother-in-law. After all, the brother-in-law was an outsider, and the subject required a private and friendly conversation. However, the brother-in-law could hardly be a dangerous person, because he had loaded up, it seemed, quite enough and, sitting in his chair, was nodding his nose every minute. Noticing himself that he was not in a reliable condition, he finally began to ask for permission to go home, but in such a lazy and sluggish voice as if, according to the Russian expression, he were dragging a collar onto a horse with pincers.

"No, no! I won't let you go!" said Nozdrev.

— No, do not offend me, my friend, really, I must go, said the brother-in-law, — you will offend me very much.

— Nonsense, nonsense! we will set up a little game this very minute.

— No, brother, set it up yourself, but I cannot, my wife will be very much aggrieved, really, I must tell her about the fair. It is necessary, brother, really necessary to give her that pleasure. No, do not hold me back!

— Oh, to hell with her, your wife! as if you were going to do something truly important together!

— No, brother! she is such a respectable and faithful woman! She provides such services… believe me, I have tears in my eyes. No, do not hold me back; as an honest man, I shall go. I assure you of this on my true conscience.

— Let him go, what good is he! — Chichikov said quietly to Nozdrev.

— Quite right! — said Nozdrev. — I hate such softies to death! — and added aloud: — Well, the devil with you, go and coddle with your wife, you fetyuk![ Fetyuk — a word offensive to a man, derived from Fita — a letter considered by some to be an indecent letter. (Note by N. V. Gogol.) 

]

— No, brother, do not call me a fetyuk, — replied the brother-in-law, — I owe her my life. She is really so kind, sweet, she shows such affection… it moves me to tears; she will ask what I saw at the fair, I must tell her everything, she is really so sweet.

— Well, go then, tell her your tall tales! Here is your cap.

— No, brother, you should not speak of her like that at all; by doing so, one might say, you offend me personally, she is so sweet.

— Well, then clear out to her quickly!

— Yes, brother, I am going, excuse me for not being able to stay. I would be glad to with all my soul, but I cannot.

The brother-in-law continued repeating his excuses for a long time, not noticing that he had already been sitting in the chaise for quite a while, had long since passed through the gates, and that only empty fields had been before him for a long time. It must be assumed that the wife did not hear many details about the fair.

— Such rubbish! — said Nozdrev, standing by the window and looking at the departing carriage. — Look at how he dragged himself off! The side horse is not bad, I have wanted to get my hands on it for a long time. But there is just no way to get along with him. A fetyuk, a simple fetyuk!

After this they went into the room. Porphyry brought the candles, and Chichikov noticed a deck of cards in the host's hands that had appeared out of nowhere.

— Well, brother, — said Nozdrev, pressing the sides of the deck with his fingers and bending it slightly so that the paper cracked and snapped off. — Well, to pass the time, I am starting the bank at 300 rubles!

But Chichikov pretended as if he had not even heard what was being said, and said as if suddenly remembering:

— Ah! lest I forget: I have a request for you.

— What is it?

— Give me your word first that you will fulfill it.

— But what is the request?

— Come now, just give me your word!

— Very well.

— Word of honor?

— Word of honor.

— Here is the request: you have, I suppose, many deceased peasants who have not yet been struck off the census?

— Well, yes, what of it?

— Transfer them to me, in my name.

— And what do you want them for?

— Well, I need them.

— But what for?

— Well, I just need them… that is my business, in short, I need them.

— Well, you have surely got something up your sleeve. Confess, what is it?

— What could I have up my sleeve? One cannot start anything out of such a trifle.

— Then why do you need them?

— Oh, how curious! He wants to touch every piece of rubbish with his hand, and even smell it too!

— But why do you not want to say?

— What profit is it to you to know? Well, just because, it was a passing fancy.

— Then here it is: until you tell me, I will not do it!

— Now you see, that is already dishonest on your part: you gave your word, and then you back out.

— Well, have it your way, but I will not do it until you tell me what for.

"What should I tell him?" Chichikov thought, and after a minute's reflection declared that he needed the dead souls to acquire standing in society, that he did not possess large estates, so until that time he would have at least some souls or other.

— You are lying, you are lying! — said Nozdrev, not letting him finish. — You are lying, brother!

Chichikov himself noticed that he had not devised it very cleverly and the pretext was rather weak.

— Well, then I will tell you more directly, — he said, correcting himself, — only, please, do not let it slip to anyone. I have decided to get married; but you must know that the bride's father and mother are extremely ambitious people. It is such a predicament, really: I am not glad I got involved, they insist that the groom must have no fewer than 300 souls, and since I am short by almost 150 peasants…

— Well, you are lying! You are lying!— shouted Nozdrev again.

— Well, right here, — said Chichikov, — I haven't lied even this much, — and showed with his thumb on his pinky the smallest possible part.

— I'll bet my head that you're lying!

— However, that is insulting! what am I, really! why must I necessarily be lying?

— Well, I know you: you're a big scoundrel, let me tell you that as a friend! If I were your superior, I would hang you on the first tree.

Chichikov was offended by such a remark. Any expression, even slightly coarse or offending to propriety, was unpleasant to him. He did not even like to allow any kind of familiar treatment with himself, unless the person was of a very high rank. And therefore now he was completely offended.

— By God, I would hang you, — repeated Nozdrev, — I'm telling you this frankly, not to offend you, but simply speaking as a friend.

— Everything has its limits, — said Chichikov with a sense of dignity. — If you want to show off with such talk, then go to the barracks, — and then added: — If you don't want to give them as a gift, then sell them.

— Sell them! But I know you, you're a rascal, you won't give much for them, will you?

— Oh, but you're a fine one too! look at you! are they made of diamonds or something?

— Well, there it is. I knew you already.

— Good heavens, brother, what kind of a stingy impulse is this. You ought to just give them to me.

— Well, listen, to prove to you that I'm not a skinflint at all, I won't take anything for them. Buy a stallion from me, and I'll give them to you in the bargain.

— Good heavens, what do I need a stallion for? — said Chichikov, truly astonished by such a proposal.

— What for? why, I paid ten thousand for him, and I'm giving him to you for four.

— But what do I need a stallion for? I don't keep a stud farm.

— But listen, you don't understand: I'll only take three thousand from you now, and you can pay me the remaining thousand later.

— But I don't need a stallion, God be with him!

— Well, buy the bay mare.

— I don't need a mare either.

— For the mare and for the gray horse you saw at my place, I'll take only two thousand from you.

— But I don't need horses.

— You'll sell them, they'll give you three times as much for them at the first fair.

— Then you'd better sell them yourself, since you're sure you'll gain three times as much.

— I know I'll gain, but I want you to get a profit too.

Chichikov thanked him for the kind disposition and flatly refused both the gray horse and the bay mare.

— Well then, buy some dogs. I'll sell you such a pair, it simply makes your skin crawl! wire-haired, with whiskers, the coat stands up like bristles. The barrel-shape of the ribs is inconceivable to the mind, the paw is all in a clump, it doesn't touch the ground.

— But what do I need dogs for? I'm not a hunter.

— But I want you to have dogs. Listen, if you don't want dogs, then buy a barrel organ from me, a wonderful barrel organ; as an honest man, it cost me fifteen hundred myself. I'm giving it to you for nine hundred rubles.

— But why do I need a barrel organ? I'm not a German to go dragging it along the roads and begging for money.

— But this isn't the kind of barrel organ Germans carry. It's an organ; look at it on purpose: all made of mahogany. Here, I'll show it to you again! — Here Nozdrev, grabbing Chichikov by the arm, began dragging him into another room, and no matter how the latter dug his feet into the floor and insisted that he already knew what kind of barrel organ it was, he had to hear once more how Malbrough went on a campaign. — Since you don't want it for money, then listen to this: I'll give you the barrel organ and all the dead souls I have, and you give me your chaise and three hundred rubles to boot.

— Well, what next, and what will I travel in?

— I'll give you another chaise. Come to the shed, I'll show it to you! You'll just repaint it, and it will be a miracle of a chaise.

"How the restless devil has possessed him!" — Chichikov thought to himself and decided at all costs to get rid of any chaises, barrel organs, and all possible dogs, despite the barrel-shape of ribs and clumpiness of paws inconceivable to the mind.

— But the chaise, the barrel organ, and the dead souls, all together!

— I don't want them, — Chichikov said once more.

— Why don't you want them?

— Because I simply don't want to, and that's that.

— What a man you are, really! one can't deal with you, as I see,"as is common between good friends and comrades, such a person, really!.. Now it is clear that you are a double-faced man!

— Well, what am I, a fool, or what? You judge for yourself: why should I acquire a thing, strictly unnecessary for me?

— Well, please, do not talk. Now I know you very well. Such a, really, rascal! Well, listen, do you want to play a game of bank? I will put all the deceased on the card, the hurdy-gurdy too.

— Well, deciding on bank means subjecting oneself to uncertainty, — said Chichikov and meanwhile glanced sideways at the cards that were in his hands. Both decks seemed to him very similar to artificial ones, and the very markings looked very suspicious.

— Why uncertainty? — said Nozdrev. — No uncertainty! if only luck is on your side, you can win a devilish lot. There it is! what luck! — he said, starting to deal to stir up excitement. — What luck! what luck! look: it just keeps hitting! there is that cursed nine, on which I lost everything! I felt it would betray me, but already, closing my eyes, I think to myself: "The devil take you, betray me, you cursed thing!"

When Nozdrev was saying this, Porfiry brought a bottle. But Chichikov refused resolutely both to play and to drink.

— Why don't you want to play? — said Nozdrev.

— Well, because I am not disposed to. And, to tell the truth, I am not at all a fan of playing.

— Why not a fan?

Chichikov shrugged his shoulders and added:

— Because I am not a fan.

— You are rubbish then!

— What can be done? that is how God created me.

— Just a simpleton! I thought before that you were at least somewhat of a decent person, but you do not understand any social conduct. It is impossible to talk to you as with a close person… no straightforwardness, no sincerity! a perfect Sobakevich, such a scoundrel!

— But why are you scolding me? Am I to blame that I do not play? Sell me the souls alone, if you are such a person who trembles over this nonsense.

— You will get a bald devil! I wanted to, I wanted to give them for free, but now you will not get them! Even if you give three kingdoms, I will not give them. Such a trickster, a nasty stove-setter! From now on I want to have no business with you. Porfiry, go tell the groom not to give oats to his horses, let them eat only hay.

Chichikov did not expect this last conclusion at all.

— Better if you just did not show your face to me! — said Nozdrev.

Despite, however, such a falling out, the guest and the host had supper together, although this time there were no wines with fancy names on the table. Only one bottle stuck out with some kind of Cypriot, which was what they call sour stuff in all respects. After supper Nozdrev said to Chichikov, leading him into a side room where a bed had been prepared for him:

— Here is a bed for you! I do not even want to wish you good night!

Chichikov remained after Nozdrev's departure in a most unpleasant frame of mind. He was inwardly annoyed with himself, scolded himself for stopping by and wasting time for nothing. But even more he scolded himself for talking to him about the business, acting incautiously, like a child, like a fool: for the business was not of such a kind to be entrusted to Nozdrev… Nozdrev is a rubbish man, Nozdrev can lie, add things, spread God knows what, some more gossip will come out — it is not good, not good. "I am just a fool," — he said to himself. He slept very poorly that night. Some small, very lively insects bit him unbearably painfully, so that he scratched the stung place with his whole palm, saying: "Oh, may the devil take you along with Nozdrev!" He woke up in the early morning. His first business was, having put on his dressing gown and boots, to go across the yard to the stable to order Selifan to harness the chaise immediately. Returning through the yard, he met Nozdrev, who was also in a dressing gown, with a pipe in his teeth.

Nozdrev greeted him in a friendly way and asked how he had slept.

— So-so, — replied Chichikov very dryly.

— And I, brother, — said Nozdrev, — such filth was crawling all night that it is foul to tell, and in my mouth after yesterday it is as if a squadron spent the night. Imagine: I dreamed that I was flogged, really! and, imagine, by whom? You will never guess: Staff Captain Potseluev together with Kuvshinnikov.

"Yes,""It would be good," Chichikov thought to himself, "if you were actually thrashed in reality."

"By God! And how painful it was! I woke up: devil take it, something really is itching, must be witches' fleas. Well, you go and get dressed now, I will join you shortly. I just need to give the scoundrel of a steward a piece of my mind."

Chichikov went to his room to dress and wash. When he emerged into the dining room afterward, a tea service with a bottle of rum was already on the table. Traces of yesterday's lunch and dinner were present in the room; it seemed a broom had not touched it at all. Bread crumbs were scattered on the floor, and tobacco ash was even visible on the tablecloth. The host himself, who was not long in entering, had nothing under his dressing gown except an open chest, on which a sort of beard grew. Holding a pipe and sipping from a cup, he would have been a fine subject for a painter who dislikes the look of gentlemen who are slicked down and curled like barbershop signs, or cropped close with a comb.

"Well, so what do you think?" Nozdrev said, after a short silence. "Don't want to play for the souls?"

"I already told you, brother, that I do not play; as for buying, if you please, I will buy."

"I don't want to sell, that would not be friendly. I am not going to strip the skin off devil knows what. A game of banker is another matter. Let's deal at least one hand!"

"I already said no."

"And you don't want to trade?"

"I don't."

"Well, listen, let's play checkers, if you win, they are all yours. For I have many who should be struck from the census. Hey, Porfiry, bring the checkerboard here."

"It is a wasted effort, I will not play."

"But this is not a game of chance; there can be no luck or trickery here: it is all based on skill; I even warn you that I do not know how to play at all, unless you give me a head start."

"Let me see," Chichikov thought to himself, "I shall play checkers with him! I used to play checkers not badly, and it will be hard for him to pull off any tricks here."

"Very well, so be it, I will play checkers."

"The souls are at stake for 100 rubles!"

"Why so much? It is enough if they go for 50."

"No, what kind of stake is 50? Better yet, I will include some medium-grade puppy or a gold watch seal for that sum."

"Well, as you wish!" Chichikov said.

"How much of a head start will you give me?" Nozdrev said.

"On what grounds? None, of course."

"At least let me have the first two moves."

"I don't want to, I play poorly myself."

"We know you and how poorly you play!" Nozdrev said, moving a checker.

"It has been a long time since I picked up checkers!" Chichikov said, also moving a checker.

"We know you and how poorly you play!" Nozdrev said, moving a checker.

"It has been a long time since I picked up checkers!" Chichikov said, moving a checker.

"We know you and how poorly you play!" Nozdrev said, moving a checker, while at the same time moving another checker with the cuff of his sleeve.

"It has been a long time since I picked up... Eh, eh! What is this, brother? Move that one back!" Chichikov said.

"Move what?"

"The checker," Chichikov said, and at the same time saw another one right under his nose, which seemed to be making its way to becoming a king; only God knew where it came from. "No," Chichikov said, rising from the table, "it is quite impossible to play with you! One does not move like that, three checkers at once!"

"Why three? It was a mistake. One moved by accident, I will move it back, if you like."

"And where did the other one come from?"

"What other one?"

"This one here, that is making its way to the king row?"

"Well, I'll be, as if you don't remember!"

"No, brother, I counted all the moves and remember everything; you only just placed it there. Its place is over there!"

"What, its place is where?" Nozdrev said, turning red. "Why, you, brother, as I see it, are a storyteller!"

"No, brother, it seems you are the storyteller, but not a very successful one."

"Who do you take me for?" Nozdrev said. "Would I really start cheating?"

"I don't take you for anyone, but from this moment on I will never play."

"No, you cannot refuse," Nozdrev said, getting heated, "the game has begun!"

"I have the right to refuse, because you are not playing as befits an honest person."

"No, you're lying, you can't say that!"

"No, brother, you are the one lying yourself!"

"I did not cheat, and you cannot refuse, you must finish the game!"

"You shall not make me do that," Chichikov said coolly and,...approaching the board, he jumbled the checkers.

Nozdrev flared up and approached Chichikov so closely that the latter retreated two steps back.

— I will make you play! It is nothing that you jumbled the checkers, I remember all the moves. We shall set them up again just as they were.

— No, brother, the matter is finished, I will not play with you.

— So you do not want to play?

— You see for yourself that it is impossible to play with you.

— No, tell me straight out, you do not want to play? — said Nozdrev, stepping even closer.

— I do not want to! — said Chichikov and moved, however, both hands closer to his face just in case, for things were indeed getting heated.

This precaution was quite timely, because Nozdrev swung his arm... and it could very well have happened that one of our hero's pleasant and full cheeks would have been covered with indelible dishonor; but, having luckily parried the blow, he seized Nozdrev by both his provocative arms and held him firmly.

— Porphyry, Pavlushka! — Nozdrev shouted in a frenzy, struggling to break free.

Hearing these words, Chichikov, in order not to make the house servants witnesses to such a scandalous scene and at the same time feeling that holding Nozdrev was useless, released his arms. At that very moment, Porphyry entered and with him Pavlushka, a sturdy fellow with whom getting involved was not at all advantageous.

— So you do not want to finish the game? — said Nozdrev. — Answer me straight out!

— It is impossible to finish the game, — said Chichikov and glanced out the window. He saw his chaise, which stood completely ready, and Selifan seemed to be waiting for a signal to drive up to the porch, but it was absolutely impossible to get out of the room: two sturdy serf fools stood in the doorway.

— So you do not want to finish the game? — repeated Nozdrev with a face burning as if on fire.

— If you played as is fitting for an honest person. But now I cannot.

— Ah! so you cannot, you scoundrel! when you saw that things were not going your way, then you cannot! Beat him! — he shouted frantically, turning to Porphyry and Pavlushka, while he himself grabbed a cherry wood pipe stem in his hand. Chichikov turned as pale as a sheet. He wanted to say something, but felt that his lips were moving without sound.
