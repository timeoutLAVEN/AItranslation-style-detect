DEAD SOULS

Text printed from the edition:

N. V. Gogol, Collected Works in seven volumes, vol. 5,

IHL, Moscow 1967

VOLUME ONE

Chapter One

Into the gateway of the inn of the provincial town NN rolled a rather handsome small spring carriage, the sort bachelors ride in: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners with about a hundred serfs, in a word, all those whom are called men of moderate means. In the carriage sat a gentleman, not handsome, but not bad-looking either, neither too fat nor too thin; not exactly old, though not so young either. His arrival caused absolutely no stir in the town and was not accompanied by anything special. Only two Russian peasants standing by the tavern door opposite the inn made some remarks, though these referred more to the carriage than to the man inside it. "Look at that," said one to the other, "what a wheel! what do you think, would that wheel make it, if it happened to, all the way to Moscow or not?" "It would make it," replied the other. "And to Kazan, I suppose, it would not make it?" "To Kazan it would not make it," replied the other. And that was the end of the conversation. Also, when the carriage pulled up to the inn, he encountered a young man in white canvas trousers, very tight and short, in a frock coat with pretensions to fashion, from under which a shirtfront could be seen, fastened with a Tula pin with a bronze pistol. The young man turned back, looked at the carriage, held down with a hand the cap that was almost blown off by the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage drove into the courtyard, the gentleman was met by the tavern servant, or waiter, as they are called in Russian inns, so lively and fidgety that it was impossible even to make out what his face looked like. He ran out briskly, with a napkin in his hand, all long and in a long demi-cotton coat with the back almost up at the nape of his neck, shook out his hair, and briskly led the gentleman up the whole wooden gallery to show him the room granted by God. The room was of a known sort, for the inn was also of a known sort, that is, exactly the kind of inn found in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day travelers get a comfortable room with cockroaches peeping like prunes out of every corner, and a door into the neighboring room, always blocked by a chest of drawers, where a neighbor is installed, a silent and calm person, but extremely curious, interested in knowing all the details about the traveler. The exterior facade of the inn matched its interior: it was very long, two stories high; the lower part was not plastered and remained in dark red bricks, even darker from all manner of nasty weather changes and dirty enough already in themselves; the upper part was painted in an eternal yellow color. Down below were little shops with horse collars, ropes, and bagels. In the corner of one of these little shops, or rather in the window, sat a sbiten seller with a samovar of red copper and a face as red as the samovar, so that from afar one might have thought there were two samovars in the window, if one of the samovars had not had a black as pitch beard.

While the arriving gentleman was inspecting his room, his belongings were brought in. First of all, a suitcase of white leather, somewhat worn, showing that it was not on the road for the first time. The suitcase was carried in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a sheepskin coat, and by the valet Petrushka, a fellow about thirty, in a roomy secondhand coat, evidently from a master's shoulder, a fellow somewhat stern in appearance, with very large lips and nose. After the suitcase came in a small mahogany box with inlaid work of Karelian birch, boot lasts, and a roast chicken wrapped in blue paper. When all this had been brought in, the coachman Selifan went off to the stable to fuss around the horses, and the valet Petrushka began settling himself in the little front hall, a very dark little den, where he had already managed to drag his overcoat and with it some of his own peculiar smell,which was reported and the bag of various valet’s clothes that was brought along after it. In this little kennel he fixed a narrow three-legged bed to the wall, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, beaten down and flat as a pancake, and perhaps just as greasy as a pancake, which he managed to extort from the innkeeper.

Meanwhile the servants were getting things done and fussing about, and the master went to the common room. Anyone who travels knows very well what these common rooms are like: the same walls painted with oil paint, darkened at the top by pipe smoke and polished at the bottom by the backs of various travelers, and even more so by local merchants, since on market days the merchants came here in groups of six and seven to drink their well-known pair of teas; the same smoke-blackened ceiling; the same smoky chandelier with lots of dangling little glass pieces that jumped and jingled every time the waiter ran over the worn oilcloths, briskly waving a tray on which sat as many teacups as there are birds on the seashore; the same paintings covering the whole wall in oil colors—in short, everything the same as everywhere else; the only difference was that one painting showed a nymph with breasts so huge that the reader has surely never seen the like. Such a trick of nature, however, often appears in various historical paintings, brought to us in Russia, unknown when, from where, or by whom, sometimes even by our nobles, lovers of art, who bought them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who brought them. The master took off his cap and unwound from his neck a woolen scarf of rainbow colors, the kind a wife makes with her own hands for a married man, giving proper instructions on how to wrap up, and as for bachelors—I certainly cannot say who makes it, heaven knows, I never wore such scarves. Having unwound the scarf, the master ordered dinner to be served. Meanwhile, as the usual inn dishes were brought to him, such as cabbage soup with a puff pastry pie, specially kept for travelers for several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, roast chicken, a salted cucumber, and the eternal puff pastry sweet pie, always ready at hand; while all this was being served, both reheated and simply cold, he made the servant, or waiter, talk nonsense about who had owned the inn before and who owned it now, how much income it brought in, and what a scoundrel their boss was; to which the waiter, as usual, replied, “Oh, a great, sir, swindler.” As in enlightened Europe, so in enlightened Russia there are now very many respectable people who cannot eat in an inn without talking to the servant, and sometimes even joking around with him. However, the traveler was not asking only idle questions; he inquired with extraordinary precision who the governor of the city was, who the chairman of the chamber was, who the prosecutor was—in short, he did not leave out a single important official; but with even greater precision, if not concern, he asked about all the major landowners: how many serfs each had, how far they lived from the city, what sort of character they had, and how often they came to the city; he also inquired carefully about the state of the region: whether there had been any diseases in their province, outbreaks of fever, deadly fevers, smallpox, and the like, and all so thoroughly and with such precision that it showed more than simple curiosity. In his manner the master had something solid about him and blew his nose extremely loudly. No one knows how he did it, but his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, completely harmless distinction won him a great deal of respect from the inn servant, so that every time he heard that sound, he shook his hair, stood up more deferentially, and, bowing his head from above, asked whether anything was needed. After dinner the master drank a cup of coffee and sat down on the sofa, placing a pillow behind his back, the kind that in Russian inns, instead of elastic wool, is stuffed with something extremely like brick and cobblestone. There he began to yawn and ordered himself taken to his room, where, lying down, he slept for two hours. Having rested,he wrote on a scrap of paper, at the inn servant’s request, his rank, name, and surname, for notification where necessary, to the police. On the paper the servant, as he went down the stairs, read out syllable by syllable the following: “College Councilor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, on his own business.” While the servant was still deciphering the note syllable by syllable, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself went off to look at the city, with which he seemed satisfied, for he found that it was in no way inferior to other provincial cities: the yellow paint on the stone houses struck the eye sharply, and the gray on the wooden ones darkened modestly. The houses were one, two, and one and a half stories high, with the eternal mezzanine, very handsome, in the opinion of the provincial architects. In places these houses seemed lost among the street, broad as a field, and endless wooden fences; in other places they crowded together, and here there was more bustle and liveliness among the people. There were signs, almost washed out by rain, with pretzels and boots, here and there with blue trousers painted on them and the signature of some Arshavsky tailor; where a shop with caps and visors, and the inscription: “Foreigner Vasily Fedorov”; where a billiard table was painted with two players in tailcoats, such as are worn in our theaters by guests entering the stage in the final act. The players were shown with cues aimed, arms bent back somewhat awkwardly, and slanting legs, just having done entrechats in the air. Beneath all this was written: “And this is the establishment.” Here and there tables stood right on the street with walnuts, soap, and gingerbread that looked like soap; where an eatery with a painted fat fish and a fork stuck into it. Most often one could see darkened double-headed state eagles, now replaced by the laconic inscription: “Tavern.” The pavement was poor everywhere. He also looked into the city garden, which consisted of thin little trees, poorly established, with supports at the bottom in the shape of triangles, very nicely painted with green oil paint. However, although these saplings were no taller than reeds, the newspapers, describing the illumination, said that “our city has been embellished, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, broad-branched trees, giving coolness on a hot day,” and that at the same time “it was very moving to behold how the hearts of citizens trembled in an excess of gratitude and poured forth streams of tears in token of appreciation to the lord mayor.” After questioning the sentry box keeper in detail about where one could get closer, if necessary, to the cathedral, to the government offices, to the governor, he went to look at the river flowing through the middle of the city, tore off along the way a poster nailed to a post, intending to read it carefully when he got home, looked intently at a lady of not bad appearance walking along the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in a military livery, carrying a bundle in his hand, and, casting over everything once more as if to remember the place well, went home straight to his room, supported lightly on the stairs by the inn servant. After drinking tea, he sat down at the table, ordered a candle brought to him, took the poster from his pocket, held it to the candle, and began to read, squinting his right eye a little. There was not much remarkable in the handbill: it announced a play by Mr. Kotzebue, in which Rollo was played by Mr. Poplyovin, Kora by Miss Zyablova, and the other characters were even less noteworthy; however, he read them all, even got as far as the price of the pit, and learned that the handbill had been printed at the provincial government printing office, then turned it over to the other side to see if there was anything there too, but finding nothing, he rubbed his eyes, folded it neatly, and put it in his little chest, where he was in the habit of storing everything that came his way. The day, it seems, ended with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour cabbage soup, and a sound sleep all the way to the end of the pump handle, as they say in some parts of the vast Russian state.

The whole next day was devoted to visits. The newcomer set out to pay calls on all the city dignitaries. He paid his respects to the governor, who, it turned out, like Chichikov, was neither fat,not thin in build, had the Order of St. Anna on his neck, and it was even rumored that he had been awarded the star;[ Star — the Order of St. Stanislaus.

] however, he was a great good fellow and even embroidered on tulle himself at times. Then he went to the vice governor, then he was at the prosecutor's, at the chairman of the chamber's, at the police chief's, at the tax farmer's, at the head of the state factories… it is a pity that it is somewhat hard to remember all the powerful men of this world; but it is enough to say that the visitor showed extraordinary activity with regard to visits: he even called to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical board and the city architect. And then he sat for a long time still in the britzka, thinking whom else he might pay a visit to, but there were no more officials in the city. In conversations with these rulers he knew very skillfully how to flatter each one. To the governor he hinted offhand that when you enter his province, it is like entering paradise, the roads are velvet everywhere, and that those governments which appoint wise dignitaries deserve great praise. To the police chief he said something very flattering about the city watchmen; and in conversations with the vice governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were still only state councilors, he even mistakenly said twice, your excellency, which pleased them very much. The result was that the governor invited him to come that very day to his home gathering, while the other officials, for their part, some invited him to dinner, some to a little Boston, some to a cup of tea.

As for himself, the visitor, it seemed, avoided talking much about himself; if he did speak, it was in some generalities, with noticeable modesty, and his speech in such cases took on somewhat bookish turns: that he was a worthless worm of this world and not worthy of much concern, that he had experienced much in his lifetime, suffered in service for the truth, had many enemies who had even attempted his life, and that now, wishing to settle down, he was looking to choose at last a place of residence, and that, having arrived in this city, he considered it an absolute duty to pay his respects to its first dignitaries. That is all that was learned in the city about this new face, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor's gathering. Preparing for this gathering took more than two hours, and here the visitor showed such attention to his toilette as is not even seen everywhere. After a short after-dinner nap he ordered water to wash up, and rubbed soap for an extremely long time over both cheeks, supporting them from inside with his tongue; then, taking the towel from the inn servant's shoulder, he wiped his full face all around with it, starting behind the ears and snorting twice first right in the inn servant's face. Then he put on his shirtfront in front of the mirror, plucked out the two hairs sticking out of his nose, and immediately afterward found himself in a tailcoat of cranberry color with a sheen. Dressed in this way, he rolled off in his own carriage along the endlessly wide streets, lit by a meager illumination from lanterns flickering here and there. However, the governor's house was so brightly lit that it would have been fitting even for a ball; a carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes at the entrance, the distant cries of the postilions, in short, everything as it should be. Entering the hall, Chichikov had to shut his eyes for a moment, because the glare from the candles, lamps, and ladies' dresses was terrible. Everything was flooded with light. Black tailcoats flashed and swirled separately and in groups here and there, like flies swirling over white shining sugar in the hot July summer, when the old housekeeper chops and divides it into sparkling fragments before the open window; the children all gaze on, gathered around, watching curiously the movements of her stiff hands as they raise the hammer, while the airy squadrons of flies, lifted by the light breeze, boldly fly in like full masters and, taking advantage of the old woman's poor eyesight and the sun troubling her eyes, pepper the tempting pieces here in scattered fashion, there in thick swarms. Fed by the rich summer, which at every step was already setting out tempting dishes, they flew in not at all to eat, but only to show themselves, to strut back and forth over the sugar heap, to rub their hind or front legs against one another,or scratch them under their little wings, or, stretching out both front paws, rub them over their heads, turn around and fly off again, and fly back again with new annoying squadrons. Before Chichikov had time to look around, he was already seized by the arm by the governor, who introduced him at once to the governor's wife. The visiting guest did not disgrace himself here either: he said some compliment, quite suitable for a middle-aged man holding a rank neither too high nor too low. When the established pairs of dancers pressed everyone against the wall, he, with his hands clasped behind his back, looked at them very intently for two minutes. Many ladies were well dressed and in fashion, others dressed in whatever God had sent to the provincial town. The men here, as everywhere, were of two kinds: some were slender, always fluttering around the ladies; some of them were of such a sort that it was hard to distinguish them from St. Petersburg men, they had equally carefully and tastefully combed sideburns, or simply respectable, very smoothly shaved oval faces, they sat down next to the ladies just as casually, spoke French just as much, and made the ladies laugh just as they did in St. Petersburg. The other kind of men were fat, or just like Chichikov, that is, not too fat, but not thin either. These, on the contrary, glared sideways and backed away from the ladies, looking only around to see whether the governor's servant was setting up the green table for whist anywhere. Their faces were full and round, some even had warts, a few were freckled, they wore no hair on their heads in tufts, nor curls, nor in the manner of “devil take me,” as the French say, their hair was either cut short or slicked down, and their features were more rounded and solid. These were the respectable officials in the city. Alas! fat men know better how to manage their affairs in this world than slender ones. Slender ones serve mostly on special assignments or are only listed on the rolls and dart hither and thither. Their existence is somehow too light, airy, and completely unreliable. Fat ones never occupy indirect positions, only direct ones, and if they do sit somewhere, they sit securely and firmly, so that rather the place will crack and buckle under them than they will fall off. They do not like outward brilliance. Their tailcoats are not cut as smartly as those of the slender ones, but there is divine grace in their strongboxes. In three years the slender one has not a single soul left unpledged to the pawnshop. The fat one calmly, and lo and behold, there appears somewhere at the edge of town a house bought in his wife's name, then at the other end another house, then near the town a little village, and then even a whole estate with all its lands. At last the fat one, having served God and the state, having earned everyone's respect, leaves service, settles down, and becomes a landowner, a fine Russian gentleman, a hospitable host, and lives, and lives well. And after him the slender heirs, in the Russian way, squander all their father's property on courier rides. It cannot be concealed that reflections almost of this sort occupied Chichikov at the time when he was examining the company, and the result was that he finally joined the fat ones, where he found almost all familiar faces: the prosecutor with very black thick eyebrows and a left eye that was slightly winking as if to say, “Come, brother, into the next room, there I'll tell you something,” a serious and taciturn man, however; the postmaster, a short man, but a wit and philosopher; the chairman of the chamber, a very sensible and agreeable man, all of whom greeted him like an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov bowed somewhat to one side, though not without pleasantness. There he also made the acquaintance of the very courteous and polite landowner Manilov and of the rather awkward-looking Sobakevich, who at first step trod on his foot, saying, “I beg your pardon.” There they also slipped him a card for whist, which he accepted with the same polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not get up again until dinner. All conversation ceased completely, as always happens when one finally turns to a useful task. Although the postmaster was very talkative, even he, having taken the cards in hand,at once assumed a thoughtful expression on his face,

The next day Chichikov went to dinner and the evening gathering at the police chief’s, where from three o’clock in the afternoon they settled down to whist and played until two in the morning. There, among other things, he made the acquaintance of the landowner Nozdryov, a man about thirty, a lively fellow, who after three or four words started addressing him as “you.” Nozdryov was also on “you” terms with the police chief and the prosecutor and spoke to them in a friendly way, but when they sat down to play for high stakes, the police chief and the prosecutor examined his tricks extremely closely and watched almost every card with which he played. The next day Chichikov spent the evening with the chairman of the chamber, who received his guests in a dressing gown, somewhat greasy, and among them were two ladies of some sort. Then he was at the vice-governor’s evening party, at a large dinner with the tax farmer, at a small dinner with the prosecutor, which, however, cost a great deal; at a snack after mass given by the city head, which also cost as much as a dinner. In short, he had no hour to stay at home, and he came to the inn only to sleep. The visitor knew how somehow to find his way in everything and showed himself to be an experienced man of the world. Whatever the conversation was about, he always knew how to keep it going: if the talk turned to horse breeding, he talked about horse breeding too; if they talked about good dogs, he offered very sensible remarks there as well; if they discussed the investigation conducted by the treasury chamber, he showed that he was not unfamiliar with officials’ tricks; if there was talk of billiards, he did not miss there either; if they talked about virtue, he reasoned very well about virtue too, even with tears in his eyes; about distilling strong liquor, he knew his stuff; about customs overseers and officials, he judged them as if he himself were both an official and an overseer. But what was remarkable was that he knew how to cloak all this in a certain dignity, he knew how to carry himself well. He spoke neither loudly nor softly, but exactly as he should. In short, whichever way you turned it, he was a very respectable man. All the officials were pleased with the arrival of the new face. The governor said of him that he was a well-intentioned man; the prosecutor, that he was a capable man; the gendarme colonel, that he was a learned man; the chairman of the chamber, that he was an informed and respectable man; the police chief, that he was a respectable and agreeable man; the police chief’s wife, that he was the most agreeable and most courteous man. Even Sobakevich himself, who rarely spoke well of anyone, having come rather late from the city and already completely undressed and lying down on the bed next to his thin wife, said to her: “I, my dear, was at the governor’s evening party, and I dined with the police chief, and I made the acquaintance of Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov: a most pleasant man!” To which his wife replied: “Hm!” and nudged him with her foot.

Such an opinion, very flattering for the guest, was formed about him in the town, and it lasted until one strange trait of the guest and his undertaking, or, as they say in the provinces, his passage, of which the reader will soon learn, threw almost the whole town into complete perplexity.

Chapter Two

For more than a week the gentleman who had arrived had been living in the town, driving about to evening gatherings and dinners and thus spending, as they say, time very pleasantly. At last he decided to extend his visits beyond the town and pay a call on the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps he was prompted to do so by another, more important reason, a matter more serious, closer to the heart... But the reader will learn all about this gradually and in due time, if only he or she has the patience to read the story offered here, a very long one, which is to spread out wider and more spaciously as it nears the end that crowns the whole work. The coachman Selifan was ordered to harness the horses early in the morning to the familiar britzka; Petrushka was ordered to stay at home and look after the room and the suitcase. It will not be superfluous for the reader to become acquainted with these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not such prominent figures, and even so,what are called secondary or even tertiary ones, although the main moves and springs of the poem are not based on them and only here and there touch them and barely catch them, — but the author loves to be extremely thorough in everything, and in this respect, despite being a Russian himself, wants to be as meticulous as a German. This will not take much time or space, however, because there is not much to add to what the reader already knows, namely that Petrushka walked around in a somewhat loose brown coat from his master's shoulder and had, as was customary for people of his rank, a large nose and lips. He was more taciturn than talkative in character. He even had a noble inclination toward enlightenment, that is, reading books whose contents did not trouble him: it was completely all the same to him whether it was the adventures of a love-struck hero, a simple alphabet book, or a prayer book, — he read everything with equal attention; if they had given him chemistry, he would not have refused that either. He liked not what he read, but the reading itself more, or, better said, the very process of reading, that from letters there always comes some word, which at times means God knows what. This reading was done mostly while lying down in the hall, on the bed and on the mattress, which from such circumstances had become battered and thin like a pancake. Besides his passion for reading, he had two more habits, which made up two other characteristic traits of his: to sleep without undressing, just as he was, in the same coat, and to always carry with him some special air of his own, his own smell, giving off a somewhat inhabited comfort, so that it was enough for him to set up his bed somewhere, even in a room that had until then been unoccupied, and drag there the overcoat and belongings, and it already seemed as if people had lived in that room for ten years. Chichikov, being a very sensitive man and even in some cases finicky, would draw in the air with a fresh nose in the morning and only wrinkle his face and shake his head, saying, "You, brother, who knows what, do you sweat or what. You ought to go to the bathhouse at least." To which Petrushka answered nothing and tried at once to busy himself with some task. Or he would go up with a whip to the master's frock coat hanging there, or simply put something away. What he was thinking at that time, when he was silent, — perhaps he was saying to himself, "And you, too, are something else, haven't you gotten tired of repeating the same thing forty times," — God knows, it is hard to know what a house serf thinks when his master is instructing him. So that, for the first time, this is what can be said about Petrushka. The coachman Selifan was a completely different man... But the author is greatly ashamed to occupy readers for so long with people of the lower class, knowing from experience how unwillingly they make the acquaintance of lower estates. Such is the Russian: a strong passion for becoming close with someone who is even one rank above him, and a tip-of-the-hat acquaintance with a count or a prince is better for him than any close friendship. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a collegiate councillor. Court councillors may perhaps get acquainted with him, but those who have already climbed to general rank, well, God knows, may perhaps even cast one of those contemptuous looks that a man throws proudly on everything crawling at his feet, or, what is worse, may perhaps pass by with a fatal lack of attention for the author. But however sad either of these may be, it is still necessary to return to the hero. So, having given the necessary orders the evening before, rising very early in the morning, washing, wiping himself from head to toe with a wet sponge, which was done only on Sundays, — and that day happened to be Sunday, — having shaved in such a way that his cheeks became true satin in terms of smoothness and sheen, putting on a frock coat of cranberry color with a spark and then an overcoat on large bearskins, he descended the stairs, supported under the arm now on one side, now on the other by the inn servant, and got into the carriage. With a crash the carriage drove out from under the inn gate into the street. A priest walking by took off his hat, several boys in soiled shirts stretched out their hands, saying:“‘Sir, spare something for the poor orphan!’ The coachman, noticing that one of them was very fond of standing on the footboard, lashed him with his whip, and the britzka began to bounce over the stones. The striped barrier, seen in the distance, was greeted not without joy, since it gave notice that the pavement, like any other torment, would soon be over; and after banging his head a few more times rather hard against the body of the carriage, Chichikov at last sped along soft ground. Hardly had the city fallen behind than, in our usual fashion, nonsense and wildness began to appear on both sides of the road: hummocks, spruce woods, low thin bushes of young pines, scorched trunks of old ones, wild heather, and all that sort of rubbish. There were villages stretched out like a string, their construction resembling old stacked firewood, covered with gray roofs and carved wooden decorations beneath them in the form of hanging embroidered towels. A few peasants, as usual, were yawning, sitting on benches before the gates in their sheepskin coats. Women with heavy faces and bandaged breasts looked out from the upper windows; from the lower ones a calf peered or a pig stuck out its blind snout. In short, familiar sights. After passing the fifteenth verst, he remembered that, according to Manilov, his village was supposed to be here, but the sixteenth verst also flew by, and the village still could not be seen, and if not for two peasants who happened to meet them, they would hardly have managed to get it right.

When asked whether the village of Zamanilovka was far off, the peasants took off their hats, and one of them, the smarter one, with a wedge-shaped beard, replied:

— Manilovka, perhaps, and not Zamanilovka?

— Well yes, Manilovka.

— Manilovka! And when you go one more verst, then there, that is, straight to the right.

— To the right?—the coachman called back.

— To the right, — said the peasant. — That will be the road to Manilovka; but there is no Zamanilovka at all. It is called that, that is, its name is Manilovka, and there is no Zamanilovka here at all. There, straight on the hill, you will see a house, stone, two stories, the master’s house, in which, that is, the master himself lives. That is Manilovka for you, and there is no Zamanilovka here and never was.

They drove on to look for Manilovka. After going two versts, they came to a turn onto a country road, but even after two, three, and four versts, it seemed, had been covered, the two-story stone house was still nowhere to be seen. Then Chichikov remembered that if a friend invites you to his village fifteen versts away, that means it is a good thirty to get there. The village of Manilovka could attract few by its location. The master’s house stood alone on a hill, that is, on a rise exposed to every wind that might occur to blow. The slope of the hill on which it stood was covered with trimmed turf. Scattered across it in the English manner were two or three flowerbeds with lilac bushes and yellow acacias; five or six birches, in small clumps, here and there raised their thin-leaved sparse tops. Beneath two of them was visible a gazebo with a flat green dome, wooden blue columns, and an inscription: “Temple of Solitary Reflection”; a little lower lay a pond covered with greenery, which, however, is nothing unusual in the English gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this rise, and partly on the slope itself, gray log huts darkened here and there, which our hero, for reasons unknown, immediately began counting and counted more than two hundred; nowhere among them was there a growing tree or any greenery at all; everywhere only logs could be seen. The view was enlivened by two women who, gathering up their dresses in a picturesque way and tucking them in from all sides, waded knee-deep in the pond, dragging an old torn seine by two wooden poles, where two tangled crayfish could be seen and a flashing roach had been caught; the women seemed to be quarrelling with each other and were exchanging insults over something. Off to the side, at some distance, a pine forest darkened in a dull bluish color. Even the weather had been suitably obliging: the day was neither clear nor gloomy, but of some pale gray color such as is seen only on the old uniforms of garrison soldiers, that, however, peaceful army, though somewhat given to drunkenness on Sundays. This piebald horse was sly to the core and only seemed, for appearances’ sake, to be pulling, while the bay lead horse and the side horse of a dun color, called the Assessor because it had been bought from some assessor, were working with all their heart, so much so that even in their eyes the pleasure they took in it was visible. “Be sly, be sly! I’ll outsmart you yet,” said Selifan, rising up and cracking the whip at the sluggard. “You just do your job, you German trouser-wearer! The bay horse is a respectable horse, he does his duty, and I’ll gladly give him an extra measure, because he’s a respectable horse, and the Assessor is a good horse too... Come, come! Why are you flicking your ears? You fool, listen when you’re spoken to! I’m not going to teach you, you boor, to do wrong. Look where he’s creeping to!” Here he cracked the whip at him again, adding, “Oh, you savage! Damn you, Napoleon!” Then he shouted to them all, “Hey there, my dears!” and lashed all three again, no longer as punishment, but to show that he was pleased with them. Having given such pleasure, he turned again to the piebald horse: “You think you can hide your behavior. No, live honestly if you want people to treat you with respect. At the landowner’s place we were at, there are good people. I’m glad to talk if someone is a good person; with a good person we’re always our own friends, close pals; whether to have tea or a bite to eat, gladly, if he’s a good person. Everyone will show respect to a good person. Our master is respected by everyone, because, you know, he served the state, he’s a collegiate councillor...”

Reasoning this way, Selifan finally strayed into the most distant abstractions. If Chichikov had listened, he would have learned many details referring to himself personally; but his thoughts were so occupied with his own subject that only a heavy clap of thunder made him come to and look around. The whole sky was completely covered with clouds, and the dusty post road was sprinkled with raindrops. At last the clap of thunder sounded a second time, louder and closer, and the rain suddenly poured down in torrents. At first, falling at an angle, it lashed one side of the carriage body, then the other, then, changing both its manner of attack and becoming completely straight, it drummed directly on top of the carriage body; at last the splashes began reaching his face. This made him draw the leather curtains closed, with two round little windows set in them for looking at the road scenery, and order Selifan to drive faster. Selifan, interrupted also in mid-speech, realized that there was indeed no time to lose, pulled out from under the driver’s box some rag of gray cloth, put it on with its sleeves, grabbed the reins, and shouted at his troika, which was barely stepping along, for it felt a pleasant slackening from the instructive speeches. But Selifan could by no means remember whether they had passed two or three turns. Figuring it out and recalling the road somewhat, he guessed that there had been many turns that he had all missed. Since a Russian man, in decisive moments, will find something to do without going into lengthy reasoning, after turning right onto the first crossroad he shouted, “Hey there, respectable friends!” and set off at a gallop, scarcely thinking about where the road he had taken would lead.

The rain, however, seemed to have set in for a long while. The dust lying on the road quickly turned to mud, and it became harder and harder for the horses to pull the britzka. Chichikov was already beginning to worry greatly at not seeing Sobakevich’s village for so long. By his reckoning, they should have arrived long ago. He looked out to the sides, but the darkness was so thick you couldn’t see your hand in front of your face.

“Selifan!” he said at last, leaning out of the britzka.

“What is it, master?” replied Selifan.

“Take a look, can you see the village?”

“No, master, can’t see it anywhere!” After that, waving the whip, Selifan started a song, not really a song, but something long like that, with no end to it. It took in everything: all the encouraging and urging cries,with which they feed horses all over Russia from one end to the other, adjectives of every kind without further distinction, whichever word first came to their tongue. In this way it came to the point that he finally began calling them secretaries.

Meanwhile Chichikov began to notice that the carriage was swaying from side to side and giving him very hard jolts. This made him realize that they had left the road and were probably trudging across a harrowed field. Selifan seemed to have figured it out himself, but said nothing.

"What, you scoundrel, what road are you taking?" said Chichikov.

"Well, sir, what can I do, with weather like this, you can't see a whip, it's so dark!" Having said this, he tilted the carriage so badly that Chichikov had to hold on with both hands. Only then did he notice that Selifan had had a drink.

"Keep it steady, keep it steady, you'll tip us over!" he shouted.

"No, sir, how could I tip us over," said Selifan. "It's not good to tip over, I know that myself, I would never tip us over." Then he began to turn the carriage slightly, turning and turning, and at last he tipped it completely onto its side. Chichikov fell into the mud, arms and legs flailing. Selifan stopped the horses, though they would have stopped on their own anyway, because they were utterly exhausted. Such an unexpected event completely amazed him. Climbing down from the box, he stood in front of the carriage, braced his hands on his hips, while the master floundered in the mud, trying to get out, and after some thought said: "Would you believe it, it really did tip over!"

"You are drunk as a cobbler!" said Chichikov.

"No, sir, how could I be drunk! I know it is not a good thing to be drunk. I spoke with a friend, because you can talk with a good person, there is nothing wrong in that. And we had a bite together. A bite is not an offense, you can have a bite with a good person."

"And what did I tell you last time when you got drunk? Huh? Forgotten?" said Chichikov.

"No, your nobility, how could I forget? I already know my business. I know that it is not good to be drunk. I spoke with a good person, because..."

"I'll give you such a beating that you'll learn how to talk with a good person!"

"As your grace wishes," Selifan replied, agreeing to everything. "If beating, then beating, I am not at all against it. Why not beat me if I deserve it, it is the master's will. He should be beaten, because the peasant gets unruly, order must be kept. If it's for a reason, then beat him; why not beat him?"

At such reasoning the master was completely at a loss for what to answer. But at that moment, it seemed, fate itself had decided to take pity on him. In the distance they heard a dog barking. Delighted, Chichikov ordered the horses to be driven on. A Russian coachman has a good instinct in place of eyes, which is why it sometimes happens that, with his eyes shut, he drives at full speed and always ends up somewhere. Selifan, unable to see a thing, headed the horses straight toward the village, stopping only when the carriage struck the fence with the shafts and there was nowhere left to go. Through the thick veil of pouring rain Chichikov could only make out something like a roof. He sent Selifan to look for the gate, which would undoubtedly have taken a long time if Russia did not have bold dogs instead of doormen, dogs that announced him so loudly that he covered his ears with his fingers. A light flashed in one window and reached, in a misty stream, to the fence, showing our travelers the gate. Selifan began knocking, and soon a figure covered in an armyak stuck out after opening the gate, and master and servant heard a hoarse woman's voice:

"Who's knocking? Why all the racket?"

"Travelers, mother, let us stay the night," said Chichikov.

"Would you believe it, what a speedy one," said the old woman, "arriving at this hour! This isn't an inn for you, a landowner lives here."

"What can we do, mother? We lost our way from the road. We can't spend the night in the steppe at this hour."

"Yes, it's dark weather, bad weather," added Selifan.

"Shut up, fool," said Chichikov.

"And who are you?" said the old woman.

"A nobleman, mother."

The word nobleman made the old woman seem to think for a moment.

"Wait, I'll tell the mistress,"— she said, and in two minutes she came back with a lantern in her hand.

The gate was unlocked. A light flickered in another window too. The carriage, after driving into the yard, stopped in front of a small house, which was hard to make out in the darkness. Only one half of it was lit by the light coming from the windows; one could also see a puddle in front of the house, directly struck by that same light. Rain beat loudly on the wooden roof and flowed in murmuring streams into the barrel set out to catch it. Meanwhile the dogs were barking with every possible voice: one, throwing its head back, kept it up so long and so earnestly, as if it were getting God knows what kind of salary for it; another barked off quickly, like a sexton; between them rang an incessant treble, like a post bell, probably from a young puppy, and all this was finally topped by a bass, maybe an old dog gifted with a mighty canine nature, because it growled like a singing double bass does when a concert is in full swing: the tenors rise on tiptoe from an intense desire to hit a high note, and everything that exists strains upward, throwing back its head, while it alone, tucking its unshaven chin into its neckcloth, crouching and lowering itself almost to the ground, lets out from there its note, which makes the panes shiver and rattle. From such a choir of dogs alone, one could have guessed that the little village was a respectable one; but our soaked and chilled hero was thinking of nothing but a bed. No sooner had the carriage come to a complete stop than he jumped down onto the porch, staggered, and nearly fell. Another woman came out onto the porch, younger than the first, but very much like her. She showed him into a room. Chichikov cast two quick glances: the room was hung with old striped wallpaper; paintings with some birds; between the windows, antique little mirrors with dark frames in the shape of curled leaves; behind every mirror there was either a letter, or an old deck of cards, or a stocking; a wall clock with painted flowers on the dial... it was impossible to notice anything more. He felt that his eyes were sticking together, as if someone had smeared them with honey. A minute later the mistress came in, a woman of advanced years, in some sleeping cap put on in haste, with flannel around her neck, one of those mothers, small landowners, who complain about poor harvests and losses and hold their heads slightly to one side, while at the same time slowly gathering a bit of money into coarse homespun bags arranged in the drawers of a chest of drawers. In one bag they put all the silver roubles, in another the half-rubles, in a third the quarter-rubles, although it seems at first glance as if the chest of drawers contains nothing but linen, and night jackets, and skeins of thread, and a ripped-open cloak that is later to be turned into a dress if the old one somehow burns during the baking of holiday cakes with all sorts of little fried pastries, or simply wears out by itself. But the dress will not burn and will not wear out by itself: the old lady is thrifty, and the cloak is destined to lie for a long time in ripped-open form, and then go by will to the niece of a great-niece, together with all the other junk.

Chichikov apologized for disturbing her with his unexpected arrival.

— Nothing, nothing, said the mistress. What a time God brought you here! Such turmoil and blizzard... You ought to have something to eat after the journey, but it's nighttime, and nothing can be prepared.

The mistress's words were interrupted by a strange hissing, so that the guest was briefly frightened; the noise was like the whole room had filled with snakes; but looking up, he calmed down, for he realized that the wall clock had taken it into its head to strike. The hissing was immediately followed by a rasping sound, and finally, straining with all their might, they struck two o'clock with a sound as if someone were beating on a broken pot with a stick, after which the pendulum went back to clicking peacefully right and left.

Chichikov thanked the mistress, saying that he needed nothing, that she need not worry about anything, that besides a bed he required nothing, and only wondered where exactly he had come and how far it was from here to the landowner Sobakevich, to which the old woman said,and had never heard such a name, and that there was no such landowner at all.

"At least do you know Manilov?" Chichikov said.

"Who is Manilov?"

"A landowner, ma'am."

"No, never heard of him, there is no such landowner."

"What ones are there, then?"

"Bobrov, Svinin, Kanapatyev, Kharpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov."

"Rich people or not?"

"No, father, not many rich ones at all. Some have twenty souls, some thirty, but those with a hundred, there are none like that."

Chichikov noticed that he had driven into a pretty remote place.

"How far, at least, is it to the town?"

"About sixty versts. What a pity I have nothing for you to eat! Won't you have some tea, my dear sir?"

"Thank you, ma'am. I need nothing but a bed."

"True, after such a journey you very much need rest. Here, make yourself comfortable, sir, on this sofa. Hey, Fetinya, bring a mattress, pillows, and a sheet. What weather God has sent us: such thunder, I had a candle burning before the icon all night. Ah, father, your back and side are all dirty like a pig's! Where did you get so smeared?"

"Thank God it's only smeared, one should be grateful it didn't break my sides off altogether."

"Holy saints, what a business! Do you need something to rub your back with?"

"Thank you, thank you. Don't worry, just tell your maid to dry and clean my clothes."

"Do you hear that, Fetinya!" said the hostess, turning to the woman who was coming out onto the porch with a candle, having already managed to drag in the mattress and, after shaking it up on both sides with her hands, spread a whole flood of feathers all over the room. "Take their coat together with the underclothes and first dry them by the fire, as they used to do for the late master, and then rub and beat them out good."

"Yes, ma'am!" said Fetinya, spreading the sheet over the mattress and placing the pillows.

"Well, there's your bed ready," said the hostess. "Goodbye, father, I wish you a good night. Do you need anything else? Maybe you're used to someone scratching your heels at night, father? My late husband could never fall asleep without that."

But the guest declined the heel-scratching too. The hostess went out, and he immediately hastened to undress, handing Fetinya all the tack he had taken off, both outer and under, and Fetinya, also wishing him a good night on her side, dragged away these wet armors. Left alone, he looked at his bed with no little pleasure, for it was almost up to the ceiling. Fetinya, as it seemed, was a master at beating up mattresses. When, after putting a chair in place, he climbed onto the bed, it sank under him almost to the floor, and the feathers pushed out beyond its bounds flew into all the corners of the room. After blowing out the candle, he covered himself with a chintz quilt and, curling up under it like a pretzel, fell asleep at once. He woke the next day quite late in the morning. The sun was shining straight into his eyes through the window, and the flies, which had slept peacefully yesterday on the walls and ceiling, all turned toward him: one landed on his lip, another on his ear, a third tried to settle on his eye itself, and the one that had the bad luck to sit too close to his nostril he, half asleep, drew right into his nose, which made him sneeze hard, -

the circumstance that had caused his awakening. Looking around the room, he now noticed that the pictures were not all birds: among them hung a portrait of Kutuzov and, painted in oils, some old man with red cuffs on his coat, as they used to sew under Pavel Petrovich. The clock again emitted a hiss and struck ten; a female face peeked through the door and vanished in the same instant, because Chichikov, wanting to sleep better, had stripped himself completely. The face that looked in seemed somewhat familiar to him. He began to think it over: who could it be, and at last remembered that it was the mistress of the house. He put on his shirt; his clothes, already dried and cleaned, lay beside him. After dressing, he went to the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that the Indian rooster, which had come up to the window at that moment, and the window was very close to the ground, suddenly babbled something to him in its strange language, probably "wish you health," to which Chichikov called it a fool. Going up to the window, he began to examine the view before him: the window looked almost into a henhouse; at any rate, the narrow yard in front of it was entirely filled with birds and all sorts of domestic animals. There was no counting the turkeys and hens; among them the rooster walked about with measured steps, shaking his comb and turning his head to one side, as if listening to something; a pig with its family was there too; there, while rooting through a heap of trash, it swallowed a chick by the way and, without noticing it, kept on stuffing itself with watermelon rinds as usual. This small yard, or henhouse, was cut off by a plank fence, beyond which stretched spacious vegetable gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, borage, and other kitchen vegetables. Scattered here and there through the garden were apple trees and other fruit trees, covered with nets to protect them from magpies and sparrows, the latter of which were drifting in whole slanting clouds from one place to another. For the same reason, several scarecrows had been set up on long poles, with outstretched arms; one of them wore the mistress's own bonnet. Beyond the gardens came the peasants' huts, which, though built irregularly and not arranged in proper streets, nonetheless, as Chichikov observed, showed the well-being of their inhabitants, because they were properly maintained: the weathered shingles on the roofs had everywhere been replaced with new ones; the gates had nowhere sagged, and in the peasants' covered sheds facing him he noticed, here a spare almost new cart, there two. "Why, her little village is no small one," he said and decided to chat and get better acquainted with the mistress right away. He peeked through the crack of the door from which she had just poked out her head, and, seeing her sitting at the tea table, went in to her with a cheerful and friendly look.

"Good day, sir. How did you sleep?" said the mistress, rising from her seat. She was dressed better than yesterday, in a dark dress and no longer in a sleeping cap, though something was still tied around her neck.

"Good, good," Chichikov said, sitting down in an armchair. "And how are you, ma'am?"

"Badly, my dear sir."

"How so?"

"Insomnia. My lower back hurts all the time, and the leg above the ankle is aching as well."

"It will pass, it will pass, ma'am. Don't pay it any mind."

"God grant it does pass. I've been rubbing it with pork fat and wetting it with turpentine too. And what will you have with your tea? There's fruit brandy in the flask."

"Fine, ma'am, we'll have some fruit brandy too."

Reader, I think you have already noticed that Chichikov, despite his friendly manner, was speaking with much more freedom than with Manilov and was not being ceremonious at all. It must be said that in Russia, if we have not yet caught up with foreigners in some things, we have long since surpassed them in the art of social manners. One cannot count all the shades and subtleties of our behavior. A Frenchman or a German would never in his life figure out and understand all its peculiarities and distinctions; he would speak almost in the same voice and the same language both to a millionaire and to a petty tobacconist, though of course inwardly he would act the scoundrel in due measure before the first. That is not how it is with us. We have such sages who, with a landowner who has two hundred souls,They will speak quite differently to the one who has three hundred than to the one who has five hundred, and to the one who has five hundred differently again than to the one who has eight hundred. In short, you could go all the way up to a million and you would still find shades of difference. Suppose, for example, there is an office, not here but in some faraway land, and in that office, suppose, there is an office manager. Please look at him when he is sitting among his subordinates, you simply cannot say a word from fear alone! Pride and nobility, and what does his face not express? just take a brush and paint it: Prometheus, a resolute Prometheus! He scans like an eagle, moves smoothly, measuredly. The very same eagle, as soon as he leaves the room and approaches the office of his superior, hurries like a partridge with papers under his arm, almost out of breath. In society and at parties, if everyone there is of lower rank, Prometheus will remain Prometheus, but once someone a little higher appears, such a transformation comes over Prometheus that even Ovid could not invent it: a fly, smaller than a fly, vanished into a grain of sand! "Why, that is not Ivan Petrovich," you say, looking at him. "Ivan Petrovich is taller, while this one is short and thin; the other speaks loudly, in a bass voice, and never laughs, but this fellow, heaven knows what he is: he chirps like a bird and keeps laughing." You come closer, look, and it is indeed Ivan Petrovich! "Heh-heh," you think to yourself... But let us return to the characters. Chichikov, as we have already seen, had resolved not to be ceremonious at all and therefore, taking a cup of tea in his hand and pouring some fruit brandy into it, began speaking as follows:

— You have, my dear, a fine little village. How many souls are there in it?

— Souls? There are just under eighty in it, my dear sir, said the mistress, but the trouble is, times are bad, and last year we had such a poor harvest, God preserve us.

— Still, the peasants look sturdy, and the huts are solid. May I ask your surname? I am so absent-minded... I arrived at night...

— Korobochka, collegiate secretary.

— Many thanks. And your first name and patronymic?

— Nastasya Petrovna.

— Nastasya Petrovna? That is a fine name, Nastasya Petrovna. I have an aunt, my mother's sister, Nastasya Petrovna.

— And what is your name? asked the landowner. I suppose you are a judge?

— No, my dear, replied Chichikov with a smile, not a judge, I just travel about on my own business.

— Ah, so you are a buyer! What a pity, truly, that I sold my honey so cheaply to the merchants, and you, my dear sir, would surely have bought it from me.

— I would not have bought the honey either.

— What else then? Flax? But I have very little flax now, only half a pood.

— No, my dear, a different sort of goods. Tell me, have any of your peasants died?

— Oh, father, eighteen people, said the old woman with a sigh. And all such fine folk died, all workers. After that, to be sure, more were born, but what good are they? They are all such little runts. Then the judge came around and says, the tax has to be paid per soul. The people are dead, but you pay as if they were alive. Last week my blacksmith burned to death, such a skillful blacksmith, and he knew locksmithing too.

— Was there a fire at your place, my dear?

— God spared us from such a disaster, a fire would have been even worse; he burned himself, my dear sir. Something caught fire inside him, he drank too much, only a blue flame came out of him, he burned away completely, burned away and turned black as coal, and he was such a master blacksmith! and now I have no way to go out: there is no one to shoe the horses.

— It is all the will of God, my dear! said Chichikov with a sigh, one cannot argue against the wisdom of God... Would you let them go to me, Nastasya Petrovna?

— Whom, my dear sir?

— Why, all these ones who died.

— But how am I to let them go?

— Quite simply. Or, if you prefer, sell them. I will give you money for them.

— But how? I truly do not understand. Surely you do not mean to dig them up from the ground?

Chichikov saw that the old woman had gone too far and that he needed to explain the matter to her. In a few words he explained that the transfer or purchase would be recorded only on paper and that the souls would be entered as though they were alive.

— But what do you want them for? said the old woman, staring at him with bulging eyes.

— That is my affair.

— But they are dead.— But who says they’re alive? That’s exactly why they’re a loss to you, because they’re dead: you pay for them, and now I’ll spare you the trouble and the payment. Do you understand? Not only will I spare you, I’ll also give you fifteen rubles on top of that. Well, is it clear now?

— Honestly, I don’t know, — said the landlady slowly. — I’ve never sold dead people before.

— Of course! It would be more like a miracle if you had sold them to anyone. Or do you think there’s really some use in them?

— No, I don’t think that. What use could there be? There’s no use at all. What bothers me is only that they’re already dead.

“Well, this woman seems pretty thickheaded!” Chichikov thought to himself.

— Listen, mother. Just think it through carefully: you’re going bankrupt, paying tax on him as if he were alive…

— Oh, my dear man, don’t even mention it! — the landowner chimed in. — Just last week I paid out more than one hundred and fifty. And I even greased the assessor.

— Well, you see, mother. And now just consider that you won’t need to grease the assessor anymore, because now I’m paying for them; I, not you; I’m taking on all the obligations. I’ll even complete the deed at my own expense, do you understand that?

The old woman fell into thought. She could see that the deal did indeed seem profitable, only it was far too new and unprecedented; and so she began to fear greatly that this buyer might somehow cheat her; he had come, after all, heaven knows from where, and at night besides.

— So then, mother, do we have a deal or what? — said Chichikov.

— Honestly, my dear man, it’s never happened before that I should sell the deceased. I’ve parted with the living, yes, and just the year before last I sold two girls to the archpriest for one hundred rubles each, and he was very grateful, they turned out to be splendid workers: they weave tablecloths themselves.

— Well, I’m not talking about the living, let them be. I’m asking about the dead.

— Honestly, I’m afraid at the start that I might somehow end up taking a loss. Perhaps you, my dear man, are deceiving me, and they… well… they’re worth more somehow.

— Listen, mother… oh, you’re impossible! What could they be worth? Just look at it: they’re dust. Do you understand? Just dust. Take any worthless, last-rate thing, even a simple rag, and even a rag has a price: at the very least it’ll be bought for a paper mill, but this is good for nothing. Well, tell me yourself, what is it good for?

— That’s true enough, truly. It’s absolutely good for nothing; but what stops me is only that they’re already dead.

“Good lord, what a blockhead!” Chichikov said to himself, already beginning to lose patience. “Try dealing with her! She’s got me sweating, the damned old woman!” Here he took a handkerchief from his pocket and began wiping the sweat that had indeed broken out on his forehead. Still, Chichikov was angry for nothing: some people, even respectable ones, even government people, turn out to be complete Korobochkas in practice. Once they’ve fixed something in their head, nothing can overpower it; pile on as many arguments as you like, clear as day, they all bounce off like a rubber ball off a wall. After wiping his sweat, Chichikov decided to try whether he could lead her to the point by some other route.

— You, mother, — he said, — either don’t want to understand me, or you’re just saying this on purpose, just to say something… I’m offering you money: fifteen rubles in banknotes. Do you understand? That’s money. You won’t find it lying in the street. Well, admit it, how much did you sell the honey for?

— At twelve rubles a pud.

— You’ve taken a little sin upon your soul, mother. You didn’t sell it for twelve.

— By God, I sold it.

— Well, you see? So it’s honey. You may have gathered it for about a year, with care, with effort, with trouble; you traveled around, smoked out the bees,[ Smoked out the bees  — to stupefy them with smoke so the combs can be removed.

] fed them in the cellar all winter; but dead souls are a matter not of this world. From your side, you put in no effort at all. It was God’s will that they should leave this world, causing damage to your household. There you got twelve rubles for your labor and your care, and here you’re getting something for nothing, for free, and not twelve, but fifteen, and not in silver, but all in blue banknotes.— After such strong arguments, Chichikov was almost no longer in doubt that the old woman would finally give in.

— Really, — answered the landowner, — my poor inexperienced widow’s business! better I wait a little, maybe merchants will come, and I’ll take a look at the prices.

— Shame, shame, mother! Just shame! What are you saying, think for yourself! Who would buy them? What use could anyone make of them?

— God protect us! What frightful things you say! — said the old woman, crossing herself.

The old woman fell into thought again.

The devil frightened the landowner terribly.

The fair-haired one was one of those people,in whose character there is at first glance some stubbornness. Before you can even open your mouth, they are already ready to argue, and it seems they will never agree to something that is plainly opposite to their way of thinking, that they will never call a fool smart, and that above all they will not agree to dance to someone else's tune. But it always ends with their character showing softness, with them agreeing precisely to what they had rejected, calling the fool smart, and then dancing as well as can be to someone else's tune, in short, they start with smoothness and end with slime.

— Nonsense! said Nozdryov in reply to some remark of the blond man, put his cap on his head, and the blond man went after them.

— The vodka, sir, has not been paid for... said the old woman.

— Ah, good, good, mother. Listen, son-in-law! pay, please. I don't have a kopeck in my pocket.

— How much do you need? said the son-in-law.

— Why, father, just a two-copeck piece, said the old woman.

— You're lying, you're lying. Give her a poltina,[A two-copeck piece in silver is eighty kopecks in banknotes. A poltina is fifty kopecks in banknotes.

] that's quite enough for her.

— Too little, sir, said the old woman, but she took the money with gratitude and hurried off to open the door for them. She was not losing out, because she had charged four times what the vodka cost.

The visitors sat down. Chichikov's britzka was driving alongside the britzka in which Nozdryov and his son-in-law were seated, and so the three of them could talk freely with one another during the journey. Behind them followed, constantly falling behind, Nozdryov's little carriage on lean townspeople's horses. In it sat Porfiry with the puppy.

Since the conversation the travelers were carrying on was not very interesting for the reader, it would be better if we said something about Nozdryov himself, who may perhaps turn out to play not quite the last role in our poem.

Nozdryov's face is probably already somewhat familiar to the reader. Everyone has met plenty of people like that. They are called lively fellows, are already known as good companions in childhood and at school, and yet are often soundly beaten. There is always something open, direct, and dashing about their faces. They make friends quickly, and before you know it they are addressing you informally. They seem to make friendships for life, but it almost always happens that the new friend quarrels with them that very evening at a friendly feast. They are always talkers, carousers, daredevils, noticeable people. At thirty-five Nozdryov was exactly what he had been at eighteen and twenty: a lover of a good time. His marriage changed him not at all, especially since his wife soon departed this world, leaving behind two little children who were absolutely no use to him. A pretty nurse, however, looked after the children. He could not stay at home for more than a day. His keen nose could smell from dozens of versts away where there was a fair with all sorts of gatherings and balls. He was there in an instant, arguing and stirring up trouble at the green table, for, like all such people, he had a little passion for cards. As we have already seen in the first chapter, he played cards not altogether innocently or cleanly, knowing many different cheats and other tricks, and so the game very often ended in another kind of game, either he was beaten with boots, or they would slip a cheat past his thick and very fine side-whiskers, so that he would sometimes return home with only one side-whisker left, and even that a rather thin one. But his healthy, full cheeks were so well made and contained so much vital force that the side-whiskers soon grew back, even better than before. And what is strangest of all, something that can happen only in Russia, after some time he would meet again those very friends who had thrashed him, and meet them as though nothing had happened, and he, as they say, was fine, and they were fine.

Nozdryov was, in a certain respect, a historical man. There was no gathering he attended without some story. Some story was sure to happen, either the gendarmes would lead him out of the hall by the arms, or his own friends would be forced to throw him out.If that did not happen, then there would still be something that would not happen with another person: either they would get drunk in the buffet in such a way that they could only laugh, or they would lie in the most outrageous way, so that in the end they would themselves feel ashamed. And they would lie completely for no reason at all: suddenly they would tell how they had a horse of some blue or pink coat, and other nonsense of the sort, so that the listeners would eventually all back away, saying, "Well, old boy, it seems you have started telling whoppers." There are people who have a nasty little habit of doing a neighbor harm, sometimes for no reason whatsoever. Someone else, for example, even a man in rank, with a noble appearance, with a star on his chest, will shake your hand, talk with you about profound, thought-provoking matters, and then, look, right there, before your eyes, he will do you a dirty trick. And he will do it just like a simple collegiate registrar, and not at all like a man with a star on his chest, talking about thought-provoking matters, so that you can only stand there and wonder, shrugging your shoulders, and nothing more. Nozdrev had the same strange passion. The closer anyone became to him, the quicker he would do them dirty. He would spread some ridiculous tale, one more foolish than it is hard to invent, ruin a wedding, a business deal, and not consider himself your enemy at all. On the contrary, if chance brought him to meet you again, he would treat you as a friend once more and even say, "You’re such a scoundrel, you never come to see me." In many respects Nozdrev was a many-sided man, that is, a jack-of-all-trades. The very next moment he would invite you to go anywhere, even to the ends of the earth, to join any venture you liked, to trade anything at all for anything you wanted. A gun, a dog, a horse — everything was an object for barter, but not at all with the aim of gaining anything: it all came simply from some restless agility and liveliness of character. If he had the luck at a fair to run into a simpleton and cheat him at cards, he would buy up a whole heap of things he had previously noticed in the shops: collars, candles for smoking, scarves for the nurse, a stallion, raisins, a silver washbasin, Dutch linen, fine flour, tobacco, pistols, herring, paintings, sharpening tools, pots, boots, earthenware — as much as his money would cover. However, it rarely happened that it was brought home; almost the same day it would all be lost to some other, luckier player, and sometimes even his own pipe with its tobacco pouch and mouthpiece would be added, and another time the whole four-horse team with everything: the carriage and coachman, so that the owner himself would set off in a short frock coat or arkhaluk to look for some acquaintance to borrow his carriage. Such was Nozdrev! Perhaps they will call his character hackneyed, and say that there are no Nozdrevs left now. Alas! those who say that will be wrong. Nozdrev will not die out of the world for a long time yet. He is everywhere among us and may only be walking around in a different coat, but people are thoughtless and unperceptive, and a man in a different coat seems to them to be a different person.

Meanwhile, three carriages had already drawn up to the porch of Nozdrev’s house. There was no preparation in the house for receiving them. In the middle of the dining room stood wooden trestles, and on them two peasants were whitewashing the walls, humming some endless song; the whole floor was splattered with whitewash. Nozdrev immediately ordered the peasants and the trestles out and ran into another room to give instructions. The guests heard him ordering lunch from the cook; judging from this, Chichikov, who was already beginning to feel a certain appetite, saw that they would not sit down to the table before five o’clock. Nozdrev, returning, led the guests around to inspect everything he had on his estate, and in a little over two hours showed them absolutely everything, so that nothing was left to show. First they went to look over the stable, where they saw two mares, one dapple gray, the other chestnut, then a bay stallion, unimpressive in appearance, but for whom Nozdrev swore he had paid ten thousand.

"Ten thousand? You didn’t pay that for him," the brother-in-law remarked. "He’s not worth even one."

"By God, I did pay ten thousand,"— said Nozdryov.

— You can swear as much as you like, — replied his brother-in-law.

— Well, if you want, let’s bet! — said Nozdryov.

His brother-in-law did not want to bet.

Then Nozdryov showed them the empty stalls, where there had once also been good horses. In this same stable they saw a goat, which, according to old belief, it was considered necessary to keep with horses, and which, as it seemed, got along with them, wandering under their bellies as if at home. Then Nozdryov took them to see a wolf cub tied up. “Here’s a wolf cub! — he said. — I feed him raw meat on purpose. I want him to be a perfect beast!” They went to look at the pond, in which, according to Nozdryov, there were fish of such size that two men could barely haul one out, though his relative did not fail to doubt it. “I’ll show you, Chichikov, — said Nozdryov, — an excellent pair of dogs: the black ones’ build is simply astonishing, and their marking is perfect!” — and he led them to a very nicely built little house, surrounded by a large yard fenced in on all sides. When they entered the yard, they saw all kinds of dogs there, rough-coated and smooth-coated, of every possible color and pattern: reddish-brown, black with tan points, pale-piebald, reddish-brown piebald, red-piebald, black-eared, gray-eared… There were all kinds of names, all the imperative forms: Shoot, Scold, Flutter, Fire, Skosyry, Cherkay, Torment, Scorch, Severga, Little Swallow, Reward, Guardian. Nozdryov was among them exactly like a father among his family; all of them, tails raised high at once, tails called rules by dog fanciers, flew straight toward the guests and began greeting them. About ten of them put their paws on Nozdryov’s shoulders. Scold showed the same friendship to Chichikov and, rising onto his hind legs, licked his lips with his tongue so that Chichikov immediately spat it out. They inspected the dogs, which amazed them with the strength of their black flesh, — they were good dogs. Then they went to inspect the Crimean bitch, who was already blind and, according to Nozdryov, was soon to die, though two years earlier she had been a very fine bitch; they inspected the bitch as well — she was indeed blind. Then they went to inspect the water mill, where there was no porhlitsa, into which the upper stone, spinning rapidly on the spindle, is set, — “the flapping one,” in the strange expression of the Russian peasant.

— And here the smithy will soon be too! — said Nozdryov. After walking a little farther, they did indeed see the smithy, and inspected it too.

— On this field, — said Nozdryov, pointing with his finger at the field, — there are so many hares that you can’t see the ground; I myself caught one by the hind legs with my own hands.

— Well, you can’t catch a hare with your hand! — noted his brother-in-law.

— But I did catch one, I caught it on purpose! — answered Nozdryov. — Now I’ll take you to see, — he continued, turning to Chichikov, — the boundary where my land ends.

Nozdryov led his guests across a field that in many places consisted of hummocks. The guests had to pick their way between fallow plots and harrowed strips. Chichikov was starting to feel tired. In many places their feet squeezed water up from the ground, so low-lying was the place. At first they were careful and stepped cautiously, but then, seeing that it served no purpose, they waded straight ahead, not distinguishing where the mud was thicker and where it was thinner. After going quite a distance, they did indeed see the boundary, consisting of a wooden post and a narrow ditch.

— Here’s the boundary! — said Nozdryov. — Everything you see on this side is mine, and even on the other side, that whole forest yonder, turning blue in the distance, and everything beyond the forest is all mine.

— But when did that forest become yours? — asked his brother-in-law. — Did you just buy it recently? It wasn’t yours before.

— Yes, I bought it recently, — answered Nozdryov.

— Then how did you manage to buy it so quickly?

— Why, I only bought it the day before yesterday, and paid dearly for it, damn it.

— But you were at the fair at that time.

— Oh, Sofron! Can’t a man be at the fair and buy land at the same time? Well, I was at the fair, and my manager bought it here without me.

— Yes, well, if it was the manager! — said his brother-in-law, but even there he doubted it and shook his head.The guests returned by the same nasty road to the house. Nozdryov led them into his study, in which, however, there were no signs of what is usually found in studies, that is, books or papers; only sabers hung there and two rifles, one worth 300, the other 800 rubles. The son-in-law, after looking around, only shook his head. Then Turkish daggers were shown, on one of which, by mistake, was engraved: “Master Saveliy Sibiryakov.” After that, Nozdryov showed the guests a barrel organ. He immediately cranked out something before them. The barrel organ played not without pleasantness, but in the middle of it, it seems, something happened, for the mazurka ended with the song: “Marlbrough Goes to War,” and “Marlbrough Goes to War” unexpectedly finished with some long-familiar waltz. Nozdryov had already long stopped turning the handle, but there was one very lively pipe in the barrel organ, which simply refused to quiet down, and so it kept whistling alone for a long time. Then came pipes, wooden, clay, meerschaum, smoked and unsmoked, covered with suede and uncovered, a shank with an amber mouthpiece, recently won, a tobacco pouch embroidered by some countess who had fallen head over heels in love with it somewhere at a post station, whose little hands, according to him, were the most subtle superflu, a word that probably meant for him the highest point of perfection. After having a bite of balyk, they sat down to the table around five o'clock. Dinner, as it seemed, was not a major thing in Nozdryov’s life; the dishes did not play a big role: some of it had burned, and some of it had not cooked at all. It was clear that the cook was guided more by some inspiration and put in whatever first came to hand: if pepper was nearby, he sprinkled pepper, if cabbage turned up, he shoved in cabbage, stuffed in milk, ham, peas, in short, go ahead and let it roll, as long as it was hot, and some taste would surely come out of it. But Nozdryov leaned on the wines: before the soup was even served, he had already poured the guests each a large glass of port and another of Sauternes, because there is no plain Sauternes in provincial and district towns. Then Nozdryov ordered a bottle of Madeira brought, better than which even the field marshal himself had never drunk. The Madeira truly burned even in the mouth, because merchants, already knowing the taste of landowners who liked good Madeira, doctored it mercilessly with rum, and sometimes even poured aqua regia into it, hoping that the Russian stomachs would bear anything. Then Nozdryov ordered another special bottle brought, which, according to him, was both Burgundy and Champagne together. He poured very industriously into both glasses, to the right and to the left, to the son-in-law and to Chichikov; Chichikov noticed, however, in passing, that he was not adding much for himself. This made him cautious, and as soon as Nozdryov began rambling on or poured for the son-in-law, he would tip his own glass into the plate at once. In a short time rowanberry vodka was brought to the table, which, according to Nozdryov, had the perfect taste of cream, but in which, to everyone's amazement, fusel oil could be heard in all its strength. Then they drank some balm, bearing a name that was even hard to remember, and the host himself the next time already called it by another name. Dinner had long since been over, and the wines had all been sampled, but the guests still sat at the table. Chichikov absolutely did not want to speak to Nozdryov in front of the son-in-law about the main matter. After all, the son-in-law was an outsider, and the matter required a private and friendly talk. Besides, the son-in-law could hardly be a dangerous man, because he seemed to have drunk enough, and sitting on a chair, kept nodding off every minute. Noticing himself that he was not in a reliable condition, he finally began asking to go home, but in such a lazy and languid voice, as if, to use the Russian expression, he were dragging a collar onto a horse with tongs.

— And not at all! I won't let you go! — said Nozdryov.

“No, don’t offend me, my friend, indeed, I’m going,” said the son-in-law, “you’ll offend me very much.”

“Nonsense, nonsense! we’ll knock up a little bank game this very minute.”

“No, you do it yourself, brother, I can’t, my wife will be greatly put out, indeed, I must tell her about the fair. Brother, it’s necessary, indeed, necessary, to give her pleasure. No, don’t hold me back!”

“Well, to hell with the wife! what an important business you’ll be doing together!”

“No, brother! she is so respectable and faithful! She does such services… believe me, I have tears in my eyes. No, don’t hold me back; as an honest man, I’ll go. I assure you of that on my true conscience.”

“Let him go, what good is he!” said Chichikov quietly to Nozdryov.

“Why, it’s true!” said Nozdryov. “I simply hate such softies to death!” and he added aloud: “Well, the devil take you, go home to your wife, fetyuk![Fetyuk is a rude word for a man, derived from the letter Fita, which some considered an indecent letter. (Note by N. V. Gogol.)]”

“No, brother, don’t call me a fetyuk,” replied the son-in-law, “I owe her my life. She is truly kind, dear, shows such affection… it moves me to tears; she asks what I saw at the fair, everything must be told, she is truly, truly dear.”

“Well then go and tell her nonsense! Here’s your cap.”

“No, brother, you really ought not to speak of her like that; in a way, you’re offending me myself, she is so dear.”

“Well then get off to her at once!”

“Yes, brother, I’ll go, excuse me for not being able to stay. I’d be glad to from the bottom of my heart, but I can’t.”

The son-in-law kept repeating his apologies for a long time, not noticing that he had long been sitting in the britzka, had long since driven out of the gate, and that there were long since only empty fields before him. One must think the wife heard very few details about the fair.

“What trash!” said Nozdryov, standing by the window and looking at the departing carriage. “Look how he’s lumbering off! The little lead horse is not bad, I’ve long wanted to hook him. But you can’t get on with him in any way. A fetyuk, just a fetyuk!”

“And what about it, brother,” said Nozdryov, pressing the sides of the pack with his fingers and bending it a little so that the paper cracked and a slip flew off. “Well, to pass the time, I’ll hold a bank for three hundred rubles!”

But Chichikov pretended not to hear what was being said and said, as if suddenly remembering:

“Oh! so I don’t forget: I have a request for you.”

“What is it?”

“First give your word that you’ll do it.”

“But what request?”

“Well, just give your word!”

“Very well.”

“On your word of honor?”

“On my word of honor.”

“Here’s the request: you’ve probably got many dead serfs who still haven’t been crossed off the revision, haven’t you?”

“Well, I do, what of it?”

“Transfer them to me, into my name.”

“And what for do you need them?”

“Well, I need them.”

“But why?”

“Well, I just need them… it’s my affair, in short, I need them.”

“Well, I’m sure you’ve got something up your sleeve. Admit it, what is it?”

“What have I got up my sleeve? Something like that can’t be up to much.”

“But why won’t you tell me?”

“And what profit is it to you to know? well, just because, a whim came over me.”

“Then here’s the thing: until you tell me, I won’t do it!”

“Well, you see, that’s already dishonest on your part: you gave your word and now you’re backing out.”

“Well, as you please, but I won’t do it until you say what for.”

“What should I say to him?” Chichikov thought, and after a minute’s reflection declared that the dead souls were needed for him to gain weight in society, that he did not have large estates, so for the time being at least some souls would do.

“You’re lying, lying!” said Nozdryov, not letting him finish. “You’re lying, brother!”

Chichikov himself noticed that he had not invented it very cleverly and that the excuse was quite weak.

“Well then I’ll tell you more directly,” he said, recovering himself, “only please don’t tell anyone. I’ve set my mind on getting married; but you must know that the bride’s father and mother are extremely ambitious people. Such a commission, indeed: I’m sorry I got involved, they insist that the groom must have no fewer than three hundred souls, and since I’m short by almost a hundred and fifty peasants…”

“Well, you’re lying! lying!”again Nozdriov shouted.

— Well, here it is, said Chichikov, — I have not lied by so much as this, — and he showed with his thumb on his little finger the very smallest part.

— I'll stake my head that you're lying!

— But that's insulting! What am I, really? Why must I be lying?

— Well, I know you: you're a great swindler, let me tell you that as a friend! If I were your superior, I'd hang you on the first tree.

Chichikov was offended by such a remark. Anything at all rude or offensive to decorum was already unpleasant to him. He did not even like to allow any familiarity with himself under any circumstances, unless the person was of too high a rank. And so now he was completely offended.

— By God, I would hang you, repeated Nozdryov, — I tell you that frankly, not to insult you, but just as a friend.

— There are limits to everything, said Chichikov with dignity. — If you want to parade such speech, then go to the barracks, — and then he added: — If you don't want to give them as a gift, then sell them.

— Sell them! But I know you, you're a rascal, you wouldn't pay much for them, would you?

— Ah, but you're no better! Look at you! Are they diamond, or what?

— Well, that's it. I knew you all along.

— Come now, what do I need a stallion for? said Chichikov, in real amazement at such an offer.

— How is that what for? I paid ten thousand for him, and I'm giving him to you for four.

— But what do I need a stallion for? I don't keep a stud.

— I don't need a stallion, damn him!

— I don't need a mare either.

— I don't need horses.

— Then you'd better sell them yourself, if you're sure you'll make three times the profit.

Chichikov thanked him for his goodwill and flatly refused both the gray horse and the dun mare.

— Well, what then am I to ride in?

“That devil of a restless spirit has possessed him!” thought Chichikov to himself, and decided at all costs to get rid of all carriages, organs, and every possible dog, despite the inconceivable barrel-like ribs and lumpy paws.

— Well, to go into bank means to expose oneself to the unknown, said Chichikov, and meanwhile glanced sideways at the cards in his hands. Both suits seemed to him very much like marked cards, and the whole pack looked highly suspicious.

— Why unknown? said Nozdryov. No unknown at all! As long as luck is on your side, you can win an awful lot. There it is! what luck! he said, beginning to deal in order to stir up rivalry. What luck! what luck! there, it’s just thumping away! there’s that cursed nine on which I lost everything! I felt it would sell, but then, with my eyes shut, I think to myself: “Devil take you, sell, you cursed card!”

When Nozdryov said this, Porfiry brought a bottle. But Chichikov flatly refused both to play and to drink.

— Why don’t you want to play? said Nozdryov.

— Well, because I’m not in the mood. To be honest, I’m not at all fond of playing.

Chichikov shrugged and added:

— Because I’m not fond of it.

Chichikov had not expected the last decision at all.

Despite this quarrel, however, guest and host dined together, though this time there were no wines with fancy names on the table. — Here’s your bed! I don’t even wish you good night!

After Nozdryov left, Chichikov remained in the most unpleasant mood. He inwardly resented himself, blamed himself for stopping by and wasting time for nothing But even more he blamed himself for bringing up the matter with him, for acting carelessly, like a child, like a fool: for this was not at all the sort of business to be entrusted to Nozdryov... Nozdryov was trash, Nozdryov could lie, add things, spread heaven knows what, and some other gossip would come of it—bad, very bad. “I’m simply a fool,” he said to himself. He slept very badly that night. Some tiny, fiercely active insects bit him unbearably painfully, so that with his whole handful he scratched at the wounded spot, muttering: “Ah, may the devil take you, along with Nozdryov!” He woke early in the morning. His first act was, after putting on his robe and boots, to go across the yard to the stable and order Selifan to harness the carriage at once. — So-so, Chichikov replied very dryly.

— As for me, brother, said Nozdryov, such filth kept crawling in all night that it’s disgusting to talk about, and in my mouth after yesterday it was exactly as if a squadron had spent the night there. Chichikov went into the room to dress and wash. When he came out into the dining room after that, a tea service with a bottle of rum was already on the table. There were traces of yesterday’s dinner and supper in the room; it seemed the floor brush had not been used at all. Bread crumbs were lying on the floor, and tobacco ash was even visible on the tablecloth. The master himself, who soon came in, had nothing on under his robe except an open chest, on which some kind of beard was growing. Holding a pipe in his hand and sipping from a cup, he was very good for a painter who does not love the fear of gentlemen plastered down and curled, like barbers’ signs, or clipped close with the comb.

“Well then, what do you think?” said Nozdryov after a slight pause. “Don’t want to gamble for souls?”

“I already told you, brother, I don’t gamble; if you want to sell, I’ll buy.”

“I don’t want to sell it, that wouldn’t be friendly. I’m not going to peel the skin off who knows what. To bet on a necktie, that’s another matter. Let’s at least throw in a waist!”

“I already said no.”

“And you don’t want to exchange?”

“No.”

“Well, listen, let’s play checkers, you win — they’re all yours. I’ve got a lot of them that need to be crossed off the revision list. Hey, Porfiry, bring the checkerboard here.”

“Waste of time, I won’t play.”

“But this isn’t gambling; there can be no luck or cheating here: it’s all skill; I even warn you that I don’t know how to play at all, unless you give me something up front.”

“Let me see, — thought Chichikov to himself, — I’ll play checkers with him! I was pretty good at checkers, and it’ll be hard for him to pull any tricks here.”

“All right, I’ll play checkers, then.”

“Souls go for a hundred rubles!”

“Why so much? Fifty is enough.”

“No, what kind of stake is fifty? Better yet, for that amount I’ll include some middling puppy or a gold watch seal.”

“Well, all right!” said Chichikov.

“How much will you give me up front?” said Nozdryov.

“What for? Of course, nothing.”

“At least let my first two moves count.”

“I don’t want to, I’m a bad player myself.”

“We know your sort, how badly you play!” said Nozdryov, making a move with a checker.

“It’s been a long time since I’ve held checkers in my hands!” said Chichikov, moving a checker as well.

“We know your sort, how badly you play!” said Nozdryov, making a move with a checker.

“It’s been a long time since I’ve held checkers in my hands!” said Chichikov, moving a checker.

“We know your sort, how badly you play!” said Nozdryov, moving a checker, but at the same time he also moved another checker with his sleeve cuff.

“It’s been a long time since I’ve held them in my hands!... Hey, hey! what’s this, brother? Move it back!” said Chichikov.

“Move what?”

“That checker,” said Chichikov, and at the same time he saw right under his nose another one, which seemed to be making its way to king. Only God knew where it had come from. “No,” said Chichikov, getting up from the table, “there’s no way to play with you! They don’t move like that, three checkers at once!”

“Why three? That was by mistake. One moved unintentionally, I’ll move it back, all right.”

“And where did the other one come from?”

“What other one?”

“This one here, trying to make king?”

“Well, I’ll be damned, as if you don’t remember!”

“No, brother, I counted every move and remember everything; you just placed it there now. It belongs over there!”

“What do you mean, over there?” said Nozdryov, blushing. “Why, brother, I see you’re a fabricator!”

“No, brother, I think you’re the fabricator, only not a successful one.”

“And who do you take me for?” said Nozdryov. “Do you think I’d cheat?”

“I don’t take you for anyone, but I’ll never play from now on.”

“No, you can’t отказаться,” said Nozdryov, growing heated, “the game has started!”

“I have the right to refuse, because you’re not playing the way a decent person should.”

“No, you’re lying, you can’t say that!”

“No, brother, you’re the one lying!”

“I didn’t cheat, and you can’t refuse, you must finish the game!”

“You won’t make me do that,” said Chichikov coolly, andhaving come up to the board, he mixed up the checkers.

Nozdryov flared up and came so close to Chichikov that the latter stepped back two paces.

"I'll make you play! It doesn't matter that you mixed up the checkers, I remember all the moves. We'll set them up again just as they were."

"No, brother, it's over, I won't play with you."

"So you don't want to play?"

"You see for yourself that it's impossible to play with you."

"No, tell me straight, you don't want to play?" said Nozdryov, coming closer still.

"I don't want to!" said Chichikov and, however, brought both hands a little closer to his face just in case, for things were indeed getting hot.

This precaution was very much in place, because Nozdryov swung his arm... and it might very well have happened that one of our hero's pleasant and full cheeks would have been covered with indelible disgrace; but, having happily dodged the blow, he seized Nozdryov by both his fiery hands and held him tight.

"Porfiry, Pavlushka!" Nozdryov shouted in a rage, struggling to break free.

Hearing these words, Chichikov, so as not to make the servants witnesses to the scandalous scene and at the same time feeling that holding Nozdryov was useless, let go of his hands. At that very moment Porfiry came in, and with him Pavlushka, a sturdy fellow to deal with whom was by no means advantageous.

"So you don't want to finish the game?" said Nozdryov. "Answer me straight!"

"There is no way to finish the game," said Chichikov and looked out the window. He saw his britzka, standing all ready, and Selifan seemed to be waiting for a sign to draw it up to the porch, but there was no way to get out of the room: two sturdy serf dolts were standing in the doorway.

"So you don't want to finish the game?" repeated Nozdryov, his face burning like fire.

"If only you played as befits an honest man. But now I can't."

"Ah! so you can't, scoundrel! when you saw that it wasn't going your way, now you can't! Beat him!" he shouted wildly, turning to Porfiry and Pavlushka, while he himself seized a cherry-wood pipe stem. Chichikov turned as pale as a sheet. He wanted to say something, but felt that his lips were moving without a sound.
