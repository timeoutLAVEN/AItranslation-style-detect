Anton Pavlovich Chekhov

The Lady with the Dog

I

     It was said that a new person had appeared on the seafront: a lady with a dog. Dmitri Dmitrich Gurov, who had been living in Yalta for two weeks and had grown used to it, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in the pavilion at Vernet's, he saw a young lady walk along the seafront, not tall, a blonde, in a beret: a white spitz ran after her.

     And afterward he met her in the town garden and on the square several times a day. She strolled alone, always in the same beret, with the white spitz; no one knew who she was, and people simply called her: the lady with the dog.

     "If she is here without a husband and without acquaintances," Gurov reasoned, "it would not be amiss to get acquainted with her."

     He was not yet forty, but he already had a daughter of twelve and two sons in high school. He had been married early, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed a year and a half older than he. She was a tall woman, with dark eyebrows, erect, dignified, solid, and, as she called herself, thinking. She read a great deal, did not write the hard sign in letters, called her husband not Dmitri but Dimitri, while he secretly considered her narrow-minded, narrow, inelegant, was afraid of her, and did not like being at home. He had begun to be unfaithful to her long ago, was unfaithful often, and probably for that reason almost always spoke ill of women, and when they were talked about in his presence, he called them:

     — The lower race!

     It seemed to him that he was sufficiently taught by bitter experience to call them whatever he liked, but still, without the "lower race," he could not have lived even two days. In the company of men he was bored, ill at ease, with them he was taciturn, cold, but when he was among women, he felt free and knew what to talk about with them and how to behave; and even to be silent with them was easy for him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature there was something attractive, elusive, that disposed women toward him, drew them to him; he knew this, and he too was drawn to them by some force.

     Repeated experience, truly bitter experience, had long ago taught him that every intimacy, which at first so pleasantly diversifies life and seems a sweet and easy adventure, among decent people, especially Muscovites, who are slow to move, indecisive, inevitably grows into a whole task, extraordinarily complex, and the situation in the end becomes burdensome. But at every new meeting with an interesting woman, this experience somehow slipped from his memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

     And so one evening he was dining in the garden, and the lady in the beret came up unhurriedly to occupy the neighboring table. Her expression, her walk, her dress, her hairstyle told him that she was from decent society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here… In the stories about the impurity of local morals there was much untruth; he despised them and knew that such stories were for the most part invented by people who would themselves gladly have sinned if they knew how, but when the lady sat down at the neighboring table three steps from him, he recalled these stories about easy conquests, about trips to the mountains, and the tempting thought of a quick, fleeting affair, of a romance with an unknown woman whose name and surname you do not know, suddenly took possession of him.

     He gently beckoned the spitz to him and, when it came up, shook his finger at it. The spitz growled. Gurov shook his finger again.

     The lady glanced at him and immediately lowered her eyes.

     — He doesn't bite, — she said and blushed.

     — May I give him a bone? — And when she nodded affirmatively, he asked affably: — Have you been in Yalta long?

     — Five days.

     — And I am already dragging out my second week here.

     They were silent for a little while.

     — Time passes quickly, and meanwhile it is so boring here! — she said, without looking at him.

     — It is only customary to say that it is boring here. A provincial lives somewhere in Belyov or Zhizdra — and he is not bored, but when he comes here: "Ah, boring! Ah, dust!" You would think he had come from Granada.

     She laughed.Then both continued eating in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked along together - and a light, playful conversation began between people who are free, contented, and who do not care where they go or what they talk about. They strolled and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was a lilac color, so soft and warm, and a golden strip ran across it from the moon. They talked about how stuffy it was after the hot day. Gurov told her that he was from Moscow, a philologist by education, but worked in a bank; he had once prepared to sing in a private opera but gave it up, and he had two houses in Moscow... And from her he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg, but had married in S., where she had already lived for two years, that she would stay in Yalta for another month or so, and that her husband might come for her, as he too wanted to rest. She could not explain where her husband worked - in the provincial government or in the provincial zemstvo board - and she herself found it funny. And Gurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

     Later, in his room, he thought about her, about how tomorrow she would probably meet him. It had to be. Going to bed, he remembered that she had so recently been a schoolgirl, studying, just as his daughter did now, and he remembered how much shyness and awkwardness there still was in her laughter, in her conversation with a stranger - it must have been the first time in her life that she was alone, in such surroundings, where people followed her, looked at her, and spoke to her with only one secret purpose, which she could not help suspecting. He remembered her thin, weak neck, her beautiful gray eyes.

     "There is something pitiful about her, after all," he thought, and began to fall asleep.

     

II

     

     A week passed after they met. It was a holiday. The rooms were stuffy, and in the streets the dust swirled in a whirlwind and tore off hats. All day one wanted to drink, and Gurov often went into the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeyevna now water with syrup, now ice cream. There was nowhere to go.

     In the evening, when it had calmed down a little, they went to the pier to see the steamer arrive. There were many strollers at the landing; people had gathered to meet someone and were holding bouquets. And here two peculiarities of the smart Yalta crowd stood out clearly: elderly ladies were dressed like young ones, and there were many generals.

     Because of the rough sea the steamer arrived late, when the sun had already set, and before docking at the pier it turned around for a long time. Anna Sergeyevna looked through her lorgnette at the steamer and the passengers, as if searching for acquaintances, and when she turned to Gurov, her eyes glittered. She talked a great deal, and her questions were abrupt, and she immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

     The smart crowd dispersed, faces could no longer be seen, the wind died down completely, and Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood there as if waiting to see whether anyone else would come off the steamer. Anna Sergeyevna had already fallen silent and was smelling the flowers, not looking at Gurov.

     "The weather has improved toward evening," he said. "Where shall we go now? Shall we go somewhere?"

     She did not answer.

     Then he looked at her intently and suddenly embraced her and kissed her on the lips, and he was overwhelmed by the scent and moisture of the flowers, and at once he looked around fearfully: had anyone seen?

     "Let us go to your place..." he said quietly.

     And both walked quickly.

     Her room was stuffy and smelled of the perfume she had bought in a Japanese shop. Looking at her now, Gurov thought: "What meetings do not happen in life!" From the past he retained a memory of carefree, good-natured women, cheerful from love, grateful to him for happiness, even if very brief; and of such women - like, for example, his wife - who loved without sincerity, with excessive talk, affectedly, with hysteria, with an expression as though it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of two or three others, very beautiful, cold, on whose faces a predatory expression suddenly flashed, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it could give, and these were no longer in their first youth, capricious, unreasonable, domineering, unintelligent women, and when Gurov cooled toward them,but their beauty aroused hatred in him, and the lace on their underwear seemed to him then like fish scales.

     But here was the same timidity, the angularity of inexperienced youth, an awkward feeling; and there was an impression of bewilderment, as if someone had suddenly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the dog," took what had happened in some special, very serious way, as if it were her fall - so it seemed, and it was strange and out of place. Her features had drooped and wilted, and long hair hung mournfully on either side of her face, she fell to thinking in a dejected pose, like a sinner in an old painting.

     "It is not good," she said. "You are the first now not to respect me."

     There was a watermelon on the table in the room. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat unhurriedly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

     Anna Sergeyevna was touching, she breathed purity, a decent, naive woman who had lived little; the solitary candle burning on the table barely lit her face, but it was clear that she felt bad in her soul.

     "Why would I be able to stop respecting you?" asked Gurov. "You yourself do not know what you are saying."

     "Let God forgive me!" she said, and her eyes filled with tears. "This is awful."

     "You are as if justifying yourself."

     "How can I justify myself? I am a bad, low woman, I despise myself and do not think of justification. I did not deceive my husband, but myself. And not only now, but for a long time I have been deceiving. My husband, perhaps, is an honest, good man, but he is a lackey! I do not know what he does there, how he serves, but I know only that he is a lackey. I was twenty years old when I married him, I was tormented by curiosity, I wanted something better; surely there is, I said to myself, another life. I wanted to live! To live and live... Curiosity burned me... you do not understand this, but, I swear to God, I could no longer control myself, something was happening to me, I could not be restrained, I told my husband I was ill, and I came here... And here I kept walking around as if in a daze, like a madwoman... and now I have become a vulgar, nasty woman whom anyone can despise.

     Gurov was already bored with listening, he was irritated by the naive tone, this repentance, so unexpected and inappropriate; if it had not been for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a role.

     "I do not understand," he said quietly, "what is it you want?"

     She hid her face on his chest and pressed close to him.

     "Believe, believe me, I beg you..." she said. "I love an honest, pure life, but sin is loathsome to me, I myself do not know what I am doing. Simple people say: the unclean one has confused me. And I can now say of myself that the unclean one has confused me."

     "Enough, enough..." he muttered.

     He looked into her fixed, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke quietly and tenderly, and she gradually calmed down, and cheerfulness returned to her; they both began to laugh.

     Then, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment, the city with its cypresses had a completely dead look, but the sea was still noisy and beating against the shore; one boat was rocking on the waves, and on it a little lantern flickered sleepily.

     They found a cabdriver and drove to Oreanda.

     "I just now learned your surname downstairs in the front hall: it is written on the board, von Diderits," said Gurov. "Is your husband a German?"

     "No, his grandfather, it seems, was a German, but he himself is Orthodox."

     In Oreanda they sat on a bench, not far from the church, looked down at the sea and were silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist, white clouds stood motionless on the mountain tops. The leaves did not stir on the trees, the cicadas were chirping, and the monotonous, muffled noise of the sea, coming from below, spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. It had made this noise below when there was as yet neither Yalta nor Oreanda here, now it makes a noise and will make a noise just as indifferently and muffledly when we are no more. And in this constancy, in this complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, there lies, perhaps, the pledge of our eternal salvation, of the continuous movement of life on earth, of continuous perfection. Sitting beside a young woman who at dawn had seemed so beautiful,Calmed and enchanted by this fairy-tale setting - the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the wide sky - Gurov thought about how, in essence, if you think about it, everything is beautiful in this world, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget about the higher purposes of existence, about our human dignity.

     

     A man came up - probably a watchman - looked at them and walked away. And this detail seemed so mysterious and also beautiful. They could see a steamer arriving from Feodosia, lit by the morning dawn, already without lights.

     - Dew on the grass, - said Anna Sergeyevna after a silence.

     - Yes. Time to go home.

     They returned to the city.

     Then every noon they met on the embankment, had breakfast together, had lunch, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept badly and that her heart beat anxiously, asked the same questions over and over, agitated now by jealousy, now by fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the public garden or in the park, when there was no one near them, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with a wary look and fear that someone might see, the heat, the smell of the sea and the constant flickering before his eyes of idle, well-dressed, well-fed people seemed to have transformed him; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how enticing, was impatiently passionate, did not leave her side for a single step, while she often grew pensive and kept asking him to admit that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, but only saw in her a vulgar woman. Almost every evening, later on, they drove somewhere out of town, to Oreanda or to the waterfall; and the outing was a success, the impressions were invariably beautiful and majestic each time.

     They expected her husband to arrive. But a letter came from him in which he informed her that his eyes had become inflamed and begged his wife to return home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeyevna hurried to leave.

     - It is good that I am leaving, - she told Gurov. - It is fate itself.

     She went by carriage, and he accompanied her. They traveled the whole day. When she got into the railway carriage and when the second bell rang, she said:

     - Let me have one more look at you... One more look. Like this.

     She did not cry, but she was sad, as if ill, and her face trembled.

     - I will think of you... remember you, - she said. - God be with you, stay. Do not remember me unkindly. We are parting forever, it has to be so, because we should never have met at all. Well, God be with you.

     The train left quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and a minute later the noise could no longer be heard, as if everything had conspired on purpose to put an end as quickly as possible to this sweet oblivion, this madness. And, left alone on the platform and gazing into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the chirping of grasshoppers and the humming of the telegraph wires with a feeling as though he had just awakened. And he thought that here in his life there had been yet another adventure or escapade, and it too was already over, and now only a memory remained... He was touched, sad, and felt a slight remorse; after all, this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he had been affable and warm with her, but all the same in his manner with her, in his tone and caresses, there showed like a shadow a light mockery, the somewhat coarse arrogance of a happy man who was moreover almost twice her age. All the time she had called him kind, extraordinary, lofty; evidently he had seemed to her not what he really was, which meant he had unwittingly deceived her...

     Here at the station it already smelled of autumn, the evening was cool.

     "It is time for me to go north too, - thought Gurov, leaving the platform. - Time!"

     

III

     

     At home in Moscow everything was already wintry, the stoves were heated, and in the morning, when the children were getting ready for school and drinking tea, it was dark, and the nurse would light the fire for a short while. The frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of riding in a sleigh, it is pleasant to see the white earth, the white roofs, one breathes softly, wonderfully, and at such a time one recalls one's youthful years. The old lindens and birches, white with hoarfrost, have a good-natured expression, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms,and near them one no longer wants to think of mountains and the sea.

     Gurov was a Muscovite, he returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and walked along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the ringing of bells, the recent trip and the places in which he had been lost all their charm for him. Little by little he immersed himself in Moscow life, was already greedily reading three newspapers a day and said that he did not read Moscow newspapers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties, anniversaries, and he was already flattered that he had famous lawyers and actors among his acquaintances and that at the Doctors' Club he played cards with a professor. He could already eat a whole portion of selianka served in a frying pan...

     A month or so would pass, and Anna Sergeyevna, it seemed to him, would be covered in mist in his memory and would only occasionally come to him in dreams with a touching smile, as other women had come. But more than a month passed, deep winter set in, and in his memory everything was still clear, as if he had parted from Anna Sergeyevna only yesterday. And the memories burned brighter and brighter. Whether the voices of his children preparing their lessons reached his study in the evening quiet, or he heard a romance or the organ in a restaurant, or the blizzard howled in the fireplace, suddenly everything came back to life in his memory: what had happened on the pier, and the early morning with mist on the mountains, and the steamer from Feodosia, and the kisses. He walked about the room for a long time and remembered, and smiled, and then the memories turned into dreams, and the past in his imagination mingled with what would be. Anna Sergeyevna did not come to him in dreams, but followed him everywhere like a shadow and watched over him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed better than he had been then, in Yalta. She gazed at him in the evenings from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the gentle rustle of her clothing. On the street he followed women with his eyes, looking for one who resembled her...

     And already a strong desire tormented him to share his memories with someone. But at home it was impossible to speak of his love, and outside the home there was no one to speak to. Not with the lodgers, and not at the bank. And what was there to talk about? Had he really been in love then? Had there been anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relations with Anna Sergeyevna? And he had to speak vaguely of love, of women, and no one guessed what the matter was, and only his wife moved her dark eyebrows and said:

     — For you, Dimitri, the role of a fatal man does not suit you at all.

     One night, coming out of the Doctors' Club with his partner, an official, he could not restrain himself and said:

     — If you knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!

     The official got into his sledge and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

     — Dmitri Dmitrich!

     — What?

     — And earlier you were right: the sturgeon does have a bit of a smell!

These words, so ordinary, for some reason suddenly revolted Gurov, seemed to him humiliating, impure. What wild manners, what faces! What stupid nights, what uninteresting, unnoticeable days! A frantic game of cards, gluttony, drunkenness, constant conversations all about the same thing. Pointless affairs and conversations all about the same thing snatch away for their share the best part of time, the best powers, and in the end what remains is some clipped, wingless life, some nonsense, and one cannot leave or run away, as if one were sitting in a madhouse or in a prison company!

     Gurov did not sleep all night and was indignant, and then spent the whole day with a headache. And on the following nights he slept badly, kept sitting in bed and thinking or pacing from corner to corner. The children bored him, the bank bored him, he did not want to go anywhere or talk about anything.

     In December during the holidays he prepared for a journey and told his wife that he was leaving for Petersburg to plead for a certain young man, - and he left for S. Why? He himself did not know well. He wanted to see Anna Sergeevna and talk, arrange a meeting, if possible.

     He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the whole floor was covered with gray soldier's cloth and on the table was an inkwell, gray with dust, with a horseman on a horse who had his arm raised with a hat, and his head knocked off. The porter gave him the necessary information: von Diderits lives on Old Goncharnaya Street, in his own house - it is not far from the hotel, he lives well, richly, has his own horses, everyone in town knows him. The porter pronounced it thus: Drydyrits.

     Gurov went unhurriedly to Old Goncharnaya, found the house. Right opposite the house stretched a fence, gray, long, with nails.

     "You would run away from such a fence," thought Gurov, glancing now at the windows, now at the fence.

     He considered: today is a non-office day, and the husband is probably at home. And it made no difference anyway, it would be tactless to enter the house and embarrass. If he were to send a note, it might fall into the husband's hands, and then everything could be ruined. Best of all was to rely on chance. And he kept walking along the street and near the fence, waiting for that chance. He saw a beggar enter the gate and the dogs attack him, then, an hour later, he heard piano playing, and the sounds came faint, unclear. Anna Sergeevna must have been playing. The front door suddenly opened, and an old woman came out, and after her ran the familiar white spitz. Gurov wanted to call the dog, but his heart suddenly began to pound, and from excitement he could not remember the spitz's name.

     He walked and hated the gray fence more and more, and already thought with irritation that Anna Sergeevna had forgotten about him and perhaps was already amusing herself with another, and that was so natural in the position of a young woman who was forced to see this accursed fence from morning till evening. He returned to his room and sat for a long time on the sofa, not knowing what to do, then had dinner, then slept for a long time.

     "How stupid and restless all this is," he thought, waking up and looking at the dark windows; it was already evening. "So I slept for some reason. What am I going to do now at night?"

     He sat on the bed, covered with a cheap gray blanket, just like a hospital one, and teased himself with vexation:

     "So much for the lady with the dog... So much for the adventure... So here you sit."

     Earlier in the morning, at the station, a poster with very large letters had caught his eye: "The Geisha" was being performed for the first time. He remembered this and went to the theater.

     "It is very possible that she attends first performances," he thought.

     The theater was full. And here, as in all provincial theaters generally, there was mist above the chandelier, the gallery was noisily restless; in the first row before the performance began stood local dandies with their hands behind their backs; and here, in the governor's box, in the first seat sat the governor's daughter in a boa, while the governor himself modestly hid behind the curtain, and only his hands were visible; the curtain swayed, the orchestra tuned for a long time. All the while the public was entering and taking their seats, Gurov eagerly searched with his eyes.

     Anna Sergeevna entered too. She sat in the third row, and when Gurov looked at her, his heart contracted, and he understood clearly,that for him now there was no one in all the world nearer, dearer, and more important; she, lost in the provincial crowd, this little woman, unremarkable in any way, with a vulgar lorgnette in her hands, now filled his entire life, was his grief, his joy, the only happiness he now desired for himself; and to the sounds of a bad orchestra, of wretched provincial violins, he thought about how beautiful she was. He thought and daydreamed.

     Together with Anna Sergeyevna a young man with small side-whiskers came in and sat down beside her, very tall, stoop-shouldered; with every step he wagged his head and seemed to be constantly bowing. He was probably the husband whom she had once in Yalta, in a burst of bitter feeling, called a lackey. And indeed, in his long figure, in his side-whiskers, in his small bald patch, there was something lackey-like and modest; he smiled sweetly, and on his buttonhole there gleamed some scholarly badge, just like a lackey's number.

     During the first intermission the husband went out to smoke, and she remained in her seat. Gurov, who was also sitting in the stalls, went up to her and said in a trembling voice, smiling with effort:

     — Hello.

     She glanced at him and turned pale, then glanced again in horror, not believing her eyes, and tightly clasped in her hands both her fan and her lorgnette, evidently struggling with herself not to faint. Both were silent. She sat, he stood, frightened by her confusion, not daring to sit down beside her. The tuning violins and the flute began to play, and suddenly it became frightening, as though everyone were looking from all the boxes. But then she got up and quickly walked toward the exit; he followed her, and both walked senselessly, along corridors, up and down staircases, now ascending, now descending, and before their eyes flashed certain people in judicial, teachers', and appanage uniforms, all with badges; ladies flashed by, coats on hangers, a draft blew, enveloping them in the smell of cigarette butts. And Gurov, whose heart was beating violently, thought:

     "O Lord! And what are these people for, this orchestra..."

     And at that moment he suddenly remembered how that evening at the station, after seeing Anna Sergeyevna off, he had told himself that everything was over and they would never see each other again. But how far it still was from the end!

     On the narrow, gloomy staircase where it said "Way to the Amphitheater," she stopped.

     — How you frightened me! — she said, breathing heavily, still pale, stunned. — Oh, how you frightened me! I am barely alive. Why did you come? Why?

     — But understand, Anna, understand... — he said in a low voice, hurrying. — I beg you, understand...

     She looked at him with fear, with entreaty, with love, looked intently, to fix his features more firmly in her memory.

     — I suffer so! — she continued, not listening to him. — I thought only of you the whole time, I lived by thoughts of you. And I wanted to forget, to forget, but why, why did you come?

     Higher up, on the landing, two schoolboys were smoking and looking down, but Gurov did not care, he drew Anna Sergeyevna to him and began kissing her face, her cheeks, her hands.

     — What are you doing, what are you doing! — she said in horror, pushing him away from her. — We have both gone mad. Leave today, leave right now... I adjure you by all that is holy, I implore you... Someone is coming!

     Someone was coming up the stairs from below.

     — You must leave... — Anna Sergeyevna continued in a whisper. — Do you hear, Dmitri Dmitrich? I will come to you in Moscow. I have never been happy, I am unhappy now and never, never will be happy, never! Do not make me suffer even more! I swear, I will come to Moscow. And now let us part! My dear, good, precious one, let us part!

     She pressed his hand and began quickly descending, still looking back at him, and by her eyes it was clear that she really was not happy. Gurov stood for a while, listened, then when everything had grown quiet, found his coat check and left the theater.

     

IV

     

     And Anna Sergeyevna began coming to him in Moscow. Once every two or three months she would leave S. and tell her husband that she was going to consult a professor about her female ailment — and her husband believed and did not believe. Having arrived in Moscow,She stayed at the "Slavic Bazaar" and immediately sent a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov went to see her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

     One day he was on his way to her like this on a winter morning (the messenger had been at his place the evening before and had not found him in). His daughter was walking with him, whom he wanted to see to the gymnasium; it was on the way. A thick wet snow was falling.

     "It's three degrees above freezing now, and yet it's snowing," Gurov said to his daughter. "But that warmth is only on the surface of the earth; in the upper layers of the atmosphere the temperature is quite different."

     "Papa, why is there no thunder in winter?"

     He explained that too. He talked and thought about how he was going to a rendezvous and not a living soul knew about it, and probably never would know. He had two lives: one open, which everyone who needed to see and know saw and knew, full of conventional truth and conventional deception, completely resembling the lives of his acquaintances and friends, and another, which flowed in secret. And by some strange coincidence, perhaps accidental, everything that was important, interesting, necessary to him, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, that constituted the kernel of his life, occurred in secret from others, while everything that was his lie, his shell, in which he hid to conceal the truth, such as his service in the bank, his arguments at the club, his "lower race," his going with his wife to anniversaries, all this was open. And by himself he judged others, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that under the cover of secrecy, as under the cover of night, each person's true and most interesting life went on. Every personal existence rests on secrecy, and perhaps partly for this reason a cultured person fusses so nervously about having personal secrecy respected.

     After seeing his daughter to the gymnasium, Gurov set off for the "Slavic Bazaar." He took off his fur coat downstairs, went up, and quietly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, exhausted by the journey and by waiting, had been expecting him since the previous evening; she was pale, looked at him and did not smile, and as soon as he entered she fell against his chest. It was as if they had not seen each other for two years; their kiss was long, prolonged.

     "Well, how are you living there?" he asked. "What's new?"

     "Wait, I'll tell you right now… I can't."

     She could not speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed a handkerchief to her eyes.

     "Well, let her cry, and meanwhile I'll sit here," he thought, and sat down in an armchair.

     Then he rang and said to bring him tea; and afterward, while he drank the tea, she still stood, turned away toward the window… She was crying from agitation, from the sorrowful awareness that their life had turned out so sadly; they saw each other only in secret, hiding from people like thieves! Was their life not broken?

     "Well, stop it!" he said.

     It was obvious to him that this love of theirs would not end soon, and it was unknown when it would. Anna Sergeyevna was becoming more and more attached to him, adored him, and it would be unthinkable to tell her that all this must one day come to an end; and she would not have believed it anyway.

     He went up to her and took her by the shoulders to caress her, to joke, and at that moment he saw himself in the mirror.

     His head had already begun to turn gray. And it seemed strange to him that he had aged so much in recent years, had grown so much less handsome. The shoulders on which his hands lay were warm and trembled. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already close to beginning to fade and wither, like his own life. Why did she love him so? He had always seemed to women not to be who he was, and they loved in him not himself but the person created by their imagination, whom they had greedily sought in their lives; and then, when they noticed their mistake, they still loved him. And not one of them had been happy with him. Time passed; he made acquaintances, became close, parted, but not once did he love; there had been everything, but only not love.

     And only now, when his head had turned gray, did he fall in love properly, truly, for the first time in his life.

     Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other,as very close, dear people, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had destined them for each other, and it was incomprehensible why he was married and she was married; and they were exactly like two migratory birds, a male and a female, that had been caught and forced to live in separate cages. They forgave each other what they were ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love of theirs had changed them both.

     Before, in sad moments, he had comforted himself with all sorts of arguments that happened to come into his head, but now he had no time for arguments, he felt deep compassion, he wanted to be sincere, tender...

     "Stop, my good girl," he said. "You've had a cry, and that's enough... Now let's talk, let's think of something."

     Then they consulted for a long time, talked about how to save themselves from the necessity of hiding, deceiving, living in different cities, not seeing each other for long periods. How to free themselves from these unbearable bonds?

     "How? How?" he asked, clutching his head. "How?"

     And it seemed that just a little more and a solution would be found, and then a new, beautiful life would begin; and it was clear to both that the end was still far, far away and that the most complex and difficult part was only just beginning.

     

     

The Black Monk

     

I

     

     Andrei Vasilyich Kovrin, a master's degree holder, was tired and had upset his nerves. He was not undergoing treatment, but somehow in passing, over a bottle of wine, he had spoken with a friend who was a doctor, and the doctor had advised him to spend the spring and summer in the country. Incidentally, a long letter arrived from Tanya Pesotskaya, who asked him to come to Borisovka and stay for a visit. And he decided that he really did need to take a trip.

     At first, this was in April, he went to his own place, to his ancestral Kovrinka, and here he lived in solitude for three weeks; then, after waiting for the roads to improve, he set off by horse to his former guardian and educator Pesotsky, a horticulturist known in Russia. From Kovrinka to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, it was reckoned to be no more than seventy versts, and traveling on the soft spring road in a comfortable spring carriage was a true delight.

     Pesotsky's house was enormous, with columns, with lions whose plaster had peeled off, and with a liveried footman at the entrance. The old park, gloomy and severe, laid out in the English style, stretched almost a whole verst from the house to the river and here ended at a steep, abrupt clay bank, on which grew pines with exposed roots resembling shaggy paws; below, the water gleamed desolately, snipes darted about with a plaintive squeak, and there was always such a mood here that one might as well sit down and write a ballad. But near the house itself, in the courtyard and in the fruit orchard, which together with the nurseries occupied about thirty desyatins, it was cheerful and joyful even in bad weather. Such amazing roses, lilies, camellias, such tulips of all possible colors, from bright white to black as soot, in general such a wealth of flowers as at Pesotsky's, Kovrin had never happened to see anywhere else. Spring was still only at the beginning, and the very real luxury of the flower beds was still hidden in the greenhouses, but even what bloomed along the alleys and here and there in the flowerbeds was enough, while walking through the garden, to feel oneself in a kingdom of delicate colors, especially in the early hours, when dew sparkled on every petal.

     What was the decorative part of the garden and what Pesotsky himself contemptuously called trifles had once in Kovrin's childhood produced a fairy-tale impression. What whims were there, exquisite deformities and mockeries of nature! There were espaliers of fruit trees, a pear shaped like a pyramidal poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an umbrella made from an apple tree, arches, monograms, candelabra, and even 1862 made of plums, a number signifying the year when Pesotsky first took up horticulture. There were also beautiful slender little trees with straight and strong trunks like palms, and only after peering closely could one recognize in these little trees gooseberries or currants. But what most gladdened the garden and gave it a lively appearance,So this is a constant movement. From early morning until evening, around the trees, bushes, on the alleys and flower beds, like ants, people swarmed with wheelbarrows, hoes, watering cans…

     Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys' in the evening, at about ten o'clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semyonovich, in great anxiety. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer foretold frost by morning, but meanwhile the gardener Ivan Karlovich had gone to town and there was no one to rely on. At supper they talked only about the morning frost and it was decided that Tanya would not go to bed and would walk through the garden at about one o'clock to see that everything was in order, and Yegor Semyonovich would get up at three o'clock or even earlier.

     Kovrin spent the whole evening with Tanya and after midnight went with her into the garden. It was cold. In the yard there was already a strong smell of burning. In the large fruit orchard, which was called the commercial orchard and brought Yegor Semyonovich several thousand rubles of net income every year, a black, thick, acrid smoke spread over the ground and, enveloping the trees, saved those thousands from the frost. The trees here stood in checkerboard order, their rows were straight and regular, like ranks of soldiers, and this strict pedantic regularity and the fact that all the trees were of the same height and had completely identical crowns and trunks made the picture monotonous and even boring. Kovrin and Tanya walked along the rows where smoldering fires of manure, straw, and all sorts of refuse were burning, and occasionally they met workers who wandered in the smoke like shadows. Only the cherries, plums, and some varieties of apple trees were in bloom, but the whole garden was drowning in smoke, and only near the nurseries did Kovrin breathe deeply.

     "I used to sneeze here from the smoke as a child," he said, shrugging his shoulders, "but I still don't understand how smoke can save from frost."

     "Smoke replaces clouds when there are none…" Tanya answered.

     "And what are clouds for?"

     "In cloudy and overcast weather there is no morning frost."

     "Is that so!"

     He laughed and took her by the hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with thin black eyebrows, the raised collar of her coat that prevented her from moving her head freely, and all of her, thin, slender, in a dress damp from the dew, touched him.

     "Lord, she's already grown up!" he said. "When I left here last time, five years ago, you were still quite a child. You were so skinny, long-legged, bareheaded, you wore a short little dress, and I teased you by calling you a heron… What time does!"

     "Yes, five years!" Tanya sighed. "Much water has flowed under the bridge since then. Tell me, Andryusha, in all conscience," she spoke animatedly, looking into his face, "have you grown unaccustomed to us? However, why am I asking? You're a man, you're already living your own interesting life, you're a somebody… Estrangement is so natural! But be that as it may, Andryusha, I want you to consider us your own. We have the right to that."

     "I do consider you so, Tanya."

     "Honest word?"

     "Yes, honest word."

     "You were surprised today that we have so many of your photographs. After all, you know, my father adores you. Sometimes it seems to me that he loves you more than me. He's proud of you. You're a scientist, an extraordinary person, you've made a brilliant career for yourself, and he's certain that you turned out this way because he raised you. I don't prevent him from thinking so. Let him.

     Dawn was already beginning, and this was especially noticeable from the distinctness with which the clouds of smoke and the crowns of the trees began to stand out in the air. Nightingales were singing, and from the fields came the cry of quails.

“But it’s time to sleep,” said Tanya. “And it’s cold, too.” She took him by the arm. “Thank you, Andryusha, for coming. Our acquaintances are uninteresting, and even those are few. We have only the garden, garden, garden—and nothing else. A standard trunk, a half-standard,” she laughed, “aport, ranet, borovinka, budding, grafting… Our whole, whole life has gone into the garden; I never even dream of anything but apple trees and pear trees. Of course, that’s good, useful, but sometimes you want something else for variety. I remember when you used to come to us during vacation or just like that, the house somehow became fresher and brighter, as if covers had been taken off the chandeliers and furniture. I was a girl then, and even so I understood.

     She spoke for a long time and with great feeling. For some reason it suddenly occurred to him that during the summer he might become attached to this small, weak, talkative creature, become infatuated and fall in love—in the situation of both of them it was so possible and natural! This thought touched and amused him; he bent down to the dear, anxious face and sang softly:

     Onegin, I will not conceal it,

     I love Tatyana madly…

     When they came home, Yegor Semyonych had already risen. Kovrin did not feel like sleeping; he struck up a conversation with the old man and went back to the garden with him. Yegor Semyonych was tall, broad-shouldered, with a large belly, and suffered from shortness of breath, but he always walked so quickly that it was hard to keep up with him. He looked extremely preoccupied, was always hurrying somewhere, and with such an expression, as if if he were even a minute late, everything would be ruined!

     “Well, brother, here’s a story…” he began, stopping to catch his breath. “On the surface of the earth, as you see, there’s frost, but lift the thermometer on a stick a couple of sazhens above the ground, and it’s warm there… Why is that so?”

     “Really, I don’t know,” said Kovrin and laughed.

     “Hmm… One can’t know everything, of course… However broad the mind may be, you can’t fit everything in there. You’re still mostly about philosophy?”

     “Yes. I read psychology, and in general I occupy myself with philosophy.”

     “And it doesn’t bore you?”

     “On the contrary, that’s the only thing I live by.”

     “Well, God grant…,” said Yegor Semyonych, thoughtfully stroking his gray side-whiskers. “God grant… I’m very glad for you… glad, brother…”

     But suddenly he listened and, making a terrible face, ran off to the side and soon disappeared behind the trees, in clouds of smoke.

     “Who tied a horse to the apple tree?” came his desperate, heart-rending shout. “What scoundrel and villain dared to tie a horse to the apple tree? My God, my God! They’ve ruined it, made it filthy, defiled it, messed it up! The garden is ruined! The garden is done for! My God!”

     When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted, insulted.

     “Well, what can you do with these accursed people?” he said in a tearful voice, spreading his hands. “Stepka was hauling manure at night and tied the horse to the apple tree! He wrapped the reins, the scoundrel, tight, so tight that the bark was rubbed in three places. How do you like that! I tell him, and he just stands there like a blockhead and blinks his eyes! He deserves to be hanged!”

     Having calmed down, he embraced Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

     “Well, God grant… God grant…” he muttered. “I’m very glad you came. Inexpressibly glad… Thank you.

     Then, with the same rapid stride and preoccupied face, he went around the whole garden and showed his former pupil all the conservatories, hothouses, ground sheds, and his two apiaries, which he called the wonder of our century.

     While they were walking, the sun rose and brightly illuminated the garden. It became warm. Foreseeing a clear, cheerful, long day, Kovrin remembered that this was still only the beginning of May and that ahead there was a whole summer, just as clear, cheerful, and long, and suddenly in his chest stirred a joyful young feeling, such as he had experienced in childhood when he ran about this garden. And he himself embraced the old man and tenderly kissed him. Both, moved, went into the house and began to drink tea from old porcelain cups, with cream, with rich, buttery pretzels—and these little things again reminded Kovrin of his childhood and youth. The beautiful present and the impressions of the past awakening in him merged together; from them his soul felt cramped, but good.

     He waited forWhen Tanya woke up, and together with her he drank coffee, went for a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read attentively, made notes, and occasionally raised his eyes to glance at the open windows or at the fresh flowers, still wet with dew, standing in vases on the table, and again lowered his eyes to the book, and it seemed to him that every little vein in him was trembling and playing with pleasure.

     

II

     

     In the country he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city. He read and wrote a great deal, studied Italian, and when he walked, he thought with pleasure that he would soon sit down to work again. He slept so little that everyone was amazed; if he accidentally fell asleep during the day for half an hour, he would then not sleep all night, and after a sleepless night, as if nothing had happened, he felt cheerful and merry.

     He talked a great deal, drank wine, and smoked expensive cigars. Young ladies#neighbors came to the Pesotskys often, almost every day, and they played the piano and sang together with Tanya; sometimes a young man, a neighbor, who played the violin well, came. Kovrin listened to the music and singing greedily and was exhausted by them, and the latter manifested itself physically in that his eyes would close and his head would droop to one side.

     One day after evening tea he was sitting on the balcony reading. In the drawing room at that time Tanya - soprano, one of the young ladies - contralto, and the young man on the violin were rehearsing a well-known serenade by Braga. Kovrin listened to the words - they were Russian - and could not understand their meaning. At last, abandoning the book and listening attentively, he understood: a young woman, sick with imagination, heard at night in the garden some mysterious sounds, so beautiful and strange that she had to recognize them as a sacred harmony which is incomprehensible to us mortals and therefore flies back to the heavens. Kovrin's eyes began to close. He got up and, exhausted, walked through the drawing room, then through the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya by the arm and went out with her onto the balcony.

     - A legend has been occupying me since this morning, - he said. - I do not remember whether I read it somewhere or heard it, but the legend is somehow strange, incongruous with anything. To begin with, it is not distinguished by clarity. A thousand years ago a certain monk, dressed in black, was walking through a desert, somewhere in Syria or Arabia... A few miles from the place where he was walking, fishermen saw another black monk, who was slowly moving over the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend apparently does not recognize, and listen further. From the mirage came another mirage, then from the other a third, so that the image of the black monk began to be transmitted endlessly from one layer of the atmosphere to another. He was seen now in Africa, now in Spain, now in India, now in the Far North... At last he went beyond the limits of the earth's atmosphere and now wanders throughout the universe, never managing to fall into the conditions under which he could fade away. Perhaps he is now being seen somewhere on Mars or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the very crux of the legend lies in the fact that exactly a thousand years after the monk walked through the desert, the mirage will again enter the earth's atmosphere and appear to people. And supposedly this thousand years is already drawing to a close... According to the meaning of the legend, we must expect the black monk not today but tomorrow.

     - A strange mirage, - said Tanya, who did not like the legend.

     - But the most amazing thing, - laughed Kovrin, - is that I simply cannot remember where this legend came into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Hear it? Or perhaps the black monk appeared to me in a dream? I swear to God, I do not remember. But the legend occupies me. I have been thinking about it all day today.

     Having let Tanya go back to the guests, he left the house and walked in thought around the flowerbeds. The sun was already setting. The flowers, because they had just been watered, gave off a moist, irritating scent. In the house they began singing again, and from a distance the violin produced the impression of a human voice. Kovrin, straining his mind to remember where he had heard or read the legend, walked unhurriedly into the park and imperceptibly reached the river.Along the path that ran along the steep bank past bare roots, he went down to the water, disturbed the sandpipers there, and startled two ducks. The gloomy pines here and there still reflected the last rays of the setting sun, but on the surface of the river it was already truly evening. Kovrin crossed to the other side on the footbridges. Before him now lay a wide field covered with young rye that had not yet bloomed. Neither human dwelling nor living soul in the distance, and it seems that the path, if you follow it, will lead to that very unknown, mysterious place where the sun had just set and where the evening glow blazes so broadly and majestically.

"How spacious, free, and quiet it is here!" thought Kovrin as he walked along the path. "And it seems the whole world is looking at me, has fallen silent, and is waiting for me to understand it..."

But then waves ran across the rye, and a light evening breeze gently touched his uncovered head. A minute later another gust of wind, but stronger now, and the rye rustled, and from behind came the muffled murmur of the pines. Kovrin stopped in amazement. On the horizon, like a whirlwind or a tornado, a tall black column rose from the earth to the sky. Its contours were unclear, but in the very first moment one could understand that it was not standing in place but moving with terrible speed, moving precisely here, straight toward Kovrin, and the closer it came, the smaller and clearer it became. Kovrin rushed to the side, into the rye, to let it pass, and barely managed to do so...

A monk in black clothing, with gray hair and black eyebrows, his arms crossed on his chest, flew past... His bare feet did not touch the ground. Having already flown about three sazhens past, he looked back at Kovrin, nodded his head, and smiled at him tenderly and at the same time slyly. But what a pale, terribly pale, thin face! Beginning to grow again, he flew across the river, struck the clay bank and the pines without a sound, and, passing through them, disappeared like smoke.

"Well, you see..." muttered Kovrin. "So the legend is true."

Not trying to explain to himself the strange phenomenon, satisfied with the mere fact that he had managed to see so closely and so clearly not only the black clothing but even the face and eyes of the monk, pleasantly agitated, he returned home.

In the park and in the garden people walked calmly, in the house they were playing, which meant that he alone had seen the monk. He badly wanted to tell Tanya and Yegor Semyonych about everything, but he realized that they would certainly take his words for delirium, and that would frighten them; better to keep silent. He laughed loudly, sang, danced the mazurka, he was cheerful, and everyone, the guests and Tanya, found that today his face was somehow special, radiant, inspired, and that he was very interesting.

     

III

     

After supper, when the guests had left, he went to his room and lay down on the sofa: he wanted to think about the monk. But a minute later Tanya came in.

"Here, Andryusha, read Father's articles," she said, handing him a stack of pamphlets and offprints. "Wonderful articles. He writes excellently."

"Well, and excellently indeed!" said Yegor Semyonych, coming in after her and laughing forcedly; he felt embarrassed. "Don't listen, please, don't read them! However, if you want to fall asleep, then by all means read them: an excellent sleeping aid."

"In my opinion, they are magnificent articles," said Tanya with deep conviction. "Read them, Andryusha, and persuade Papa to write more often. He could write a complete course on horticulture."

Yegor Semyonych laughed tensely, blushed, and began saying the phrases that embarrassed authors usually say. Finally, he began to give in.

"In that case, read Goshe's article first and these Russian little articles," he muttered, sorting through the pamphlets with trembling hands, "otherwise you won't understand. Before reading my objections, you need to know what I am objecting to. However, it's nonsense... boring stuff. And it seems it's time for bed."

Tanya left. Yegor Semyonych sat down next to Kovrin on the sofa and sighed deeply.

"Yes, my dear brother..." he began after some silence. "So it is, my most esteemed magister. So I write articles, and participate in exhibitions, and receive medals... They say Pesotsky's apples are as big as your head, and they say Pesotsky has made a fortune from his garden."In a word, Kochubey is rich and glorious. But one asks: what is all this for? The garden is indeed splendid, exemplary... It is not a garden but a whole institution of high state importance, because it is, so to speak, a step into a new era of Russian economy and Russian industry. But what for? What is the purpose?

     "The thing speaks for itself."

     "That is not what I mean. I want to ask: what will happen to the garden when I die? In the form in which you see it now, it will not last even a month without me. The whole secret of success is not that the garden is large and there are many workers, but that I love the work - do you understand? - love it, perhaps, more than myself. Look at me: I do everything myself. I work from morning till night. I do all the grafting myself, the pruning myself, the planting myself, everything myself. When people help me, I become jealous and irritated to the point of rudeness. The whole secret is in love, that is, in the keen eye of the owner, and in the owner's hands, and in that feeling when you go somewhere on a visit for an hour, you sit there, but your heart is not in the right place, you are not yourself: you are afraid that something might happen in the garden. And when I die, who will look after it? Who will work? The gardener? The workers? Yes? So this is what I will tell you, dear friend: the first enemy in our business is not the hare, not the cockchafer, and not the frost, but a stranger.

     "And Tanya?" asked Kovrin, laughing. "She cannot be more harmful than a hare. She loves and understands the work."

     "Yes, she loves and understands. If after my death the garden goes to her and she becomes the mistress, then, of course, one could not wish for anything better. Well, but what if, God forbid, she gets married?" whispered Yegor Semyonych and looked at Kovrin in fright. "That is just it! She will get married, there will be children, and then there will be no time to think about the garden.

     "What I fear most of all: she will marry some fine fellow, and he will get greedy and rent the garden out to traders, and everything will go to the devil in the very first year! In our business, women are a scourge from God!"

     Yegor Semyonych sighed and paused for a little while.

     "Maybe this is egoism, but I will speak frankly: I do not want Tanya to get married. I am afraid! There is a certain fop with a violin who comes to visit us and scrapes away; I know that Tanya will not marry him, I know it well, but I cannot bear to see him! In general, brother, I am rather a big odd fellow. I admit it.

     Yegor Semyonych got up and walked about the room in agitation, and it was evident that he wanted to say something very important, but could not bring himself to do it.

     "I love you warmly and will speak to you frankly," he decided at last, thrusting his hands into his pockets. "I treat certain delicate questions simply and say directly what I think, and I cannot stand so-called secret thoughts. I will speak directly: you are the only man to whom I would not be afraid to give my daughter. You are an intelligent man, with a heart, and you would not let my beloved work perish. And the main reason is that I love you like a son... and am proud of you. If you and Tanya were somehow to have a romance, then - well? I would be very glad and even happy. I say this directly, without affectation, as an honest man.

     Kovrin laughed. Yegor Semyonych opened the door to go out and stopped on the threshold.

     "If you and Tanya had a son, I would make a gardener out of him," he said, after thinking a little. "However, this is an empty dream... Good night.

     Left alone, Kovrin settled himself more comfortably and set to the articles. One had the title: "On Intermediate Cultivation," another: "A Few Words Concerning Mr. Z.'s Note on the Digging Over of the Soil for a New Garden," a third: "More on Budding with the Dormant Eye" - and all in the same vein. But what a restless, uneven tone, what a nervous, almost morbid fervor! Here is an article, it seems, with the most peaceful title and indifferent content: it speaks of the Russian Antonovka apple. But Yegor Semyonych begins it with "audiatur altera pars" 1 and ends with "sapienti sat" 2, and between these sayings there is a whole fountain of various venomous words directed at "the learned ignorance of our patent gentlemen gardeners, who observe nature from the height of their chairs," or at Mr. Goshe, "whose success was created by profanes and dilettantes," and right there, incongruously, a strained and insincere regret,that men who steal fruit and break trees in the process can no longer be flogged with birch rods.

     "A fine, sweet, healthy business, but even here there is passion and war," thought Kovrin. "It must be that everywhere and in every calling, people of ideas are nervous and distinguished by heightened sensitivity. Probably that is how it must be."

     He remembered Tanya, who so liked Egor Semenych's articles. Short, pale, thin, so that her collarbones showed; her eyes wide open, dark, intelligent, always peering somewhere and seeking something; her walk, like her father's, small and hurried. She talked a great deal, loved to argue, and with every even insignificant phrase she accompanied herself with expressive facial expressions and gestures. She must be extremely nervous.

     Kovrin began to read further, but understood nothing and threw it down. The pleasant excitement, the very one with which he had earlier danced the mazurka and listened to the music, now tormented him and summoned up a multitude of thoughts. He got up and began to walk about the room, thinking of the black monk. It occurred to him that if this strange, supernatural monk had been seen by him alone, then it meant that he was ill and had already reached the point of hallucinations. This consideration frightened him, but not for long.

     "But I feel fine, and I do no harm to anyone; so there is nothing bad in my hallucinations," he thought, and again he felt fine.

     He sat down on the sofa and clasped his head in his hands, restraining the incomprehensible joy that filled his whole being, then walked about again and sat down to work. But the thoughts that he read out of the book did not satisfy him. He wanted something gigantic, immense, staggering. Toward morning he undressed and reluctantly lay down in bed: he had to sleep!

     When the footsteps of Egor Semenych, who was leaving for the garden, were heard, Kovrin rang and ordered the servant to bring wine. He drank several glasses of Lafite with pleasure, then covered himself over his head; his consciousness grew foggy, and he fell asleep.

     

IV

     

     Egor Semenych and Tanya often quarreled and said unpleasant things to each other.

     One morning they had a spat about something. Tanya burst into tears and went to her room. She did not come out for dinner or for tea. Egor Semenych at first walked about important, puffed up, as if wishing to make it understood that for him the interests of justice and order were higher than anything in the world, but soon he could not hold out and lost heart. He wandered sadly about the park and kept sighing, "Ah, my God, my God!" and at dinner he did not eat a single crumb. Finally, guilty and tormented by conscience, he knocked at the locked door and called timidly:

     "Tanya! Tanya?"

     And in answer to him from behind the door came a weak voice, exhausted from tears and at the same time resolute:

     "Leave me, I beg you."

     The anguish of the hosts was reflected in the whole house, even in the people who worked in the garden. Kovrin was absorbed in his interesting work, but in the end even he became bored and uncomfortable. To somehow dispel the general bad mood, he decided to intervene and toward evening knocked at Tanya's door. He was let in.

     "Ai-ai, how shameful!" he began playfully, looking with surprise at Tanya's tear-stained, red-blotched, sorrowful face. "Can it really be so serious? Ai-ai!"

     "But if you knew how he torments me!" she said, and tears, burning, abundant tears, burst from her big eyes. "He has tormented me!" she continued, wringing her hands. "I said nothing to him... nothing... I only said that there was no need to keep... extra workers if... if one can have day laborers whenever one likes. After all... after all, the workers have done nothing for a whole week... I... I only said that, and he shouted and said to me... a lot of offensive, deeply insulting things. What for?"

     "Enough, enough," said Kovrin, adjusting her hair. "You quarreled, you cried, and that will do. You cannot stay angry for long, that is not good... especially since he loves you infinitely."

     "He has... he has ruined my whole life," continued Tanya, sobbing. "All I hear is insults and... and offenses. He considers me superfluous in his house. What of it? He is right. I will leave here tomorrow, become a telegraph operator... Let..." 

     "Well, well, well... No need to cry, Tanya. No need,"sweetie… You're both hot-tempered, once

Kovrin spoke gently and persuasively, while she kept crying, her shoulders twitching and her hands clasped, as though a terrible misfortune had truly befallen her. He felt all the more sorry for her because her grief was not serious, yet she suffered deeply. What trifles were enough to make this creature unhappy for a whole day, and perhaps for a lifetime! Consoling Tanya, Kovrin thought that, apart from this girl and her father, you could search the whole world with a lantern by day and not find people who loved him as their own, as kin; if it were not for these two people, then perhaps he, having lost his father and mother in early childhood, would never until his death have known what sincere affection and that naive, unreasoning love were, which people feel only for those very close to them, their own flesh and blood. And he felt that his half-sick, overwrought nerves, like iron to a magnet, responded to the nerves of this weeping, trembling girl. He could never have fallen in love with a healthy, sturdy, rosy-cheeked woman, but pale, weak, unhappy Tanya pleased him.

     And he willingly stroked her hair and shoulders, pressed her hands, and wiped her tears... At last she stopped crying. She complained for a long time still about her father and her hard, unbearable life in that house, imploring Kovrin to enter into her position; then she gradually began to smile and sigh that God had given her such a bad character, and in the end, laughing loudly, she called herself a fool and ran out of the room.

     When a little later Kovrin went out into the garden, Egor Semyonych and Tanya were already strolling side by side along the path as if nothing had happened, both eating rye bread with salt, since both were hungry.

     

V

     

     Pleased that he had succeeded so well in the role of peacemaker, Kovrin went into the park. Sitting on a bench and pondering, he heard the clatter of carriages and women's laughter – guests had arrived. When evening shadows began to fall in the garden, the sounds of a violin and singing voices were faintly heard, and this reminded him of the black monk. Where, in what country or on what planet was this optical incongruity now wandering?

     No sooner had he recalled the legend and pictured in his imagination that dark apparition he had seen in the rye field than from behind a pine tree, directly opposite, a man of medium height emerged silently, without the slightest rustle, bareheaded with grey hair, all in dark clothing and barefoot, resembling a beggar, and on his pale, as if dead, face his black eyebrows stood out sharply. Nodding his head amiably, this beggar or wanderer noiselessly approached the bench and sat down, and Kovrin recognized in him the black monk. For a minute the two looked at each other – Kovrin with amazement, and the monk gently and, as before, somewhat slyly, with an expression of knowing something to himself.

     "But you are a mirage," said Kovrin. "Why then are you here and sitting in one place? That does not fit with the legend."

     "It makes no difference," the monk answered, not at once, in a quiet voice, turning to face him. "The legend, the mirage, and I – all these are products of your excited imagination. I am a ghost."

     "So you do not exist?" asked Kovrin.

     "Think as you wish," said the monk and smiled faintly. "I exist in your imagination, and your imagination is part of nature, so I exist in nature as well."

     "You have a very old, wise, and highly expressive face, as though you had truly lived more than a thousand years," said Kovrin. "I did not know that my imagination was capable of creating such phenomena. But why are you looking at me with such delight? Do you like me?"

     "Yes. You are one of those few who are justly called God's chosen ones. You serve eternal truth. Your thoughts, intentions, your remarkable science, and your whole life bear a divine, heavenly seal, since they are dedicated to the rational and the beautiful, that is, to that which is eternal."

     "You said: eternal truth... But is eternal truth accessible and necessary to people if there is no eternal life?"

     "Eternal life exists," said the monk.

     "Do you believe in the immortality of people?"

     "Yes, of course. A great, brilliant future awaits you, people. And the more there are on earth like you, the sooner that future will be realized. Without you,"servants of a higher principle, living consciously and freely, humanity would be insignificant; developing in the natural order, it would have long waited for the end of its earthly history. But you will introduce it into the kingdom of eternal truth several thousand years earlier – and in this is your high merit. You embody the blessing of God, which has rested upon people.

     – And what is the goal of eternal life? – asked Kovrin.

     – The same as of all life – enjoyment. True enjoyment is in knowledge, and eternal life will present countless and inexhaustible sources for knowledge, and in this sense it is said: in my Father's house are many mansions.

     – If you knew how pleasant it is to listen to you! – said Kovrin, rubbing his hands with pleasure.

     – Very glad.

     – But I know: when you leave, I will be troubled by the question of your essence. You are a ghost, a hallucination. So, I am mentally ill, abnormal?

     – Even if so. Why be troubled? You are ill because you worked beyond your strength and are exhausted, and this means that you brought your health as a sacrifice to an idea, and the time is near when you will give your very life to it. What could be better? This is what all noble natures gifted from above strive for.

     – If I know that I am mentally ill, can I believe myself?

     – And why do you know that geniuses, whom the whole world believes, also did not see ghosts? Scientists now say that genius is akin to insanity. My friend, only ordinary, herd people are healthy and normal. Considerations about the nervous age, overwork, degeneration, etc. can seriously worry only those who see the goal of life in the present, that is, herd people.

     – The Romans said: mens sana in corpore sano 3.

     – Not everything that the Romans or Greeks said is true. Elevated mood, excitement, ecstasy – all that distinguishes prophets, poets, martyrs for an idea from ordinary people – is contrary to the animal side of man, that is, his physical health. I repeat: if you want to be healthy and normal, go to the herd.

     – Strange, you repeat what often comes to my own mind, – said Kovrin. – It is as if you have spied on and overheard my innermost thoughts. But let us not talk about me. What do you mean by eternal truth?

     The monk did not answer. Kovrin looked at him and could not make out his face: his features were clouding and blurring. Then the monk's head and hands began to disappear; his torso merged with the bench and with the evening twilight, and he vanished completely.

     – The hallucination is over! – said Kovrin and laughed. – A pity.

     He went back to the house cheerful and happy. The little that the black monk had said to him flattered not his vanity but his whole soul, his whole being. To be a chosen one, to serve eternal truth, to stand among those who several thousand years earlier will make humanity worthy of the kingdom of God, that is, will save people from several extra thousand years of struggle, sin, and suffering, to give everything to an idea – youth, strength, health, to be ready to die for the common good – what a lofty, what a happy lot! His past flashed through his memory, pure, chaste, full of labor; he remembered what he had studied and what he himself had taught others, and decided that there was no exaggeration in the monk's words.

     Tanya was walking toward him through the park. She was already wearing a different dress.

     – You are here? – she said. – And we have been looking and looking for you… But what is the matter with you? – she wondered, glancing at his rapturous, radiant face and at his eyes full of tears. – How strange you are, Andryusha.

     – I am content, Tanya, – said Kovrin, placing his hands on her shoulders. – I am more than content, I am happy! Tanya, dear Tanya, you are an extremely sympathetic being. Dear Tanya, I am so glad, so glad!

     He warmly kissed both her hands and continued:

     – I have just experienced bright, wonderful, unearthly moments. But I cannot tell you everything, because you will call me mad or will not believe me. Let us talk about you. Dear, glorious Tanya! I love you and have already grown accustomed to loving you. Your closeness, our meetings ten times a day have become a necessity of my soul. I do not know,How will I get along without you when I go back to my own place.

     "Well!" Tanya laughed. "You will forget about us in two days. We are little people, and you are a great man."

     "No, let us talk seriously!" he said. "I will take you with me, Tanya. Yes? Will you come with me? Do you want to be mine?"

     "Well!" said Tanya and wanted to laugh again, but the laugh did not come, and red patches appeared on her face.

     She began to breathe rapidly and walked quickly#quickly, but not toward the house, farther into the park.

     "I did not think about it... did not think!" she said, as if in despair clasping her hands.

     And Kovrin walked behind her and spoke with the same radiant, enraptured face:

     "I want a love that would capture me entirely, and that love only you, Tanya, can give me. I am happy! Happy!"

     She was stunned, bent over, shrank, and seemed to age ten years at once, while he found her beautiful and loudly expressed his delight:

     "How beautiful she is!"

     

VI

     

     Having learned from Kovrin that not only had the romance been settled, but that there would even be a wedding, Yegor Semyonych paced from corner to corner for a long time, trying to conceal his agitation. His hands began to tremble, his neck swelled and turned crimson, he ordered the racing droshky to be harnessed and drove off somewhere. Tanya, who had seen how he lashed the horse and how deeply, almost down to his ears, he had pulled his cap down, understood his state of mind, locked herself in her room, and cried all day.

     In the greenhouses the peaches and plums had already ripened; the packing and sending to Moscow of this delicate and fastidious cargo required much attention, labor, and fuss. Because the summer was very hot and dry, it was necessary to water every tree, which took much time and manpower, and caterpillars appeared in great numbers, which the workers and even Yegor Semyonych and Tanya, to Kovrin's great disgust, crushed directly with their fingers. On top of all that, it was already necessary to take autumn orders for fruit and trees and to carry on a large correspondence. And in the very busiest time, when it seemed no one had a free minute, the field work began, which took away more than half of the workers from the orchard; Yegor Semyonych, deeply tanned, exhausted, angry, galloped now to the orchard, now to the field and shouted that he was being torn to pieces and that he would put a bullet in his head.

     And on top of that there was the fuss over the dowry, to which the Pesotskys attached no small importance; from the clinking of scissors, the clatter of sewing machines, the fumes of irons, and the caprices of the dressmaker, a nervous, touchy lady, everyone in the house had their heads spinning. And as if on purpose, every day guests arrived who had to be entertained, fed, and even put up for the night. But all this penal servitude passed unnoticed, as in a fog. Tanya felt as though love and happiness had caught her unawares, although since the age of fourteen she had been sure for some reason that Kovrin would marry precisely her. She was amazed, bewildered, did not believe herself... Now such joy would surge over her that she wanted to fly up under the clouds and pray to God there, and then suddenly she would remember that in August she would have to part with her native nest and leave her father, or, God knows from where, the thought would come that she was insignificant, small, and unworthy of such a great man as Kovrin, and she would go to her room, lock herself in with a key, and weep bitterly for several hours. When there were guests, it would suddenly seem to her that Kovrin was extraordinarily handsome and that all the women were in love with him and envied her, and her soul would fill with delight and pride, as though she had conquered the whole world, but as soon as he smiled amiably at some young lady, she would already tremble with jealousy, go to her room, and again there were tears. These new sensations took complete possession of her; she helped her father mechanically and noticed neither the peaches, nor the caterpillars, nor the workers, nor how quickly time was passing.

     Almost the same thing was happening with Yegor Semyonych. He worked from morning till night, kept hurrying somewhere, lost his temper, became irritated, but all this was in some sort of magical half-sleep. There seemed to be two people in him already: one was the real Yegor Semyonych, who, listening to the gardener Ivan Karlovich reporting to him about the disorder, became indignant and in despair clutched his head, and the other,not the real one, but definitely half-drunk, who would suddenly interrupt a business conversation mid-sentence, touch the gardener on the shoulder, and begin to mutter:

     – Say what you will, but blood means a great deal. His mother was a remarkable, most noble, most intelligent woman. It was a delight to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel's. She painted beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang… Poor dear, may she rest in peace, she died of consumption.

     The not-real Egor Semyonych would sigh and, after a pause, continue:

     – When he was a boy and growing up with me, he had the same angelic face, clear and kind. His gaze, his movements, and his speech were gentle and refined, like his mother's. And his mind? He always astonished us with his mind. And indeed, not for nothing is he a master's graduate! Not for nothing! But wait, Ivan Karlich, what will he be in ten years! You won't be able to reach him!

     But at that point the real Egor Semyonych, catching himself, would make a terrible face, clutch his head, and shout:

     – Devils! They've ruined it, defiled it, made it vile! The garden is lost! The garden is destroyed!

     And Kovrin worked with the same diligence and did not notice the commotion. Love only poured oil on the fire. After each meeting with Tanya, happy and elated, he would go to his room and with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and declared his love to her, he would take up a book or his manuscript. What the black monk said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, the brilliant future of humanity, and so on, gave his work a special, extraordinary significance and filled his soul with pride, with a consciousness of his own loftiness. Once or twice a week, in the park or in the house, he met the black monk and conversed with him at length, but this did not frighten him; on the contrary, it delighted him, since he was already firmly convinced that such visions visit only chosen, outstanding people who have dedicated themselves to serving an idea.

     One day the monk appeared during dinner and sat in the dining room by the window. Kovrin was delighted and very deftly started a conversation with Egor Semyonych and Tanya about things that might be interesting to the monk; the black guest listened and nodded his head amiably, while Egor Semyonych and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, not suspecting that Kovrin was not talking to them but to his hallucination.

     The Dormition Fast arrived unnoticed, and soon after it the wedding day, which, at Egor Semyonych's insistence, was celebrated "with a bang," that is, with a senseless revelry that lasted two days. They ate and drank about three thousand worth, but from the bad hired music, the shrill toasts, and the servants' running about, from the noise and the crowding, they could not appreciate the taste of either the expensive wines or the marvelous hors d'oeuvres ordered from Moscow.

     

VII

     

     One of the long winter nights, Kovrin was lying in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, whose head ached in the evenings from being unaccustomed to living in the city, had long been asleep and occasionally in her delirium uttered some incoherent phrases.

     Three o'clock struck. Kovrin put out the candle and lay down; he lay for a long time with his eyes closed, but could not fall asleep because, as it seemed to him, the bedroom was very hot and Tanya was delirious. At half past four he lit the candle again, and at that moment he saw the black monk sitting in an armchair near the bed.

     – Hello, – said the monk, and after a brief pause asked: – What are you thinking about now?

     – About fame, – answered Kovrin. – In the French novel I was just reading, there is a young scholar who does foolish things and wastes away from longing for fame. That longing is incomprehensible to me.

     – Because you are intelligent. You regard fame indifferently, as a toy that does not amuse you.

     – Yes, that is true.

     – Renown does not smile upon you. What is flattering, or amusing, or instructive in the fact that your name will be carved on a tombstone and then time will erase that inscription along with the gilding? And fortunately, there are too many of you for weak human memory to retain your names.

     – Of course, – Kovrin agreed. – And why remember them? But let us talk about something else. For example, about happiness. What is happiness?When the clock struck five, he was sitting on the bed, his feet dangling onto the carpet, and he was speaking, addressing the monk:

– In ancient times one happy man eventually became frightened of his happiness – so great was it! – and, to appease the gods, he sacrificed to them his favorite ring. Do you know? And I, like Polycrates, am beginning to be a little worried by my happiness. It seems strange to me that from morning to night I experience only joy, it fills my whole being and drowns out all other feelings. I do not know what sadness, sorrow, or boredom is. Here I am not sleeping, I have insomnia, but I am not bored. I say this seriously: I am beginning to be perplexed.

– But why? – the monk was amazed. – Is joy a supernatural feeling? Should it not be the normal state of a human being? The higher a person is in mental and moral development, the freer they are, the more pleasure life gives them. Socrates, Diogenes, and Marcus Aurelius experienced joy, not sorrow. And the apostle says: rejoice always. So rejoice and be happy.

– But what if the gods become angry? – Kovrin joked and laughed. – If they take away my comfort and make me freeze and go hungry, that would hardly suit my taste.

Meanwhile Tanya woke up and looked at her husband with amazement and horror. He was speaking, addressing the armchair, gesturing and laughing: his eyes were shining and there was something strange in his laughter.

– Andryusha, who are you talking to? – she asked, grabbing his hand, which he had extended toward the monk. – Andryusha! Who?

– Eh? Who? – Kovrin became confused. – With him… Here he is sitting, – he said, pointing at the black monk.

– There is no one here… no one! Andryusha, you are ill!

Tanya embraced her husband and pressed herself close to him, as if protecting him from visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

– You are ill! – she sobbed, trembling all over. – Forgive me, dear, darling, but I noticed long ago that your soul was disturbed by something… You are mentally ill, Andryusha…

Her trembling was transmitted to him as well. He glanced once more at the armchair, which was now empty, suddenly felt weakness in his arms and legs, became frightened, and began to dress.

– It is nothing, Tanya, nothing… – he muttered, trembling. – Indeed, I am a little unwell… it is time to admit it.

– I noticed it long ago… and papa noticed it too, – she said, trying to hold back her sobs. – You talk to yourself, you smile somehow strangely… you do not sleep. O, my God, my God, save us! – she said in horror. – But do not be afraid, Andryusha, do not be afraid, for God's sake, do not be afraid…

She also began to dress. Only now, looking at her, did Kovrin understand the full danger of his situation, understand what the black monk and the conversations with him meant. It was now clear to him that he was insane.

Both, not knowing why themselves, dressed and went into the hall: she in front, he behind her. There, already awakened by the sobbing, stood Yegor Semyonych in his dressing gown with a candle in his hands, who was staying with them.

– Do not be afraid, Andryusha, – Tanya was saying, trembling as if in a fever, – do not be afraid… Papa, it will all pass… it will all pass…

Kovrin from agitation could not speak. He wanted to say to his father-in-law in a joking tone:

– Congratulate me, I think I have gone mad, – but he only moved his lips and smiled bitterly.

At nine o'clock in the morning they put his coat and fur coat on him, wrapped him in a shawl, and took him in a carriage to the doctor. He began to receive treatment.

VIII

Again summer came, and the doctor ordered him to go to the country. Kovrin had already recovered, had stopped seeing the black monk, and all that remained was for him to strengthen his physical strength. Living at his father-in-law's in the country, he drank a lot of milk, worked only two hours a day, did not drink wine, and did not smoke.

On the eve of Ilya's Day, a vigil service was held at the house in the evening. When the sexton handed the censer to the priest, the old enormous hall smelled exactly like a cemetery, and Kovrin felt bored. He went out into the garden. Not noticing the luxurious flowers, he strolled through the garden, sat on a bench, then walked through the park; reaching the river, he went down and stood there in thought, gazing at the water. The gloomy pines with mossy roots, which the previous year had seen him here so young, joyful, and vigorous, now did not whisper but stood motionless and mute, as if they did not recognize him. And indeed, his head was shaved, his long beautiful hair was gone, his gait was listless, and his face, compared with last summer, had grown plumper and paler.

     He crossed to the other bank on the footbridges. Where last year there had been rye, now cut oats lay in rows. The sun had already set, and on the horizon a broad red glow was blazing, foretelling windy weather for tomorrow. It was quiet. Peering in the direction where last year the black monk had first appeared, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes, until the evening glow began to fade…

     When he returned home, listless and dissatisfied, the vigil service was already over. Egor Semenych and Tanya were sitting on the steps of the terrace drinking tea. They had been talking about something, but when they saw Kovrin, they suddenly fell silent, and he concluded from their faces that they had been talking about him.

     "It seems it's time for you to drink your milk," Tanya said to her husband.

     "No, it's not time…" he replied, sitting down on the lowest step. "Drink it yourself. I don't want any."

     Tanya glanced anxiously at her father and said in a guilty voice:

     "You yourself notice that milk is good for you."

     "Yes, very good for me!" Kovrin grinned. "I congratulate you: since Friday I've gained another pound in weight." He pressed his head firmly with his hands and said with anguish: "Why, why did you treat me? Bromide preparations, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear over every sip, every step—all of this will in the end reduce me to idiocy. I was going mad, I had delusions of grandeur, but at least I was cheerful, vigorous, and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I've become more reasonable and solid, but I'm just like everyone else: I'm mediocre, life is boring to me… Oh, how cruelly you've treated me! I saw hallucinations, but whom did that harm? I ask: whom did that harm?"

     "God knows what you're saying!" Egor Semenych sighed. "It's even boring to listen to."

     "Then don't listen."

     The presence of people, especially Egor Semenych, now irritated Kovrin; he answered him dryly, coldly, and even rudely, and looked at him only with mockery and hatred, while Egor Semenych became embarrassed and coughed guiltily, though he felt no fault in himself. Not understanding why their dear, amiable relations had changed so sharply, Tanya pressed close to her father and anxiously peered into his eyes; she wanted to understand and could not, and it was clear to her only that relations were getting worse and worse with each day, that her father had aged greatly lately, and her husband had become irritable, capricious, faultfinding, and uninteresting. She could no longer laugh or sing, she ate nothing at dinner, she did not sleep for whole nights, expecting something terrible, and she was so exhausted that one day she lay in a faint from dinner until evening. During the vigil service it seemed to her that her father was crying, and now, when the three of them sat on the terrace, she made an effort not to think about it.

     "How happy Buddha and Mohammed or Shakespeare were that kind relatives and doctors did not treat them for ecstasy and inspiration!" said Kovrin. "If Mohammed had taken potassium bromide for his nerves, worked only two hours a day, and drunk milk, then as little would have remained of that remarkable man as of his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will in the end bring it about that humanity grows dull, mediocrity is considered genius, and civilization perishes. If you knew," said Kovrin with annoyance, "how grateful I am to you!"

     He felt intense irritation, and so as not to say too much, he quickly got up and went into the house. It was quiet,and through the open windows the scent of tobacco and yalappa wafted in from the garden. In the huge dark hall, moonlight lay in green patches on the floor and on the piano. Kovrin recalled the raptures of the past summer, when yalappa had smelled just the same and the moon had shone through the windows. To bring back the mood of the previous year, he quickly went to his study, lit a strong cigar, and ordered the servant to bring wine. But the cigar left a bitter and unpleasant taste in his mouth, and the wine did not taste as it had the year before. And what it means to lose a habit! From the cigar and two sips of wine his head went round and his heart began to pound, so that he had to take potassium bromide.

     Before going to bed, Tanya said to him:

     – Father adores you. You are angry with him about something, and it is killing him. Look: he is growing old not by days but by hours. I beg you, Andryusha, for God's sake, for the sake of your late father, for the sake of my peace, be kind to him!

     – I cannot and I do not want to.

     – But why? – asked Tanya, beginning to tremble all over. – Explain to me, why?

     – Because I do not like him, that is all, – Kovrin said carelessly and shrugged his shoulders, – but let us not talk about him: he is your father.

     – I cannot, I cannot understand! – said Tanya, pressing her temples and staring at one point. – Something incomprehensible, something terrible is happening in our house. You have changed, you are not like yourself… You, a clever, extraordinary man, get irritated over trifles, you involve yourself in petty squabbles… Such little things excite you that sometimes one is simply amazed and cannot believe it: is it really you? Well, well, do not be angry, do not be angry, – she continued, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. – You are clever, kind, noble. You will be fair to father. He is so kind!

     – He is not kind, but good-natured. Vaudeville uncles, like your father, with well-fed, good-natured faces, extraordinarily hospitable and eccentric, once moved me and made me laugh both in stories and in vaudevilles and in life, but now they disgust me. They are egoists to the marrow of their bones. What disgusts me most is their well-fedness and that gastric, purely bull-like or boar-like optimism.

     Tanya sat down on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

     – This is torture, – she said, and by her voice it was clear that she was already extremely exhausted and that it was hard for her to speak. – Since the very winter not a single peaceful minute… After all, this is terrible, my God! I am suffering…

     – Yes, of course, I am Herod, and you and your papa are the Egyptian infants. Of course!

     His face seemed to Tanya ugly and unpleasant. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. And even before she had noticed that something was already missing from his face, as though since he had cut his hair his face had changed too. She wanted to say something hurtful to him, but at once she caught herself in a hostile feeling, became frightened, and left the bedroom.

     

IX

     

     Kovrin received an independent chair. The introductory lecture was scheduled for the second of December, and an announcement about this was posted in the university corridor. But on the appointed day he notified the inspector of students by telegram that he would not give lectures because of illness.

     He was coughing up blood. He spat blood, but it happened twice a month that it flowed copiously, and then he became extremely weak and fell into a drowsy state. This illness did not particularly frighten him, since he knew that his late mother had lived with exactly the same illness for ten years, even more; and the doctors assured him that it was not dangerous, and advised only not to get upset, to lead a regular life, and to talk less.

     In January the lecture again did not take place for the same reason, and in February it was already too late to begin the course. It had to be postponed until the following year.

     He was no longer living with Tanya, but with another woman, who was two years older than he and looked after him like a child. His mood was peaceful, submissive: he willingly obeyed, and when Varvara Nikolaevna – that was the name of his companion – was getting ready to take him to the Crimea, he agreed, although he had a presentiment that nothing good would come of this trip.

     They arrived in Sevastopol in the evening and stopped at a hotel,to rest and go to Yalta tomorrow. The journey had tired them both. Varvara Nikolaevna drank her tea, lay down to sleep, and soon fell asleep. But Kovrin did not lie down. Even at home, an hour before leaving for the station, he had received a letter from Tanya and had not dared to open it, and now it lay in his side pocket, and the thought of it agitated him unpleasantly. Sincerely, in the depths of his soul, he now considered his marriage to Tanya a mistake, was glad that he had finally broken with her, and the memory of this woman, who had in the end turned into a walking living relic, in whom, it seemed, everything had already died except her large, intently gazing, intelligent eyes, the memory of her aroused in him only pity and annoyance with himself. The handwriting on the envelope reminded him how two years ago he had been unjust and cruel, how he had taken out on people who were guilty of nothing his spiritual emptiness, boredom, loneliness, and dissatisfaction with life. Incidentally, he also remembered how one day he had torn into small scraps his dissertation and all the articles written during his illness, and how he had thrown them out the window, and the scraps, flying in the wind, caught on trees and flowers; in every line he saw strange, utterly groundless pretensions, frivolous ardor, insolence, megalomania, and this produced on him such an impression as though he were reading a description of his own vices; but when the last notebook was torn up and flew out the window, for some reason he suddenly felt annoyed and bitter, he went to his wife and said many unpleasant things to her. My God, how he tormented her! Once, wishing to hurt her, he told her that her father had played an unattractive role in their romance, since he had asked him to marry her; Egor Semenych accidentally overheard this, ran into the room, and from despair could not utter a single word, and only shifted from one foot to the other and somehow strangely lowed, as though his tongue had been taken away, while Tanya, looking at her father, cried out in a heart-rending voice and fainted. It was ugly.

     All this came to mind at the sight of the familiar handwriting. Kovrin went out onto the balcony; the weather was quiet and warm, and it smelled of the sea. The wonderful bay reflected the moon and the lights and had a color for which it was difficult to find a name. It was a tender and soft combination of blue with green; in places the water resembled blue vitriol in color, and in places it seemed the moonlight had thickened and, instead of water, filled the bay, and on the whole what harmony of colors, what a peaceful, calm, and lofty mood!

     On the lower floor, beneath the balcony, the windows were probably open, because women's voices and laughter could be heard distinctly. Apparently, there was a party there.

     Kovrin made an effort over himself, opened the letter, and, going into his room, read:

     "My father died just now. I owe this to you, because you killed him. Our garden is perishing, strangers are already managing it, that is, the very thing that poor father feared so much is happening. I owe this to you too. I hate you with all my soul and wish that you would perish soon. Oh, how I suffer! My soul burns with unbearable pain… Curse you. I took you for an extraordinary person, for a genius, I fell in love with you, but you turned out to be insane…"

     Kovrin could not read further, tore up the letter, and threw it away. He was seized by uneasiness, resembling fear. Behind the screen Varvara Nikolaevna was sleeping, and one could hear how she breathed; from the lower floor came women's voices and laughter, but he had such a feeling as though in the whole hotel there was not a single living soul besides him. Because the unfortunate, grief-stricken Tanya in her letter cursed him and wished for his destruction, he felt eerie, and he glanced fleetingly at the door, as if afraid that the unknown force which in some two years had wrought so much destruction in his life and in the lives of those close to him might come into the room and dispose of him again.

     He already knew from experience that when the nerves run wild, the best remedy for them is work. He had to sit down at the table and force himself, no matter what, to concentrate on some one thought. He took from his red portfolio a notebook on which was sketched the outline of a small compilation work,invented by him in case Crimea should seem boring without something to do. He sat down at the table and occupied himself with this outline, and it seemed to him that his peaceful, submissive, indifferent mood was returning. The notebook with the outline even led him to reflect on the vanity of worldly things. He thought about how much life takes for the insignificant or quite ordinary goods it can give a person. For example, to obtain a professorship before forty, to be an ordinary professor, to expound in a sluggish, boring, heavy language ordinary and moreover borrowed thoughts – in a word, in order to attain the position of a mediocre scholar, he, Kovrin, had needed to study for fifteen years, work day and night, endure a severe mental illness, survive an unsuccessful marriage, and commit many various follies and injustices that it would be pleasant not to remember. Kovrin now clearly recognized that he was a mediocrity, and he willingly reconciled himself to it, since, in his opinion, every person should be content with what he is.

     The outline had quite calmed him, but the torn letter showed white on the floor and prevented him from concentrating. He got up from the table, picked up the scraps of the letter and threw them out the window, but a light wind blew from the sea, and the scraps scattered across the windowsill. Again he was overcome by uneasiness, resembling fear, and it began to seem that in the entire hotel there was not a single soul besides him… He went out onto the balcony. The bay, as if alive, gazed at him with a multitude of light blue, dark blue, turquoise, and fiery eyes and beckoned him to itself. Indeed, it was hot and stifling, and a swim would not have been amiss.

     Suddenly on the lower floor beneath the balcony a violin began to play, and two tender female voices sang. It was something familiar. The romance being sung below spoke of a certain girl, sick in her imagination, who heard mysterious sounds at night in the garden and decided that it was a sacred harmony, incomprehensible to us mortals… Kovrin's breath caught, and his heart contracted with sadness, and a wonderful, sweet joy, which he had long since forgotten, trembled in his breast.

     A black tall column, resembling a whirlwind or a waterspout, appeared on the far shore of the bay. It moved across the bay toward the hotel with terrible rapidity, becoming smaller and darker, and Kovrin barely had time to step aside to make way… A monk with an uncovered gray head and black eyebrows, barefoot, with his arms crossed on his chest, rushed past and stopped in the middle of the room.

     – Why did you not believe me? – he asked with reproach, looking affectionately at Kovrin. – If you had believed me then, that you are a genius, then these two years you would not have spent so sadly and poorly.

     Kovrin already believed that he was God's chosen one and a genius; he vividly recalled all his former conversations with the black monk and wanted to speak, but blood flowed from his throat straight onto his chest, and he, not knowing what to do, passed his hands over his chest, and his cuffs became wet with blood. He wanted to call Varvara Nikolaevna, who was sleeping behind the screen, made an effort, and said:

     – Tanya!

     He fell to the floor and, raising himself on his hands, again called:

     – Tanya!

     He called Tanya, called the large garden with luxurious flowers sprinkled with dew, called the park, the pines with shaggy roots, the rye field, his wonderful science, his youth, boldness, joy, called life, which had been so beautiful. He saw on the floor near his face a large pool of blood and could no longer utter a single word from weakness, but an inexpressible, boundless happiness filled his whole being. Below beneath the balcony they were playing a serenade, and the black monk whispered to him that he was a genius and that he was dying only because his weak human body had already lost its equilibrium and could no longer serve as a shell for a genius.

     When Varvara Nikolaevna woke up and came out from behind the screen, Kovrin was already dead, and on his face was frozen a blissful smile.

     Anton Pavlovich Chekhov 

     VOLODYA 

     On one of the summer Sundays, at about five o'clock in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old youth, unattractive, sickly, and timid,He was sitting in the gazebo at the Shumikhins' dacha and feeling bored. His cheerless thoughts flowed in three directions. First, tomorrow, on Monday, he would have to take a math exam; he knew that if tomorrow he failed to solve a written problem, he would be expelled, since he had been in the sixth grade for two years and had an annual grade in algebra of 2 3/4. Second, his stay with the Shumikhins, wealthy people who laid claim to aristocracy, caused constant pain to his self-esteem. It seemed to him that Mme Shumikhina and her nieces looked upon him and his maman as poor relatives and hangers-on, that they did not respect maman and laughed at her. Once he had accidentally overheard Mme Shumikhina saying on the terrace to her cousin Anna Fyodorovna that his maman still kept trying to look young and beautify herself, that she never paid her losses and had a weakness for other people's boots and other people's tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to go to the Shumikhins', described to her what an humiliating role she played among these gentry, persuaded her, said rude things, but she, frivolous, spoiled, having squandered two fortunes in her lifetime - her own and her husband's, always gravitating toward high society, did not understand him, and Volodya had to escort her to the hateful dacha two or three times a week. 

     Third, the young man could not for a moment shake off a strange, unpleasant feeling that was completely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with the Shumikhins' cousin and guest, Anna Fyodorovna. She was a lively, loud-voiced, and giggly little lady, about thirty, healthy, sturdy, rosy, with round shoulders, a round plump chin, and a constant smile on her thin lips. She was not pretty and not young - Volodya knew that perfectly well, but for some reason he was powerless not to think about her, not to look at her when, playing croquet, she shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back, or after prolonged laughter and running up and down the stairs she would fall into an armchair and, closing her eyes, breathing heavily, pretend that her chest felt tight and stifling. She was married. Her husband, a solid architect, came to the dacha once a week, got a splendid night's sleep, and went back to the city. The strange feeling had begun in Volodya with the fact that he had for no reason come to hate this architect and rejoiced every time the man left for the city. 

     Now, sitting in the gazebo and thinking about tomorrow's exam and about maman, at whom people laughed, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (that was what the Shumikhins called Anna Fyodorovna), to hear her laughter, the rustle of her dress... This desire was not like the pure, poetic love that he knew from novels and of which he dreamed every evening as he went to bed; it was strange, incomprehensible, he was ashamed of it and afraid of it as of something very bad and impure, which was hard to admit to himself... 

     "It is not love," he told himself. "One does not fall in love with thirty-year-old married women... It is simply a little intrigue... Yes, an intrigue..." 

     Thinking about the intrigue, he recalled his invincible shyness, his lack of a mustache, his freckles, his narrow eyes, and placed himself in imagination beside Nyuta - and this pair seemed impossible to him; then he hastened to imagine himself handsome, bold, witty, dressed in the very latest fashion... 

     In the very midst of his daydreams, when he, hunched over and looking at the ground, was sitting in the dark corner of the gazebo, light footsteps were heard. Someone was walking unhurriedly along the path. Soon the footsteps fell silent, and something white flashed at the entrance. 

     "Is anyone here?" asked a woman's voice. 

     Volodya recognized this voice and raised his head in fright. 

     "Who is there?" asked Nyuta, entering the gazebo. "Ah, it is you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? And how can one keep thinking, thinking, thinking... that way one can go out of one's mind!" 

     Volodya got up and looked at Nyuta in confusion. She had just returned from the bathing house. On her shoulders hung a sheet and a fuzzy towel, and from under the white silk kerchief on her head peeped wet hair stuck to her forehead. She gave off the damp, cool smell of the bathing house and of almond soap. She was out of breath from walking quickly.The top button of her blouse was undone, so the young

Volodya listened and, in heavy, tense thought, scratched his temple. 

     "Only very proud people keep silent and love solitude," continued Nyuta, pulling his hand away from his temple. "You are a proud man, Volodya. Why do you look at me from under your brows? Kindly look me straight in the face! Come on now, you seal!" 

     Volodya decided to speak. Wanting to smile, he twitched his lower lip, blinked his eyes, and again reached for his temple. 

     "I... I love you!" he said. 

     Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed. 

     "What do I hear?!" she sang, as opera singers sing when they hear something terrible. "What? What did you say? Repeat it, repeat it..." 

     "I... I love you!" repeated Volodya. 

     And already without any participation of his own will, understanding and realizing nothing, he took half a step toward Nyuta and took her by the arm above the wrist. His eyes went dark and tears came, the whole world turned into one big, furry towel that smelled of the bathing house. 

     "Bravo, bravo!" he heard cheerful laughter. "Why are you silent? I want you to talk! Well?" 

     Seeing that no one was stopping him from holding her hand, Volodya glanced at Nyuta's laughing face and awkwardly, uncomfortably took her by the waist with both hands, his hands meeting on her back. He held her by the waist with both hands, while she, putting her arms behind her head and showing dimples at her elbows, adjusted her hair under her kerchief and said in a calm voice: 

     "You must be clever, Volodya, amiable, charming, and that can be achieved only under the influence of women's society. However, what an unpleasant... wicked face you have. You must talk, laugh... Yes, Volodya, do not be a grump, you are young and you will still have time to philosophize. Now, let me go, I am leaving. Let me go!" 

     She freed her waist without effort and, humming something, left the gazebo. Volodya remained alone. He smoothed his hair, smiled, and paced from corner to corner about three times, then sat down on the bench and smiled once more. He was unbearably ashamed, so much so that he was even surprised that human shame could reach such sharpness and force. Out of shame he smiled, whispered some incoherent words, and gestured. 

     He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and above all ashamed that he had dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist, although neither by age, nor by his outward qualities, nor by social position did he, as it seemed to him, have any right to do so. 

     He jumped up, left the gazebo, and without looking back went deep into the garden, farther from the house. 

     "Ah, if only I could leave here sooner! he thought, clutching his head. God, sooner!" 

     The train on which Volodya was supposed to travel with maman was leaving at eight forty. There were about three hours left before the train, but he would have gladly gone to the station right now, without waiting for maman. 

     Toward eight o'clock he was approaching the house. His whole figure expressed determination: come what may! He resolved to enter boldly, look straight ahead, speak loudly, no matter what. 

     He passed the terrace, the large hall, the drawing room, and stopped in the latter to catch his breath. From here he could hear tea being drunk in the adjoining dining room. Madame Shumikhina, maman, and Nyuta were talking about something and laughing. 

     Volodya listened. 

     "I assure you!" said Nyuta. "I could not believe my own eyes! When he began declaring his love to me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I did not recognize him. And you know, he has a manner! When he said he was in love with me, there was something savage in his face, like a Circassian." 

     "Really!" gasped maman, dissolving into a drawn-out laugh. "Really! How he reminds me of his father!" 

     Volodya ran back and rushed out into the fresh air. "And how can they talk about it aloud! he agonized, throwing up his hands and looking at the sky in horror. They talk aloud, cold-bloodedly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?" 

     But he had to go into the house, no matter what. He walked along the alley about three times, calmed down a little, and entered the house.- Why don't you come to drink tea on time? - m-me Shumikhina asked sternly. 

     - I'm sorry, I... I have to go, - he muttered, without raising his eyes. Maman, it's already eight o'clock! 

     - Go by yourself, my dear, - maman said languidly, - I'm staying the night at Lili's. Goodbye, my friend... Let me cross you... 

     She crossed her son and said in French, turning to Nyuta: 

     - He looks a little like Lermontov... Doesn't he? 

     Having somehow said goodbye and without looking at anyone's face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later he was already walking along the road to the station and was glad of it. Now he was neither frightened nor ashamed, he breathed easily and freely. 

     Half a verst from the station he sat down on a stone by the road and began to look at the sun, which was more than half hidden behind the embankment. At the station lights had already been lit here and there, one dim green light flickered, but the train was not yet visible. Volodya found it pleasant to sit, not move, and listen to how evening was gradually coming on. The twilight of the gazebo, the steps, the smell of the bathing shed, the laughter and the waist - all this appeared in his imagination with astonishing clarity, and all this was no longer as frightening and significant as before... 

     "Nonsense... She didn't pull her hand away and laughed when I held her by the waist, - he thought, - so she liked it. If it had been repulsive to her, she would have gotten angry..." 

     And now Volodya felt annoyed that there, in the gazebo, he had not been bold enough. He felt sorry that he was leaving so stupidly, and he was already sure that if that occasion repeated itself, he would be bolder and would look at things more simply. 

     And for the occasion to repeat itself was not difficult. At the Shumikhins' after supper they stroll for a long time. If Volodya went for a walk with Nyuta in the dark garden, then - there was the occasion! 

     "I'll return, - he thought, - and leave tomorrow with the morning train... I'll say I missed the train." 

     And he returned... M-me Shumikhina, maman, Nyuta and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace and playing vint. When Volodya lied to them that he had missed the train, they became worried that he might miss the exam tomorrow, and advised him to get up early. The whole time they played cards, he sat apart, greedily glancing at Nyuta and waiting... In his head a plan was already ready: he would go up to Nyuta in the darkness, take her by the hand, then embrace her; there was no need to say anything, since everything would be clear to both without words. 

     But after supper the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and continued playing cards. They played until one o'clock in the morning and then went to bed. 

     "How stupid this all is! - Volodya fretted, getting into bed. - But never mind, I'll wait for tomorrow... Tomorrow again in the gazebo. Nothing..." 

     He did not try to fall asleep, but sat in bed, clasping his knees with his arms, and thought. The thought of the exam was repulsive to him. He had already decided that he would be expelled and that there was nothing terrible in this expulsion. On the contrary, everything was very good, even very. Tomorrow he would be free as a bird, would put on civilian clothes, would smoke openly, come here and court Nyuta whenever he liked; and he would no longer be a schoolboy, but a "young man." And the rest, what is called a career and the future, was so clear: Volodya would join as a volunteer, become a telegraph operator, finally, an apothecary, where he would rise to the rank of pharmacist... were there few positions? An hour or two passed, and he still sat and thought... 

     At about three o'clock, when it was already getting light, the door creaked cautiously and maman entered the room. 

     - You're not asleep? - she asked, yawning. - Sleep, sleep, I'm just here for a minute... I'll just take the drops... 

     - Why do you need them? 

     - Poor Lili has spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow... 

     She took a vial of something from the little cupboard, went to the window, read the label and left. 

     - Marya Leontyevna, those are not the right drops! - Volodya heard a woman's voice a minute later. - Those are lily of the valley, but Lili is asking for morphine. Is your son asleep? Ask him to find it... 

     It was Nyuta's voice. Volodya went cold. He quickly put on his trousers, threw his coat over his shoulders and went to the door. 

     - Do you understand? Morphine! - Nyuta explained in a whisper. - It should be written there in Latin.Wake up Volodya, he'll find it...

     Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was wearing the same blouse she wore when she went swimming. Her hair was uncombed, scattered over her shoulders, her face sleepy, dark from the twilight...

     "Look, Volodya isn't asleep..." she said. "Volodya, look for morphine in the cupboard, dear! A punishment with this Lily... She always has something going on."

     Maman mumbled something, yawned, and left.

     "Go on, look," said Nyuta. "What are you standing there for?"

     Volodya went to the little cupboard, squatted down on his knees, and began sorting through the vials and boxes of medicines. His hands were trembling, and in his chest and stomach there was a feeling as if cold waves were running through all his insides. From the smell of ether, carbolic acid, and various herbs, which he grabbed at with trembling hands without any need and which scattered from this, he felt suffocated and dizzy.

     "It seems maman has left," he thought. "That's good... good..."

     "Soon?" Nyuta asked drawlingly.

     "Right now... This one, I think, is morphine..." said Volodya, having read the word "morph..." on one of the labels. "Here you are!"

     Nyuta stood in the doorway in such a way that one of her legs was in the corridor and the other in his room. She was fixing her hair, which was hard to fix, it was so thick and long! - and she looked absentmindedly at Volodya. In the loose blouse, sleepy, with her hair down, in that scant light that came into the room from the white but not yet sunlit sky, she seemed to Volodya enchanting, magnificent... Enchanted, trembling all over and with delight recalling how he had embraced that wonderful body in the gazebo, he handed her the drops and said:

     "How you..."

     "What?"

     She entered the room.

     "What?" she asked, smiling.

     He was silent and looked at her, then, as then in the gazebo, took her by the hand... And she looked at him, smiled, and waited: what would happen next?

     "I love you..." he whispered.

     She stopped smiling, thought, and said:

     "Wait, I think someone is coming. Oh, these schoolboys! she said in a low voice, going to the door and peeking into the corridor. "No, no one is visible..."

     She returned...

     Then it seemed to Volodya that the room, Nyuta, the dawn, and he himself - everything merged into one sensation of sharp, extraordinary, unprecedented happiness, for which one could give one's whole life and go to eternal torment, but half a minute passed, and all of it suddenly disappeared. Volodya saw only one full, unattractive face, distorted by an expression of disgust, and he himself suddenly felt revulsion at what had happened.

     "However, I need to leave," said Nyuta, looking at Volodya with disgust. "What an ugly, pathetic... ugh, ugly duckling!"

     How now Volodya found her long hair, loose blouse, her footsteps, her voice repulsive!..

     "Ugly duckling..." he thought after she left. "Indeed I am ugly... Everything is ugly."

     Outside the sun was already rising, birds were singing loudly; one could hear the gardener walking in the garden and his wheelbarrow creaking... And a little later came the mooing of cows and the sounds of a shepherd's pipe. The sunlight and the sounds said that somewhere in this world there is a life pure, graceful, poetic. But where was it? Neither maman nor all those people who surrounded him ever spoke to Volodya about it.

     When the footman woke him for the morning train, he pretended to be asleep...

     "To hell with it all!" he thought.

     He got out of bed at about eleven o'clock. Combing his hair before the mirror and looking at his unattractive, pale face from the sleepless night, he thought:

     "Absolutely right... Ugly duckling."

     When maman saw him and was horrified that he was not at the exam, Volodya said:

     "I overslept, maman... But don't worry, I'll present a medical certificate."

     Mme Shumikhina and Nyuta woke up at about one o'clock. Volodya heard how the awakened Mme Shumikhina opened her window with a clatter, and how Nyuta answered her coarse voice with rolling laughter. He saw,how the door opened and a string of nieces and dependents (maman was among the latter) stretched from the drawing room toward breakfast, how Nyuta's washed, laughing face flashed by, and next to her face the black eyebrows and beard of the architect who had just arrived.

     Nyuta was wearing a Little Russian costume, which did not suit her at all and made her awkward; the architect made cheap, flat jokes; the cutlets served at breakfast had a great deal of onion in them - so it seemed to Volodya. It also seemed to him that Nyuta laughed loudly on purpose and kept glancing in his direction, to let him understand that the memory of the night did not trouble her in the least and that she did not notice the presence at the table of the ugly duckling.

     At four o'clock Volodya was driving with maman to the station. The dirty memories, the sleepless night, the impending expulsion from the gymnasium, the pangs of conscience - all this now aroused in him a heavy, gloomy spite. He looked at maman's thin profile, at her little nose, at the waterproof coat Nyuta had given her, and muttered:

     - Why do you powder? It does not become you at your age! You make yourself look pretty, you do not pay your losses, you smoke other people's tobacco... disgusting! I do not love you... I do not love you!

     He insulted her, and she frightenedly rolled her little eyes, threw up her little hands, and whispered in horror:

     - What is it, my friend? My God, the coachman will hear! Be quiet, or the coachman will hear! He hears everything!

     - I do not love you... I do not love you! - he continued, gasping. - You are immoral, soulless... Do not dare wear that waterproof coat! Do you hear? Or else I will tear it to pieces...

     - Come to your senses, my child! - maman wept. - The coachman will hear!

     - And where is my father's fortune? Where is your money? You have squandered it all! I am not ashamed of my poverty, but I am ashamed that I have such a mother... When my comrades ask about you, I always blush.

     They had to ride two stations by train to the city. The whole time Volodya stood on the platform and trembled all over. He did not want to go into the carriage, since his mother was sitting there, and he hated her. He hated himself, the conductors, the smoke from the locomotive, the cold, to which he attributed his trembling... And the heavier it became in his soul, the more strongly he felt that somewhere in this world, among certain people, there was a life pure, noble, warm, refined, full of love, caresses, merriment, and freedom... He felt this and yearned so intensely that even one passenger, peering intently into his face, asked:

     - You probably have a toothache?

     In the city maman and Volodya lived at Marya Petrovna's, a lady of noble birth who rented a large apartment and sublet it to lodgers. Maman rented two rooms: in one, with windows, where her bed stood and two pictures in gold frames hung on the walls, she lived herself, and in the other, adjoining, small and dark, lived Volodya. Here stood a sofa on which he slept, and besides this sofa there was no other furniture; the whole room was occupied by woven baskets with clothes, hat boxes, and all sorts of junk that maman kept for some reason. Volodya prepared his lessons in his mother's room or in the "common room" - that was what the large room was called, where all the lodgers gathered during dinner and in the evenings.

     Returning home, he lay down on the sofa and covered himself with a blanket to stop the trembling. The hat boxes, the baskets, and the junk reminded him that he had no room of his own, no refuge where he could hide from maman, from her guests, and from the voices that now came from the "common room"; the satchel and books scattered in the corners reminded him of the examination he had not attended... For some reason quite inopportunely he recalled Menton, where he had lived with his late father when he was seven; he remembered Biarritz and two English girls with whom he had run on the sand... He wanted to revive in his memory the color of the sky and the ocean, the height of the waves, and his mood then, but he could not manage it; the English girls flashed through his imagination as if alive, while everything else blended together and blurred chaotically...

     "No, it is cold here," thought Volodya, got up, put on his overcoat, and went to the "common room."

     In the "common room" they were drinking tea.Three people were sitting around the samovar: maman, a music teacher, an old lady in tortoiseshell pince-nez, and Avgustin Mikhaylych, an elderly, very fat Frenchman who worked at a perfume factory. 

     "I haven't had dinner today," said maman. "We ought to send the maid for bread." 

     "Dunyash!" shouted the Frenchman. 

     It turned out that the mistress had sent the maid somewhere. 

     "Oh, that doesn't matter," said the Frenchman, smiling broadly. "I'll go for the bread myself. Oh, it's nothing!" 

     He put his strong, stinking cigar in a prominent place, put on his hat, and went out. After he left, maman began telling the music teacher about how she had stayed with the Shumikhins and how well she had been received there. 

     "After all, Lili Shumikhina is my relative..." she said. "Her late husband, General Shumikhin, is a cousin of my husband. And she herself is a Kolb by birth, a baroness..." 

     "Maman, that's not true!" said Volodya irritably. "Why lie?" 

     He knew perfectly well that maman was telling the truth; in her story about General Shumikhin and the Baroness Kolb by birth there was not a single word of lies, but nevertheless he still felt that she was lying. The lie was felt in her manner of speaking, in the expression of her face, in her gaze, in everything. 

     "You're lying!" Volodya repeated and struck the table with his fist so hard that all the dishes trembled and maman's tea spilled. "Why do you talk about generals and baronesses? It's all lies!" 

     The music teacher was flustered and coughed into her handkerchief, pretending she had choked, and maman began to cry. 

     "Where can I go?" thought Volodya. 

     He was already outside; he was ashamed to go to his friends. Again, inopportunely, he remembered the two English girls... He paced from corner to corner in the "common room" and went into Avgustin Mikhaylych's room. There was a strong smell of essential oils and glycerin soap. On the table, on the windowsills, and even on the chairs stood numerous flacons, little glasses, and shot glasses with liquids of various colors. Volodya took a newspaper from the table, unfolded it, and read the title: "Figaro"... The newspaper gave off some strong and pleasant smell. Then he took a revolver from the table... 

     "Come now, don't pay any attention!" the music teacher comforted maman in the next room. "He's still so young! At his age young people always allow themselves too much. One must put up with it." 

     "No, Yevgeniya Andreyevna, he is too spoiled!" maman said in a singsong voice. "There is no elder over him, and I am weak and can do nothing. No, I am unhappy!" 

     Volodya put the barrel of the revolver into his mouth, felt for something resembling a trigger or a hammer, and pressed with his finger... Then he felt for another protrusion and pressed again. Taking the barrel out of his mouth, he wiped it on the hem of his overcoat and examined the lock; he had never in his life held a weapon in his hands before... 

     "It seems this needs to be raised..." he reasoned. "Yes, it seems..." 

     Avgustin Mikhaylych came into the "common room" and, laughing, began telling about something. Volodya again put the barrel into his mouth, clenched it with his teeth, and pressed something with his finger. A shot rang out... Something struck Volodya on the back of the head with terrible force, and he fell onto the table, his face right into the shot glasses and flacons. Then he saw how his late father in a top hat with a wide black band, who had worn mourning in Menton for some lady, suddenly seized him with both arms and the two of them flew into some very dark, deep abyss. 

     Then everything became confused and vanished... 

     

Ionych

     

I

When in the provincial town of S. visitors complained of boredom and the monotony of life, the local residents, as if justifying themselves, would say that, on the contrary, in S. it was very good, that in S. there was a library, a theater, a club, there were balls, and that, finally, there were intelligent, interesting, agreeable families with whom one could become acquainted. And they pointed to the Turkin family as the most educated and talented.

     This family lived on the main street, near the governor, in their own house. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a stout, handsome dark-haired man with side-whiskers, organized amateur performances for charitable purposes, himself played old generals and coughed very amusingly while doing so. He knew many anecdotes, charades, sayings, loved to joke and make witticisms, and always had such an expression that one could not tell whether he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a thin, comely lady in pince-nez, wrote stories and novels and willingly read them aloud to her guests. The daughter, Ekaterina Ivanovna, a young girl, played the piano. In a word, each member of the family had some talent of their own. The Turkins received guests cordially and displayed their talents to them cheerfully, with heartfelt simplicity. In their large stone house it was spacious and cool in summer, half the windows looked out onto an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests sat in the house, knives clattered in the kitchen, the yard smelled of fried onion - and this each time foretold an abundant and tasty supper.

     And to Doctor Startsev, Dmitry Ionych, when he had just been appointed a zemstvo doctor and had settled in Dyalizh, nine versts from S., they also said that he, as an intelligent man, must become acquainted with the Turkins. One winter on the street he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich; they talked about the weather, the theater, cholera, and an invitation followed. In spring, on a holiday - it was Ascension - after receiving patients, Startsev set off for the town to amuse himself a little and incidentally buy himself a few things. He walked on foot, unhurriedly (he did not yet have his own horses), and all the while hummed: When I have not yet drunk tears from the cup of existence...

     

     In town he had dinner, strolled in the garden, then somehow of itself the invitation of Ivan Petrovich came to his mind, and he decided to go to the Turkins, to see what sort of people they were.

     - Hello, please - said Ivan Petrovich, meeting him on the porch. - Very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Let us go, I will introduce you to my better half. I say to him, Verochka - he continued, presenting the doctor to his wife - I say to him that he has no Roman right whatsoever to sit in his hospital, he must give his leisure to society. Is that not true, darling?

     - Sit here - said Vera Iosifovna, seating the guest beside her. - You may court me. My husband is jealous, this is an Othello, but we will try to behave so that he notices nothing.

     - Ah you, chick, spoiler... - Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. - You have come very opportunely - he turned again to the guest - my better half has written a most enormous novel and today will read it aloud.

     - Zhanchik - said Vera Iosifovna to her husband - dites que l'on nous donne du the.

     Startsev was introduced to Ekaterina Ivanovna, an eighteen-year-old girl, very much like her mother, just as thin and comely. Her expression was still childlike and her waist thin, delicate; and her virginal, already developed bosom, beautiful, healthy, spoke of spring, real spring. Then they drank tea with jam, with honey, with candies and with very tasty cookies that melted in the mouth. As evening came, guests gradually arrived, and to each of them Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes and said:

     - Hello, please.

     Then everyone sat in the drawing room, with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began thus: "The frost was growing stronger..." The windows were open wide, one could hear how knives clattered in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onion drifted in... In the soft, deep armchairs it was peaceful, the lights flickered so caressingly in the twilight of the drawing room; and now, on a summer evening,When voices and laughter drifted in from the street and there was a smell of lilac from the yard, it was hard to understand how the frost was growing fiercer and how the setting sun was lighting with its cold rays the snowy plain and the traveler walking alone along the road; Vera Iosifovna read about how a young, beautiful countess set up schools, hospitals, and libraries in her village and how she fell in love with a wandering artist - she read about things that never happen in life, and yet it was pleasant and comfortable to listen, and all such good, peaceful thoughts came into one's head - one did not want to get up.

     "Not bad..." Ivan Petrovich said quietly.

     And one of the guests, listening and carried in his thoughts somewhere very, very far away, said barely audibly:

     "Yes... indeed..."

     An hour passed, then another. In the town garden nearby an orchestra was playing and a choir of singers was singing. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, everyone was silent for about five minutes and listened to "Luchinushka," which the choir was singing, and this song conveyed what was not in the novel and what does happen in life.

     "Do you publish your works in journals?" Startsev asked Vera Iosifovna.

     "No," she answered, "I don't publish anywhere. I write it and hide it in my cupboard. Why publish?" she explained. "After all, we have means."

     And for some reason everyone sighed.

     "And now you, Kotik, play something," Ivan Petrovich said to his daughter.

     They raised the lid of the piano and opened the sheet music that was already lying in readiness. Ekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands; and then immediately struck again with all her might, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest shuddered, she stubbornly struck all in one place, and it seemed she would not stop until she had driven the keys into the inside of the piano. The drawing room filled with thunder; everything resounded: the floor, the ceiling, the furniture... Ekaterina Ivanovna was playing a difficult passage, interesting precisely because of its difficulty, long and monotonous, and Startsev, listening, pictured to himself how stones come tumbling down from a high mountain, tumbling and ever tumbling, and he wanted them to stop tumbling sooner, and at the same time Ekaterina Ivanovna, pink from exertion, strong, energetic, with a curl fallen on her forehead, was very pleasing to him. After the winter spent in Dializh, among patients and peasants, to sit in a drawing room, to look at this young, graceful, and probably pure being, and to listen to these loud, tiresome, but still cultured sounds - was so pleasant, so new...

     "Well, Kotik, today you played as never before," Ivan Petrovich said with tears in his eyes when his daughter finished and stood up. "Die, Denis, you couldn't write anything better."

     Everyone surrounded her, congratulated her, marveled, assured her that they had not heard such music in a long time, and she listened in silence, slightly smiling, and triumph was written all over her figure.

     "Splendid! superb!"

     "Splendid!" Startsev said too, yielding to the general enthusiasm. "Where did you study music?" he asked Ekaterina Ivanovna. "At the conservatory?"

     "No, I am only just planning to go to the conservatory, but for now I studied here, with Madame Zavlovskaya."

     "Did you finish your course at the local gymnasium?"

     "Oh no!" Vera Iosifovna answered for her. "We invited tutors to the house; at the gymnasium or at a boarding school, you must agree, there could be bad influences; while a girl is growing up, she must be under the influence of her mother alone."

     "And yet I will go to the conservatory," said Ekaterina Ivanovna.

     "No, Kotik loves her mama. Kotik will not upset papa and mama."

     "No, I will go! I will go!" said Ekaterina Ivanovna, joking and being capricious, and she stamped her little foot.

     And at supper Ivan Petrovich was already showing off his talents. Laughing with only his eyes, he told anecdotes, made witticisms, proposed funny riddles and solved them himself, and all the while spoke in his extraordinary language, developed by long practice in wit and evidently long since become a habit with him: most excellently, not bad, you have worn me out thank you...

     But that was not all. When the guests, full and contented, crowded in the front hall, sorting out their coats and canes, the footman Pavlusha bustled around them, or,What was his name here, Pava, a boy of about fourteen, with a close-cropped head and full cheeks.

     "Come on, Pava, do your bit!" Ivan Petrovich said to him.

     Pava struck a pose, raised his hand, and said in a tragic tone:

     "Die, unhappy woman!"

     And everyone burst out laughing.

     "Amusing," thought Startsev as he went out into the street.

     He stopped at a restaurant and had a beer, then set off on foot for his home in Dyalizh. He walked and sang softly all the way: Your voice, so tender and so languorous, is everything to me...

     

     After covering nine versts and then going to bed, he felt not the slightest fatigue; on the contrary, it seemed to him that he would gladly have walked another twenty versts.

     "Not bad at all..." he recalled as he was falling asleep, and he laughed.

     

II

     

     Startsev kept meaning to visit the Turkins, but there was a great deal of work at the hospital, and he simply could not find a free hour. More than a year passed in this way, in toil and solitude; but then a letter in a blue envelope arrived from the town...

     Vera Iosifovna had long suffered from migraines, but lately, when Kotik frightened her every day by saying she would leave for the conservatory, the attacks had begun to recur more and more often. All the town doctors had been to see the Turkins; at last it was the zemstvo doctor's turn. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter in which she begged him to come and relieve her suffering. Startsev came, and after that he began to visit the Turkins often, very often... He did in fact help Vera Iosifovna a little, and she told all her guests that he was an extraordinary, remarkable doctor. But he no longer visited the Turkins on account of her migraine...

     A holiday. Ekaterina Ivanovna finished her long, tedious exercises on the piano. Then they sat a long time in the dining room drinking tea, and Ivan Petrovich told something amusing. But then the doorbell rang; someone had to go out to the front hall to greet a visitor; Startsev took advantage of the moment of confusion and said to Ekaterina Ivanovna in a whisper, greatly agitated:

     "For God's sake, I beg you, don't torment me, let's go into the garden!"

     She shrugged, as if puzzled and not understanding what he wanted of her, but she got up and went.

     "You play the piano for three or four hours," he said, following her, "then you sit with your mother, and there's no possibility of talking to you. Give me at least a quarter of an hour, I beg you."

     Autumn was approaching, and in the old garden it was quiet, sad, and dark leaves lay on the paths. It was already getting dark early.

     "I haven't seen you for a whole week," Startsev continued, "and if you only knew what suffering that is! Let's sit down. Listen to me."

     They both had a favorite spot in the garden: a bench beneath an old, broad maple. And now they sat down on that bench.

     "What do you want?" Ekaterina Ivanovna asked drily, in a businesslike tone.

     "I haven't seen you for a whole week, I haven't heard you for so long. I passionately want, I thirst for your voice. Speak."

     She delighted him with her freshness, the naive expression of her eyes and cheeks. Even in the way her dress sat on her, he saw something extraordinarily sweet, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. And at the same time, despite this naivety, she seemed to him very intelligent and developed beyond her years. With her he could talk about literature, about art, about anything, he could complain to her about life, about people, though during a serious conversation it sometimes happened that she would suddenly begin laughing inappropriately or run off into the house. Like almost all the young ladies of S—, she read a great deal (in general, people in S— read very little, and at the local library they even said that if it weren't for the young ladies and the young Jews, they might as well close the library); this pleased Startsev immensely, and each time he would ask her with excitement what she had been reading in the past few days, and, enchanted, he would listen when she told him.

     "What did you read this week while we didn't see each other?" he asked now. "Tell me, I beg you."

     "I read Pisemsky."

     "What exactly?"

     "A Thousand Souls," answered Kotik. "And what a funny name Pisemsky had: Alexei Feofilaktych!"

     "Where are you going?" Startsev asked in horror when she suddenly got up and walked toward the house. "I absolutely must talk to you,"I must explain myself… Stay with me at least five minutes! I implore you!

     She stopped, as if wishing to say something, then awkwardly thrust a note into his hand and ran into the house, and there again sat down at the piano.

     "Today, at eleven o'clock in the evening," read Startsev, "be at the cemetery near the Demetti monument."

     "Well, this is really not clever at all," he thought, coming to his senses. "What has the cemetery to do with it? Why?"

     It was clear: Kotik was fooling around. Who, really, would seriously think of arranging a rendezvous at night, far outside the city, in a cemetery, when it could easily be arranged on the street, in the town garden? And was it fitting for him, a zemstvo doctor, an intelligent, solid man, to sigh, to receive notes, to traipse around cemeteries, to do silly things at which even schoolboys would laugh now? What would this romance lead to? What would his colleagues say when they found out? So thought Startsev, wandering in the club around the tables, and at half past ten he suddenly took himself off and went to the cemetery.

     He already had his own pair of horses and a coachman, Panteleimon, in a velvet waistcoat. The moon was shining. It was quiet, warm, but warm in an autumnal way. In the suburb, near the slaughterhouses, dogs were howling. Startsev left the horses at the edge of town, in one of the side streets, and walked to the cemetery on foot. "Everyone has their own oddities," he thought. "Kotik is strange too and - who knows? - perhaps she is not joking, she will come," and he surrendered himself to this weak, empty hope, and it intoxicated him.

     He walked about half a verst across a field. The cemetery was marked in the distance by a dark strip, like a forest or a large garden. A fence of white stone appeared, gates… By moonlight one could read on the gates: "The hour cometh in which…" Startsev went in through the wicket gate, and the first thing he saw was white crosses and monuments on both sides of a wide avenue and the black shadows cast by them and by the poplars; and all around, far off, white and black could be seen, and sleepy trees bent their branches over the white. It seemed brighter here than in the field; the maple leaves, resembling paws, stood out sharply on the yellow sand of the avenues and on the slabs, and the inscriptions on the monuments were clear. At first what struck Startsev was what he was now seeing for the first time in his life and what he probably would never see again: a world unlike anything else - a world where the moonlight was so good and soft, as if it were its cradle, where there was no life, no, none, but in every dark poplar, in every grave, one felt the presence of a mystery promising a quiet, beautiful, eternal life. From the slabs and withered flowers, together with the autumnal smell of leaves, came a breath of forgiveness, sorrow, and peace.

     All around was silence; the stars looked down from the sky in deep humility, and Startsev's footsteps rang out so sharply and incongruously. And only when the clock began to strike in the church and he imagined himself dead, buried here forever, did it seem to him that someone was looking at him, and for a minute he thought that this was not peace and not silence, but the dull anguish of nonexistence, suppressed despair…

     The Demetti monument was in the form of a chapel, with an angel on top; at one time an Italian opera was passing through S. and one of the singers died, she was buried and this monument was erected. No one in town remembered her anymore, but the little lamp above the entrance reflected the moonlight and seemed to be burning.

     There was no one. And who would go here at midnight? But Startsev waited, and, as if the moonlight were heating his passion, he waited passionately and pictured in his imagination kisses, embraces. He sat near the monument for about half an hour, then strolled along the side avenues, hat in hand, waiting and thinking about how many women and girls were buried here, in these graves, who had been beautiful, charming, who had loved, who had burned at night with passion, giving themselves to caresses. How, in essence, badly mother nature jokes with man, how insulting it is to realize this! Startsev thought so, and at the same time he wanted to shout that he wanted, that he was waiting for love at any cost; before him there were already not pieces of marble gleaming white, but beautiful bodies, he saw forms that bashfully hid in the shadows of the trees, he felt warmth, and this languor became painful…

     And as if a curtain had descended, the moon went behind the clouds,and suddenly everything became dark all around. Startsev barely found the gate - it was already as dark as an autumn night - then he wandered for about an hour and a half, searching for the lane where he had left his horses.

     "I'm tired, I can barely stand on my feet," he said to Panteleimon.

     And, sitting down with delight in the carriage, he thought:

     "Oh, I shouldn't get fat!"

III

     The next day in the evening he went to the Turkins to propose. But it turned out to be inconvenient, as Yekaterina Ivanovna was having her hair done by a hairdresser in her room. She was getting ready to go to the club for a dance evening.

     He had to sit in the dining room again for a long time and drink tea. Ivan Petrovich, seeing that the guest was pensive and bored, took little notes out of his waistcoat pocket, read a funny letter from the German steward about how all the locks had broken down on the estate and shyness had collapsed.

     "And they'll probably give a substantial dowry," thought Startsev, listening absentmindedly.

     After a sleepless night he was in a state of stupefaction, as if he had been given something sweet and soporific to drink; his soul was misty, but joyful, warm, and at the same time some cold, heavy little piece in his head was reasoning:

     "Stop, before it's too late! Is she a match for you? She is spoiled, capricious, sleeps until two o'clock, and you are a sexton's son, a zemstvo doctor..."

     "Well, so what?" he thought. "So be it."

     "Besides, if you marry her," continued the little piece, "her relatives will make you give up your zemstvo service and live in the city."

     "Well, so what?" he thought. "In the city, then in the city. They'll give a dowry, we'll set up a household..."

     Finally Yekaterina Ivanovna came in wearing a ball gown, décolleté, pretty, fresh, and Startsev admired her and was so delighted that he could not utter a single word, but only looked at her and laughed.

     She began to say goodbye, and he - there was no reason for him to stay any longer - got up, saying it was time for him to go home: patients were waiting.

     "There's nothing to be done," said Ivan Petrovich, "go on, and by the way you can give Kotik a ride to the club."

     Outside a fine rain was falling, it was very dark, and only by Panteleimon's hoarse cough could one guess where the horses were. The carriage top was put up.

     "I walk on the carpet, you walk while you lie," said Ivan Petrovich, seating his daughter in the carriage, "he walks while he lies... Giddy-up! Goodbye, please!"

     They drove off.

     "And I was at the cemetery yesterday," Startsev began. "How ungenerous and merciless of you..."

     "You were at the cemetery?"

     "Yes, I was there and waited for you almost until two o'clock. I suffered..."

     "And suffer, if you don't understand jokes."

     Yekaterina Ivanovna, pleased that she had so cunningly played a trick on the man in love with her and that she was so deeply loved, burst out laughing and suddenly cried out in fright, because at that very moment the horses were turning sharply into the gate of the club and the carriage tilted. Startsev put his arm around Yekaterina Ivanovna's waist; she, frightened, pressed against him, and he could not resist and passionately kissed her on the lips, on the chin, and embraced her more tightly.

     "Enough," she said drily.

     And a moment later she was no longer in the carriage, and a policeman near the lighted entrance of the club was shouting in a disgusting voice at Panteleimon:

     "Why have you stopped, crow? Drive on!"

     Startsev drove home, but soon returned. Dressed in someone else's tailcoat and a white stiff tie, which kept sticking up and trying to slip off his collar, he sat at midnight in the club in the drawing room and spoke to Yekaterina Ivanovna with enthusiasm:

     "Oh, how little those know who have never loved! It seems to me that no one has yet described love correctly, and it is hardly possible to describe this tender, joyful, agonizing feeling, and whoever has experienced it even once will not attempt to convey it in words. Why preambles, descriptions? Why unnecessary eloquence? My love is boundless... I ask, I implore you," Startsev finally uttered, "be my wife!"

     "Dmitri Ionych," said Yekaterina Ivanovna with a very serious expression, after thinking. "Dmitri Ionych, I am very grateful to you for the honor, I respect you, but..."

She stood up and continued standing, "I'm sorry, but I cannot be your wife. Let's speak seriously. Dmitry Ionych, you know, most of all in life I love art, I madly love, adore music, I have devoted my whole life to it. I want to be an artist, I want fame, success, freedom, and you want me to continue living in this town, to continue this empty, useless life which has become unbearable for me. To become a wife, oh no, forgive me! A person must strive toward a higher, brilliant goal, and family life would bind me forever. Dmitry Ionych (she smiled ever so slightly, because, having said 'Dmitry Ionych,' she remembered 'Alexei Feofilaktych'), Dmitry Ionych, you are a kind, noble, intelligent man, you are better than anyone..." tears came to her eyes, "I sympathize with you with all my soul, but... but you will understand..."

     And, so as not to burst into tears, she turned away and left the drawing room.

     Startsev's heart stopped beating anxiously. Coming out of the club into the street, he first of all tore off his stiff necktie and sighed with his whole chest. He felt a little ashamed and his vanity was wounded, he had not expected a refusal, and he could not believe that all his dreams, longings, and hopes had led him to such a silly end, as in a little play at an amateur performance. And he felt sorry for his feeling, for this love of his, so sorry that it seemed he would have burst into sobs or with all his might would have struck Pantelimon's broad back with his umbrella.

     For about three days his work fell from his hands, he did not eat, did not sleep, but when the rumor reached him that Ekaterina Ivanovna had left for Moscow to enter the conservatory, he calmed down and began living as before.

     Afterwards, sometimes recalling how he had wandered through the cemetery or how he had driven all over town searching for a frock coat, he would lazily stretch and say:

     "What a lot of trouble, though!"

     

IV

     

     Four years passed. Startsev already had a large practice in the town. Every morning he hastily received patients at his place in Dyalizh, then drove off to town patients, drove now not with a pair but with a troika with bells, and returned home late at night. He had grown plump, had filled out, and walked on foot reluctantly, as he suffered from shortness of breath. And Pantelimon had also grown plump, and the more he grew in width, the more sorrowfully he sighed and complained of his bitter fate: driving had worn him out!

     Startsev visited various houses and met many people, but he did not become close with anyone. The townsfolk irritated him with their conversations, their views on life, and even their appearance. Experience had taught him little by little that as long as you play cards with a townsman or have a snack with him, he is a peaceful, good-natured, and even not stupid person, but as soon as you speak with him about something inedible, for example, about politics or science, he becomes stumped or launches into such a philosophy, dull and spiteful, that one can only wave a hand and walk away. When Startsev tried to speak even with a liberal townsman, for example, about how humanity, thank God, is moving forward and that in time it will manage without passports and without the death penalty, the townsman would look at him askance and distrustfully and ask: "So then anyone can knife anyone on the street?" And when Startsev in society, at supper or over tea, said that one must work, that one cannot live without work, then everyone took this as a reproach and began to get angry and persistently argue. With all that, the townsfolk did nothing, absolutely nothing, and were not interested in anything, and there was simply no way to think of what to talk with them about. And Startsev avoided conversations, and only had a snack and played vint, and when he found a family holiday at some house and was invited to partake, he would sit down and eat in silence, looking at his plate; and everything that was said at such times was uninteresting, unjust, stupid, he felt irritation, became agitated, but remained silent, and because he always sternly kept silent and looked at his plate, he was nicknamed in the town "the puffed-up Pole," although he had never been a Pole.

     From such entertainments as the theater and concerts he avoided, but instead played vint every evening, for about three hours, with enjoyment. He had one more entertainment,into which he had been drawn imperceptibly, little by little, it was - in the evenings taking out of his pockets the papers obtained by practice, and, it happened, the papers - yellow and green, which smelled of perfume, and vinegar, and incense, and blubber, - were stuffed into all his pockets to the tune of some seventy rubles; and when several hundred had accumulated, he took them to the Mutual Credit Society and deposited them there on current account.

     During all four years after Yekaterina Ivanovna's departure he had been at the Turkins' only twice, on the invitation of Vera Iosifovna, who was still being treated for migraine. Every summer Yekaterina Ivanovna came to stay with her parents, but he had not seen her once; somehow it had not happened.

     But now four years had passed. One quiet, warm morning a letter was brought to the hospital. Vera Iosifovna wrote to Dmitry Ionych that she missed him very much, and asked him to be sure to come to her and relieve her sufferings, and by the way today was her birthday. At the bottom there was a postscript: "I join in mama's request as well. K."

     Startsev thought it over and went to the Turkins' in the evening.

     - Ah, hello, if you please! - Ivan Petrovich met him, smiling with his eyes alone. - Bonjourte.

     Vera Iosifovna, now considerably aged, with white hair, shook Startsev's hand, sighed affectedly, and said:

     - You, doctor, do not wish to court me, you never come to see us, I am already too old for you. But here a young lady has arrived, perhaps she will be more fortunate.

     And Kotik? She had grown thinner, paler, had become prettier and more slender; but this was already Yekaterina Ivanovna, and not Kotik; there was no longer the former freshness and expression of childish naivety. And in her gaze, and in her manners, there was something new - timid and guilty, as if here, in the Turkins' house, she no longer felt at home.

     - How many years, how many winters! - she said, offering Startsev her hand, and it was evident that her heart was beating anxiously; and peering intently, with curiosity, into his face, she continued: - How you have filled out! You are tanned, you have grown more manly, but on the whole you have changed little.

     And now she pleased him, very much pleased him, but something was already lacking in her, or something was superfluous - he himself could not have said what exactly, but something already prevented him from feeling as before. He did not like her pallor, the new expression, the weak smile, the voice, and a little later he already did not like the dress, the armchair in which she sat, he did not like something in the past, when he had almost married her. He remembered his love, the dreams and hopes that had stirred him four years ago - and he felt awkward.

     They drank tea with sweet pie. Then Vera Iosifovna read a novel aloud, read about what never happens in life, and Startsev listened, looked at her gray, beautiful head, and waited for her to finish.

     "Untalented," he thought, "is not the one who does not know how to write stories, but the one who writes them and does not know how to conceal it."

     - Not bad at all, - said Ivan Petrovich. Then Yekaterina Ivanovna played the piano noisily and for a long time, and when she finished, she was thanked for a long time and admired.

     "And it is good that I did not marry her," thought Startsev.

     She looked at him and, apparently, waited for him to propose going into the garden, but he was silent.

     - Let us talk, - she said, coming up to him. - How are you living? What is with you? How? I have been thinking about you all these days, - she continued nervously, - I wanted to send you a letter, I wanted to go to you in Dyalizh myself, and I had already decided to go, but then I changed my mind - God knows how you feel about me now. I awaited you today with such excitement. For God's sake, let us go into the garden. They went into the garden and sat there on a bench under the old maple, as four years ago. It was dark.

     - How are you getting on? - asked Yekaterina Ivanovna.

     - Nothing, we live a little, - answered Startsev.

     And he could not think of anything more. They were silent.

     - I am agitated, - said Yekaterina Ivanovna and covered her face with her hands, - but do not pay attention. I feel so good at home, I am so glad to see everyone and cannot get used to it. So many memories! It seemed to me that we would talk with you without stopping, until morning.Now he saw her face close up, her shining eyes, and here in the darkness she seemed younger than she had in the room, and it was as though her former childish expression had even returned to her. And indeed, she looked at him with naive curiosity, as if she wanted to take a closer look and understand the man who had once loved her so passionately, with such tenderness and so unhappily; her eyes thanked him for that love. And he remembered everything that had been, every smallest detail, how he had wandered through the cemetery, how afterward, toward morning, weary, he had returned home, and suddenly he felt sad and sorry for the past. A little flame kindled in his soul.

     "And do you remember how I used to see you off to the evening party at the club?" he said. "It was raining then, and dark..."

     The flame kept flaring up in his soul, and already he wanted to talk, to complain about life...

     "Eh!" he said with a sigh. "So you ask how I am getting on. How are we getting on here? Not at all. We grow old, we get fat, we sink. Day and night, the twenty-four hours pass, life goes by dimly, without impressions, without thoughts... By day profit, and in the evening the club, a society of card players, alcoholics, wheezers, whom I cannot stand. What good is there in it?"

     "But you have your work, a noble purpose in life. You so loved to talk about your hospital. I was somehow strange then, I imagined myself a great pianist. Now all young ladies play the piano, and I played too, like everyone else, and there was nothing special about me; I am as much a pianist as Mama is a writer. And of course, I did not understand you then, but later, in Moscow, I often thought about you. I thought only of you. What happiness it is to be a zemstvo doctor, to help the suffering, to serve the people. What happiness!" Ekaterina Ivanovna repeated with enthusiasm. "When I thought of you in Moscow, you appeared to me so ideal, so lofty..."

     Startsev remembered the banknotes that he took out of his pockets in the evenings with such pleasure, and the little flame in his soul went out.

     He stood up to go toward the house. She took him by the arm.

     "You are the best of the people I have known in my life," she continued. "We will see each other, we will talk, won't we? Promise me. I am not a pianist, I no longer deceive myself on my own account and I will neither play nor talk about music in your presence."

     When they went into the house and Startsev saw her face in the evening light and her sad, grateful, searching eyes turned toward him, he felt uneasy and thought again:

     "And it is a good thing that I did not marry then."

     He began to take his leave.

     "You have no Roman right to leave without supper," said Ivan Petrovich, seeing him off. "That is most perpendicular on your part. Come now, strike a pose!" he said, turning in the entryway to Pava.

     Pava, no longer a boy but a young man with a mustache, struck a pose, raised his hand upward, and said in a tragic voice:

     "Die, unhappy one!"

     All this irritated Startsev. Getting into the carriage and looking at the dark house and garden that had once been so dear and precious to him, he remembered everything at once - both Vera Iosifovna's novels, and Kotik's noisy playing, and Ivan Petrovich's wit, and Pava's tragic pose, and he thought that if the most talented people in the whole town were so untalented, then what must the town be like.

     Three days later Pava brought a letter from Ekaterina Ivanovna.

     "You are not coming to see us. Why?" she wrote. "I am afraid that you have changed toward us; I am afraid, and I am terrified at the very thought of it. Calm me, come and tell me that all is well.

     I must talk with you. Your E. T."

     He read this letter, thought, and said to Pava:

     "Tell her, my good man, that I cannot come today, I am very busy. Tell her I will come, say, in about three days."

     But three days passed, a week passed, and he still did not go. Once, driving past the Turkins' house, he remembered that he ought to stop in, if only for a minute, but he thought and... did not stop.

     And he never went to the Turkins' again.

     

V

     

     Several more years passed. Startsev had grown still more stout, fat, breathed heavily, and walked with his head thrown back. When he, plump and red, rides in a troika with bells and Panteleimon,too was plump and red, with a fleshy nape, sitting on the box, stretching forward his straight, wooden-like arms, and shouting to those he met, "Keep to the right!" then the picture was impressive, and it seemed that not a man but a pagan god was driving. He had a huge practice in town, no time to breathe, and already owned an estate and two houses in town, and was looking out for a third, more profitable one, and when at the Mutual Credit Society he was told about some house up for auction, he would without ceremony go to that house and, walking through all the rooms, paying no attention to the undressed women and children who looked at him with amazement and fear, would poke his stick at all the doors and say:

     "Is this the study? Is this the bedroom? And what's in here?"

     And at that he breathed heavily and wiped the sweat from his brow.

     He had many worries, but still he did not give up his zemstvo position; greed had overcome him, he wanted to be in time both here and there. In Dyalizh and in town he was already called simply Ionych. "Where is Ionych going?" or "Shouldn't we invite Ionych for a consultation?"

     Probably because his throat was swamped with fat, his voice had changed, become thin and sharp. His character had also changed: he had become heavy, irritable. When receiving patients, he usually got angry, impatiently tapped his stick on the floor, and shouted in his unpleasant voice:

     "Kindly answer only the questions! No talking!"

     He was lonely. Life was boring for him, nothing interested him.

     During the whole time he lived in Dyalizh, his love for Kotik had been his only joy and, probably, his last. In the evenings he played vint at the club and then sat alone at a large table and had supper. He was served by the footman Ivan, the oldest and most respected, they brought him Lafite No. 17, and already everyone - the club elders, the cook, and the footman - knew what he liked and what he did not like, and tried with all their might to please him, or else, God forbid, he might suddenly get angry and start banging his stick on the floor.

     While having supper, he occasionally turned around and joined in some conversation:

     "What are you talking about? Eh? About whom?"

     And when, as it happened, at some neighboring table the conversation turned to the Turkins, he would ask:

     "Which Turkins are you talking about? The ones whose daughter plays the piano?"

     That is all that can be said about him.

     And the Turkins? Ivan Petrovich had not aged, had not changed in the least, and as before kept making jokes and telling anecdotes; Vera Iosifovna read her novels to guests as before, willingly, with heartfelt simplicity. And Kotik played the piano every day, for about four hours. She had noticeably aged, was ailing, and every autumn went with her mother to the Crimea. Seeing them off at the station, Ivan Petrovich, when the train started moving, wiped away his tears and shouted:

     "Goodbye, please!"

     

 

A Commotion

     

     Mashenka Pavletskaya, a young girl who had only just finished her course at the institute, returning from a walk to the Pushkins' house, where she lived as a governess, found an extraordinary commotion. The porter Mikhailo, who opened the door for her, was agitated and red as a lobster.

     Noise was coming from upstairs.

     "Probably the mistress is having a fit..." thought Mashenka, "or she has quarreled with her husband..."

     In the front hall and the corridor she met the maids. One maid was crying. Then Mashenka saw how the master himself, Nikolai Sergeich, a small, not yet old man with a flabby face and a large bald patch, ran out of the doors of her room. He was red. He was twitching... Not noticing the governess, he passed by her and, raising his arms upward, exclaimed:

     "Oh, how awful this is! How tactless! How stupid, wild! Disgusting!"

     Mashenka went into her room, and there for the first time in her life she had to experience in all its sharpness the feeling so familiar to dependent, uncomplaining people living on charity in the homes of the rich and distinguished. They were searching her room. The mistress, Fedosya Vasilyevna, a stout, broad-shouldered lady with thick black eyebrows, bareheaded and angular, with a barely noticeable mustache and red hands, resembling in face and manners a simple peasant cook, was standing by her table and putting back into the workbag balls of wool, scraps, papers... Evidently the governess's appearance was unexpected for her, since,Looking back and seeing her pale, surprised face, she became slightly embarrassed and muttered:

     - Pardon,1 I... I accidentally scattered... caught it with my sleeve...

     And, having said something else, Madame Kushkina rustled her train and left. Mashenka looked around her room with surprised eyes and, understanding nothing, not knowing what to think, shrugged her shoulders, grew cold with fear... What was Fedosya Vasilyevna looking for in her bag? If indeed, as she says, she accidentally caught it with her sleeve and scattered it, then why did Nikolai Sergeich jump out of the room so red and agitated? Why was one drawer slightly pulled out by the table? The money box, in which the governess hid ten-kopeck coins and old stamps, was unlocked. They had unlocked it, but had not been able to lock it again, though they had scratched up the whole lock. The bookshelf, the surface of the table, the bed - everything bore fresh traces of a search. And in the basket with the linen too. The linen had been folded neatly, but not in the order in which Mashenka had left it when leaving home. The search, then, had been a real one, a most real one, but what was it for, why? What had happened? Mashenka remembered the doorman's agitation, the commotion that was still going on, the tearful maid; could all this have been connected with the search that had just taken place at her room? Was she not involved in some terrible affair? Mashenka turned pale and, entirely cold, sank down onto the basket with the linen.

     The maid entered the room.

     - Liza, do you not know why I was... searched? - the governess asked her.

     - The mistress's brooch worth two thousand has disappeared... - said Liza.

     - Yes, but why search me?

     - Everyone was searched, miss. They searched me all over too... They undressed us all completely naked and searched us... And I, miss, swear before God... Not only did I not go near their brooch, I did not even go near the dressing table. I will say the same at the police station.

     - But... why search me? - the governess continued to wonder.

     - The brooch, I say, was stolen... The mistress herself rummaged through everything with her own hands. They even searched the doorman Mikhail. A pure disgrace! Nikolai Sergeich just looks on and clucks like a hen. And you, miss, are trembling for nothing. They found nothing on you. If you did not take the brooch, then you have nothing to fear.

     - But this is, Liza, base... insulting! - said Mashenka, choking with indignation. - Why, this is mean, vile! What right did she have to suspect me and rummage through my things?

     - You live among strangers, miss, - sighed Liza. - Though you are a young lady, still... you are like a servant... It is not like living with your papa and mama...
