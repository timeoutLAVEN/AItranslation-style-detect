Anton Pavlovich Chekhov

The Lady with the Dog

     

I

     

     They said that a new face had appeared on the promenade: a lady with a dog. Dmitry Dmitrich Gurov, who had already been in Yalta for two weeks and had got used to it, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in the pavilion by Verney, he saw a young lady walk along the promenade, a short blonde woman in a beret, with a white Spitz running after her.

     And after that he met her several times a day in the town garden and on the square. She walked alone, always in the same beret, with the white Spitz; no one knew who she was, and they simply called her that: the lady with the dog.

     "If she is here without her husband and without acquaintances," Gurov thought, "then it would not be superfluous to get acquainted with her."

     He was not yet forty, but he already had a twelve-year-old daughter and two gymnasium boys. He had married early, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed one and a half times older than he was. She was a tall woman, with dark eyebrows, straight, important, solid, and, as she called herself, intellectual. She read a lot, did not write the soft sign in letters, called her husband not Dmitry but Dimitry, and he secretly considered her narrow-minded, limited, ungainly, feared her, and did not like being at home. He had been cheating on her for a long time, cheated often, and probably for that reason spoke almost always badly of women, and when they talked about them in his presence, he called them this:

     - The lower race!

     It seemed to him that he had been taught enough by bitter experience to call them anything he liked, but still he could not have lived two days without the "lower race." In the company of men he was bored, ill at ease, with them he was taciturn, cold, but when he was among women, he felt free and knew what to talk to them about and how to behave; and even staying silent with them was easy for him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature there was something attractive, elusive, that disposed women toward him, lured them; he knew it. And he himself was also drawn to them by some force.

     Repeated experience, in truth bitter experience, had long ago taught him that every intimacy, which at first so pleasantly diversifies life and seems a sweet and easy adventure, among decent people, especially among Muscovites, heavy on their feet, indecisive, inevitably grows into an entire task, extremely complicated, and in the end the situation becomes burdensome. But with every new meeting with an interesting woman, this experience somehow slipped from memory, and one wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

     And so one evening he was having dinner in the garden, while the lady in the beret was approaching slowly to take the neighboring table. Her expression, gait, dress, and hairstyle told him that she was from respectable society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here... There was much untruth in the stories about the filth of local morals, he despised them and knew that such stories were mostly invented by people who themselves would gladly sin if they knew how, but when the lady sat down at the neighboring table three steps from him, he remembered those stories about easy victories, about trips to the mountains, and the seductive thought of a quick, fleeting affair, of a romance with an unknown woman whose first and last name you did not know, suddenly took hold of him.

     He affectionately beckoned the Spitz to him and, when it came up, shook a finger at it. The Spitz growled. Gurov shook his finger again.

     The lady looked at him and immediately lowered her eyes.

     "He doesn't bite," she said and blushed.

     "May I give him a bone?" And when she nodded in assent, he asked kindly: "Have you been in Yalta long?"

     "Five days."

     "And I've already been here for the second week."

     They were silent for a little while.

     "Time passes quickly, and yet it is so boring here!" she said, not looking at him.

     "It's only customary to say it's boring here. A common man lives somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra, and it's not boring to him, but he comes here: 'Ah, boring! Ah, dust!' You'd think he came from Granada."

     She laughed.Then both went on eating in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked side by side, and a playful, light conversation began between two free, contented people who did not care where to go or what to talk about. They walked and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was lilac, so soft and warm, and a golden strip ran across it from the moon. They talked about how stifling it was after the hot day. Gurov said that he was from Moscow, educated as a philologist, but worked in a bank; he had once prepared to sing in a private opera, but gave it up, and owned two houses in Moscow… From her he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg, but had married in S., where she had lived for two years, that she would stay in Yalta for another month and perhaps her husband would come for her, since he too wanted a rest. She could never explain where her husband worked, in the provincial government office or in the provincial zemstvo administration, and this made her laugh herself. Gurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

     Then, in his room, he thought about her, about the fact that tomorrow she would probably meet him. That had to be so. Lying down to sleep, he remembered that only recently she had been a boarding school girl, studying, just as his daughter was now, remembered how much shyness and awkwardness there had been in her laughter, in her conversation with a stranger, and that this was probably the first time in her life she had been alone, in such surroundings, where they kept after her and watched her and spoke to her only with one secret purpose she could not help guessing. He remembered her thin, fragile neck, her beautiful gray eyes.

     "There is something pitiful about her after all," he thought, and began to fall asleep.

     

II

     

     A week passed after their acquaintance. It was a holiday. The rooms were stifling, and on the streets dust whirled about like a tornado, snatching hats off heads. All day long one wanted to drink, and Gurov often went into the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeyevna either water with syrup or ice cream. There was nowhere else to go.

     In the evening, when it had quieted down a little, they went to the mole to watch the steamer arrive. There were many strollers on the pier, gathered to meet someone, holding bouquets. And here two features of the elegant Yalta crowd stood out clearly: older ladies were dressed like young ones, and there were many generals.

     Because of the rough sea, the steamer arrived late, when the sun had already set, and before coming alongside the mole, it turned for a long time. Anna Sergeyevna looked at the steamer and the passengers through her lorgnette, as if searching for acquaintances, and when she spoke to Gurov, her eyes shone. She talked a lot, her questions came in short bursts, and she immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

     The elegant crowd was dispersing, faces were no longer visible, the wind had died down completely, and Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood there as if waiting to see whether anyone else would get off the steamer. Anna Sergeyevna was already silent and smelling the flowers, without looking at Gurov.

     "The weather got better toward evening," he said. "Where shall we go now? Shall we go somewhere?"

     She did not answer.

     Then he looked at her intently and suddenly put his arms around her and kissed her on the lips, and the scent and moisture of the flowers washed over him, and at once he looked around fearfully: had anyone seen?

     "Let’s go to your place…" he said softly.

     And they both walked quickly.

     In her room it was stifling, and it smelled of the perfume she had bought in the Japanese shop. Looking at her now, Gurov thought: "What meetings there are in life!" From the past he retained memories of carefree, good-natured women, cheerful from love, grateful to him for happiness, even if only a very short one; and of others, like his wife, who loved without sincerity, with excessive talk, affectedly, with hysteria, with an expression as if it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of two or three such women, very beautiful, cold, whose faces would suddenly flash with a predatory look, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it could give, and they were no longer in their first youth, capricious, unreflective, domineering, not intelligent women, and when Gurov grew cold toward them,that beauty aroused hatred in him, and the lace on their underwear then seemed to him to be like scales.

     But here there was the same timidity, the awkwardness of inexperienced youth, an awkward feeling; and there was an impression of confusion, as though someone had suddenly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this “lady with the little dog,” took what had happened in a somewhat special way, very seriously, as though it were her fall, and so it seemed, and that was strange and out of place. Her features drooped and withered, and long hair hung sadly down the sides of her face, she sat in a gloomy pose, thinking, like a sinner in an old painting.

     “It’s not right,” she said. “You’re the first to disrespect me now.”

     There was a watermelon on the table in the room. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat slowly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

     Anna Sergeyevna was touching, she breathed the purity of an honest, naive, little-lived woman; the solitary candle burning on the table scarcely lit her face, but it was clear that she felt bad in her heart.

     “Why would I stop respecting you?” asked Gurov. “You yourself don’t know what you’re saying.”

     “May God forgive me!” she said, and her eyes filled with tears. “It’s terrible.”

     “You’re almost making excuses for yourself.”

     “What do I have to excuse? I am a bad, base woman, I despise myself and am not thinking of excuses. I didn’t deceive my husband, I deceived myself. And not only now, but for a long time already I have been deceiving. My husband, perhaps, is an honest, good man, but he is a lackey! I don’t know what he does there, how he serves, I only know that he is a lackey. When I married him, I was twenty, I was tormented by curiosity, I wanted something better; there is, I told myself, another life. I wanted to live! To live and live… Curiosity burned me… you don’t understand that, but, I swear to God, I could no longer control myself, something was happening to me, I could not be held back, I told my husband I was ill, and came here… And here I kept wandering around, as if in a daze, as if mad… and now I have become a vulgar, vile woman whom anyone may despise.”

     Gurov was already bored listening, the naive tone annoyed him, this confession, so unexpected and out of place; if it weren’t for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or acting a role.

     “I don’t understand,” he said quietly, “what is it you want?”

     She hid her face on his chest and pressed herself to him.

     “Believe, believe me, I beg you…” she said. “I love an honest, pure life, and sin is disgusting to me, I myself don’t know what I’m doing. Simple people say: the evil one led me astray. And I can now say of myself that the evil one led me astray.”

     “There, there…” he murmured.

     He looked into her motionless, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke softly and tenderly, and she gradually calmed down, and cheerfulness returned to her; they both began to laugh.

     Then, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment, the town with its cypresses had a completely dead look, but the sea was still roaring and beating against the shore; one barque rocked on the waves, and a little lantern flickered drowsily on it.

     They found a cabman and drove to Oreanda.

     “Down in the hall just now I learned your surname: on the signboard it’s written von Diderits,” said Gurov. “Is your husband German?”

     “No, his grandfather seems to have been German, but he himself is Orthodox.”

     In Oreanda they sat on a bench not far from the church, looking down at the sea and keeping silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist, and white clouds stood motionless on the mountaintops. The foliage did not stir on the trees, cicadas were crying, and the monotonous, muffled шум of the sea rising from below spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. It had sounded below like that when there was still no Yalta or Oreanda here, and now it sounds and will keep sounding in the same indifferent, muffled way when we are gone. And in this permanence, in complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, perhaps lies the pledge of our eternal salvation, the unbroken movement of life on earth, unbroken perfection. Sitting beside the young woman who at dawn seemed so beautiful,Calmed and enchanted by this fairy-tale setting, the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the vast sky, Gurov thought about how, in essence, if one thinks about it, everything in this world is beautiful, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget the higher aims of existence, our human dignity.

     

     Some man approached, probably the watchman, looked at them, and went away. And this detail seemed so mysterious and also beautiful. One could see the steamer from Feodosia arriving, lit by the morning dawn, its lights already out.

     “The dew on the grass,” Anna Sergeyevna said after a silence.

     “Yes. Time to go home.”

     They returned to the city.

     Then every noon they met on the embankment, had breakfast together, dined, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept badly and that her heart was beating uneasily, kept asking the same questions, troubled now by jealousy, now by the fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or in the garden, when there was no one near them, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with glances around and fear that someone might see, the heat, the smell of the sea, and the constant flickering before his eyes of idle, elegant, well-fed people seemed to transform him completely. He told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how tempting, was impatiently passionate, did not leave her side for a moment, and she often fell into thought and kept asking him to admit that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, and saw in her only a vulgar woman. Almost every evening, later on, they would go somewhere out of town, to Oreanda or to the waterfall, and the outing always succeeded, the impressions were invariably beautiful, majestic.

     They were expecting her husband to arrive. But a letter came from him saying that his eyes had become painful, and he begged his wife to return home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeyevna hurried.

     “It’s good that I’m leaving,” she said to Gurov. “It’s fate itself.”

     She went by carriage, and he saw her off. They traveled all day. When she got into the compartment of the express train, and when the second bell rang, she said:

     “Let me look at you once more... one more look. Like this.”

     She did not cry, but she was sad, as if ill, and her face was trembling.

     “I’ll think of you... remember you,” she said. “God be with you, stay. Don’t bear me a grudge. We are parting forever, this is how it must be, because we should never have met at all. Well, God be with you.”

     The train left quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and within a minute the noise could no longer be heard, as if everything had conspired on purpose to put an end as quickly as possible to this sweet oblivion, this madness. And, left alone on the platform and looking into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the chirping of crickets and the humming of telegraph wires with the feeling that he had just woken up. And he thought that here in his life there had been one more affair or adventure, and that too had already ended, and now only a memory remained... He was touched, sad, and felt a slight remorse, for this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he had been kind and warm toward her, but still in the way he dealt with her, in his tone and caresses, there lingered a shadow of light mockery, the crude arrogance of a happy man who was, moreover, almost twice her age. All the while she called him kind, extraordinary, exalted; evidently he seemed to her not what he really was, so he had unconsciously deceived her...

     There at the station it already smelled of autumn, and the evening was cool.

     “It’s time for me to go north too,” Gurov thought, leaving the platform. “Time!”

     

III

     

     At home in Moscow everything was already wintry, the stoves were heated, and in the mornings, when the children gathered for gymnasium and drank tea, it was dark, and the nanny lit a fire for a short while. The frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sleigh riding, it is pleasant to see the white earth, the white roofs, breathing feels soft, wonderful, and at such a time one remembers one’s youth. The old lindens and birches, white with frost, have a kindly expression, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms,and near them one no longer wants to think about mountains and the sea.

     Gurov was a Muscovite, he returned to Moscow on a fine frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and walked along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the ringing of the bells, the recent trip and the places he had been to lost all their charm for him. Little by little he plunged into Moscow life, already read three newspapers a day with greed, and said that he did not read Moscow newspapers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties, anniversaries, and it already flattered him that famous lawyers and actors visited him and that he played cards with a professor at the doctors' club. He could already eat a whole portion of solyanka in a frying pan…

     Another month would pass, and Anna Sergeevna, it seemed to him, would be covered in his memory by a haze and would only occasionally appear in his dreams with a touching smile, as others did. But more than a month passed, deep winter came, and in his memory everything was still clear, as if he had parted from Anna Sergeevna only yesterday. And the memories blazed ever more strongly. Did he hear the voices of the children preparing their lessons in the evening silence of his study, did he hear a romance or an organ in a restaurant, or did a blizzard howl in the fireplace, when suddenly everything was resurrected in his memory: both what happened on the pier, and the early morning with fog on the mountains, and the steamer from Feodosia, and the kisses. He would walk around the room for a long time and remember, and smile, and then the memories would turn into dreams, and the past in his imagination would mingle with what was to come. Anna Sergeevna did not come to him in dreams, but followed him everywhere like a shadow, and watched him. With his eyes closed, he saw her as if alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed to himself better than he had been then, in Yalta. In the evenings she looked at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the gentle rustle of her clothes. In the street he followed women with his eyes, looking to see whether there was someone like her…

     And already he was tormented by a strong desire to share his memories with someone. But at home he could not talk about his love, and outside the home there was no one to talk to. Not with the tenants, and not in the bank. And what was there to say? Did he really love then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relationship with Anna Sergeevna? So he had to speak vaguely about love, about women, and no one guessed what it was about, and only his wife moved her dark eyebrows and said:

     — Dmitri, the role of a fop really does not suit you.

     One night, as he was leaving the doctors' club with his partner, a civil servant, he could not restrain himself and said:

     — If only you knew what an enchanting woman I met in Yalta!

     The civil servant got into the sleigh and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

     — Dmitri Dmitrich!

     — What?

     — And just now you were right: the sturgeon does smell a bit!

These words, so ordinary, somehow suddenly outraged Gurov, seemed humiliating, dirty. What savage customs, what faces! What pointless nights, what uninteresting, unnoticed days! Frenzied card playing, gluttony, drunkenness, constant talk about the same thing. Useless business and talk about the same thing take away the best part of time, the best energies, and in the end there remains some stunted, wingless life, some nonsense, and there is no way to leave and run away, as if you were sitting in a madhouse or in prison companies!

     Gurov did not sleep all night and was outraged, and then spent the whole day with a headache. And in the following nights he slept badly, kept sitting up in bed and thinking, or walking from corner to corner. He was tired of the children, tired of the bank, he did not want to go anywhere, or talk about anything.

     In December, during the holidays, he got ready to travel and told his wife that he was going to Petersburg to help a young man with some business, and he went to S. Why? He did not really know himself. He wanted to see Anna Sergeevna and talk, arrange a meeting, if possible.

     He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room at the hotel, where the entire floor was covered with gray soldier's cloth and there was an inkwell on the table, gray with dust, with a horseman on horseback, his hand raised with a hat, but his head knocked off. The porter gave him the necessary information: von Diderits lives on Staro-Goncharnaya Street, in his own house — not far from the hotel, lives well, richly, has his own horses, everyone knows him in the city. The porter pronounced it like this: Drydryits.

     Gurov slowly went to Staro-Goncharnaya, found the house. Right opposite the house stretched a fence, gray, long, with nails.

     "You'll run away from a fence like that," Gurov thought, glancing now at the windows, now at the fence.

     He reasoned: today is a non-sitting day, and the husband is probably at home. Besides, it would be tactless to go into the house and cause embarrassment. If he sent a note, it might end up in the husband's hands, and then everything could be ruined. Best of all was to rely on chance. And so he kept walking along the street and near the fence, waiting for that chance. He saw a beggar go through the gate and dogs attack him, then an hour later he heard someone playing the piano, and the sounds came faintly, indistinctly. It must have been Anna Sergeevna playing. The front door suddenly opened, and out came some old woman, and after her ran the familiar white spitz. Gurov wanted to call the dog, but his heart suddenly began to pound, and from excitement he could not remember what the spitz was called.

     He walked and hated the gray fence more and more, and already thought with irritation that Anna Sergeevna had forgotten about him and perhaps was already amusing herself with someone else, and that this was so natural in the position of a young woman who is forced from morning till evening to look at this cursed fence. He returned to his room and sat for a long time on the sofa, not knowing what to do, then he had lunch, then slept for a long time.

     "How stupid and restless all this is," he thought, waking up and looking at the dark windows; it was already evening. "And I even slept for nothing. What am I supposed to do now at night?"

     He sat on the bed, covered with a cheap gray blanket, as if from a hospital, and mocked himself in annoyance:

     "Well, here's your lady with a dog... Here's your adventure... Now sit here."

     Even in the morning, at the station, a poster with very large letters had caught his eye: Geisha was being performed for the first time. He remembered this and went to the theater.

     "It is quite possible that she goes to first performances," he thought.

     The theater was full. And here, as in all provincial theaters, there was haze above the chandelier, the gallery was noisily restless; in the front row before the performance began, local fops stood with their hands behind their backs; and here, in the governor's box, in the first seat sat the governor's daughter in a boa, while the governor himself modestly hid behind the curtain, and only his hands were visible; the curtain swayed, the orchestra tuned for a long time. All the while the audience was coming in and taking their seats, Gurov searched eagerly with his eyes.

     Anna Sergeevna entered too. She sat in the third row, and when Gurov looked at her, his heart contracted, and he understood clearly,that for him now there was no one in the whole world closer, dearer, and more important; she, lost in the provincial crowd, this little woman, nothing remarkable about her, with a vulgar lorgnette in her hands, now filled his whole life, was his grief, his joy, the only happiness he now desired for himself; and to the sounds of a bad orchestra, shabby suburban violins, he thought of how lovely she was. He thought and dreamed.

     Along with Anna Sergeyevna came in and sat down beside her a young man with small sideburns, very tall, stooped; at every step he shook his head and seemed to be constantly bowing. Probably this was the husband whom she had then in Yalta, in a fit of bitter feeling, called a footman. Indeed, in his long figure, in the sideburns, in the small bald spot there was something footman-like and humble, he smiled sweetly, and on his lapel some kind of scholarly badge glinted, like a servant's number.

     In the first intermission the husband went out to smoke, and she remained in her seat. Gurov, who was also sitting in the stalls, went up to her and said in a trembling voice, forcing a smile:

     "Hello."

     She looked at him and went pale, then looked again in terror, not believing her eyes, and tightly clasped her fan and lorgnette together in her hands, evidently struggling with herself not to faint. Neither of them said anything. She sat, he stood, frightened by her confusion, not daring to sit down beside her. The tuning violins and flute began to play, and suddenly it became frightening, it seemed that everyone in the boxes was looking at them. But then she stood up and quickly headed for the exit; he followed her, and both of them went aimlessly through the corridors, up and down the stairs, while people in court, teaching, and imperial estate uniforms flashed before their eyes, all with badges; ladies flashed by, fur coats hung on the racks, a draft blew through, carrying the smell of cigarette butts. And Gurov, whose heart was pounding hard, thought:

     "Oh, God! And what are these people, this orchestra..."

     And at that moment he suddenly remembered how that evening at the station, after seeing Anna Sergeyevna off, he had told himself that it was all over and they would never see each other again. But how far it still was from the end!

     On the narrow, gloomy staircase where "Way to the amphitheater" was written, she stopped.

     "How you scared me!" she said, breathing heavily, still pale, stunned. "Oh, how you scared me! I am hardly alive. Why did you come? Why?"

     "But understand, Anna, understand..." he said in a low voice, hurriedly. "I beg you, understand..."

     She looked at him with fear, with pleading, with love, looking intently so as to fix his features more firmly in her memory.

     "I suffer so much!" she continued, not listening to him. "I thought of nothing but you all the time, I lived with thoughts of you. And I wanted to forget, forget, but why, why did you come?"

     Higher up, on the landing, two gymnasium boys were smoking and looking down, but Gurov did not care, he drew Anna Sergeyevna to him and began kissing her face, cheeks, hands.

     "What are you doing, what are you doing!" she said in horror, pushing him away from her. "You and I have gone mad. Leave today, leave now... I beg you by all that is holy, I implore you... Someone is coming!"

     Someone was coming up the stairs from below.

     "You must go..." Anna Sergeyevna continued in a whisper. "Do you hear, Dmitry Dmitrich? I will come to you in Moscow. I have never been happy, I am unhappy now, and I will never, never be happy, never! Don't make me suffer even more! I swear I will come to Moscow. And now let us part! My dear, kind, precious one, let us part!"

     She shook his hand and quickly began going down, looking back at him all the while, and from her eyes it was clear that she truly was not happy. Gurov stood for a little while, listened, then, when everything had quieted down, found his coat rack and left the theater.

     

IV

     

     And Anna Sergeyevna began coming to see him in Moscow. Once every two or three months she would leave S. and tell her husband that she was going to consult a professor about her women's ailment, and her husband believed and did not believe. Having arrived in Moscow,She stayed at the Slavyansky Bazaar and at once sent a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov would go to see her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

     One winter morning he was going to see her in this way (the messenger had come to him the evening before and not found him). His daughter was going with him, whom he wanted to see off to the gymnasium, it was on the way. Large, wet snow was falling.

     “It’s three degrees above zero now, and yet it’s snowing,” Gurov said to his daughter. “But this warmth is only on the surface of the earth, in the upper layers of the atmosphere the temperature is completely different.”

     “Papa, why is there no thunder in winter?”

     He explained that too. He spoke and thought that here he was going to a meeting, and not a living soul knew about it and probably never would know. He had two lives: one open, seen and known by everyone who needed to know it, full of conventional truth and conventional deceit, completely like the lives of his acquaintances and friends, and another, lived secretly. And by some strange combination of circumstances, perhaps by chance, everything that was important, interesting, necessary to him, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, what made up the core of his life, took place in secret from others, while everything that was his falsehood, his shell, in which he hid himself to conceal the truth, such as his work at the bank, arguments at the club, his “lower race,” going with his wife to anniversaries, all this was open. And from himself he judged others, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that under the cover of secrecy, as under the cover of night, every person’s real, most interesting life went on. Every personal existence is held together by secrecy, and perhaps partly for that reason a civilized person is so nervously concerned that personal privacy be respected.

     After seeing his daughter off to the gymnasium, Gurov went to the Slavyansky Bazaar. He left his fur coat downstairs, went upstairs, and quietly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, exhausted by the journey and the waiting, had been expecting him since the evening before; she was pale, looked at him and did not smile, and as soon as he came in she was already pressed against his chest. As though they had not seen each other for two years, their kiss was long, lingering.

     “Well, how are things there?” he asked. “What’s new?”

     “Wait, I’ll tell you now... I can’t.”

     She could not speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed her handkerchief to her eyes.

     “Well, let her cry, and I’ll sit for a while,” he thought, and sat down in an armchair.

     Then he rang and said to bring him tea; and then, when he was drinking tea, she was still standing there, turned toward the window... She was crying from emotion, from the sad realization that their life had turned out so miserably; they saw each other only in secret, hiding from people like thieves! Wasn’t their life broken?

     “Come on, stop it!” he said.

     To him it was obvious that this love of theirs would not end any time soon, no one knew when. Anna Sergeyevna was becoming more and more attached to him, adored him, and it would have been inconceivable to tell her that all this must have an end someday; besides, she would not have believed it.

     He went up to her and took her by the shoulders, to comfort her, to joke, and at that moment he saw himself in the mirror.

     His hair was already beginning to turn gray. And it seemed strange to him that he had grown so old in recent years, so plain. The shoulders on which his hands rested were warm and trembling. He felt pity for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already close to beginning to fade and wither, like his own life. Why did she love him so much? He had always seemed to women not to be what he was, and they loved in him not himself, but the man created by their imagination and whom they were eagerly seeking in their own lives; and then, when they noticed their mistake, they still loved him. And not one of them had been happy with him. Time went by, he met women, became close, parted, but never once loved; there was everything, but not love.

     And only now, when his head had turned gray, did he love properly, truly, for the first time in his life.

     Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other,as very close, dear people, like husband and wife, like tender friends. It seemed to them that fate itself had meant them for each other, and it was hard to understand why he was married and she was married; and indeed they were like two migratory birds, a male and a female, caught and forced to live in separate cages. They forgave each other for what they were ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love had changed both of them.

     In the past, in sad moments, he used to calm himself with all kinds of reasoning that came to mind, but now he had no time for reasoning, he felt deep compassion, he wanted to be sincere, tender...

     "Stop, my dear," he said. "Have your cry, and that's enough... Now let's talk, let's come up with something."

     Then they talked for a long time, discussing how to free themselves from the need to hide, to deceive, to live in different cities, not seeing each other for long stretches. How to break free from these unbearable bonds?

     "How? How?" he asked, clutching his head. "How?"

     And it seemed that only a little more, and a solution would be found, and then a new, beautiful life would begin; and it was clear to both of them that the end was still far, far away and that the hardest and most difficult part was only just beginning.

     

     

The Black Monk

     

I

     

     Andrey Vasilyich Kovrin, a scholar, had grown tired and had upset his nerves. He was not under treatment, but once, over a bottle of wine, he spoke casually with his friend the doctor, and the doctor advised him to spend spring and summer in the country. At the same time, a long letter came from Tanya Pesotskaya, who asked him to come to Borisovka and stay a while. So he decided that he really did need a trip.

     First, in April, he went to his own ancestral estate, Kovrinka, and lived there in seclusion for three weeks; then, when he waited for the roads to improve, he set off by horse for his former guardian and tutor Pesotsky, a famous gardener in Russia. From Kovrinka to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, it was reckoned to be no more than seventy versts, and to travel along the soft spring road in a comfortable carriage with springs was a true pleasure.

     Pesotsky's house was enormous, with columns, with lions whose plaster was peeling, and with a liveried footman at the entrance. The old park, gloomy and severe, laid out in the English style, stretched for nearly a verst from the house to the river and ended there at a steep, abrupt clay bank, where pines grew with roots exposed like shaggy paws. Below, the water gleamed in solitude, lapwings flew about with plaintive cries, and there was always such a mood here that one could sit down and write a ballad. But right by the house, in the yard and in the orchard, which together with the nurseries covered some thirty desyatinas, it was cheerful and lively even in bad weather. Kovrin had never seen such amazing roses, lilies, camellias, such tulips of every imaginable color, from bright white to soot-black, in general such a wealth of flowers as Pesotsky's anywhere else. Spring had only just begun, and the true luxury of the flowerbeds was still hidden in the greenhouses, but even what was already blooming along the alleys and here and there in the beds was enough, while walking through the garden, to feel oneself in a kingdom of delicate colors, especially in the early hours, when dew sparkled on every petal.

     What was the decorative part of the garden, and what Pesotsky himself contemptuously called trifles, had once made a fairy-tale impression on Kovrin in childhood. What oddities, what refined monstrosities and mockeries of nature were there! There were espaliers of fruit trees, a pear shaped like a pyramidal poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an umbrella of apple tree, arches, monograms, candelabra, and even 1862 made of plums, a number signifying the year when Pesotsky first took up gardening. There were also beautiful, graceful little trees with straight trunks as sturdy as palms, and only on close inspection could one recognize in these little trees gooseberry or currant. But what most delighted in the garden and gave it a lively look,So it was constant movement. From early morning until evening, around the trees, bushes, along the alleys and flowerbeds, people with wheelbarrows, hoes, watering cans were bustling about like ants…

Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys’ in the evening, around ten o’clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semyonych, in great alarm. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer foretold frost by morning, and meanwhile the gardener Ivan Karlych had gone to town, and there was no one to rely on. At dinner they talked only about the morning frost, and it was decided that Tanya would not go to bed, but at one o’clock she would walk through the garden and see whether everything was in order, while Yegor Semyonych would get up at three, or even earlier.

Kovrin sat with Tanya all evening and after midnight went with her into the garden. It was cold. In the yard there was already a strong smell of smoke. In the large fruit orchard, which was called the commercial orchard and brought Yegor Semyonych several thousand in net annual income, a black, thick, acrid smoke lay over the ground and, wrapping around the trees, saved those thousands from the frost. The trees stood there in a checkerboard pattern, their rows straight and regular, like soldiers’ ranks, and this strict, pedantic regularity, and the fact that all the trees were the same height and had exactly identical crowns and trunks, made the picture monotonous and even dull. Kovrin and Tanya walked along the rows where fires of manure, straw, and all kinds of refuse were smoldering, and now and then they met workers wandering through the smoke like shadows. Only the cherry trees, plum trees, and some varieties of apple trees were in bloom, but the whole garden was sunk in smoke, and only near the nurseries did Kovrin breathe deeply.

“I used to sneeze here from the smoke as a child,” he said, shrugging, “but I still don’t understand how smoke can protect against frost.”

“Smoke replaces clouds when there aren’t any…” Tanya answered.

“And what are clouds for?”

“In cloudy, overcast weather there is no frost.”

“Oh, really!”

He laughed and took her hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with thin black eyebrows, the raised collar of her coat that kept her from moving her head freely, and all of her, slender, graceful, in a dress tucked up from the dew, touched him deeply.

“My God, she’s already grown up!” he said. “When I left here last time, five years ago, you were still just a child. You were so skinny, long-legged, bareheaded, wearing a short little dress, and I used to tease you for a heron… How time flies!”

“Yes, five years!” Tanya sighed. “A lot of water has flowed under the bridge since then. Tell me, Andryusha, honestly,” she said quickly, looking him in the face, “have you grown out of us? Though why am I asking? You’re a man, you already live your own interesting life, you’re a figure… Distance is so natural! But whatever happens, Andryusha, I want you to think of us as your own. We have the right to that.”

“I do, Tanya.”

“Cross your heart?”

“Yes, cross my heart.”

“You were surprised today that we have so many of your photographs. You know, my father adores you. Sometimes it seems to me he loves you more than me. He’s proud of you. You’re a scholar, an extraordinary person, you’ve made a brilliant career for yourself, and he’s sure you turned out this way because he raised you. I don’t stop him from thinking that. Let him.”

Dawn was already beginning, and this was especially noticeable in the sharpness with which the smoke clouds and tree crowns began to stand out in the air. Nightingales were singing, and the cry of quails could be heard from the fields.

“However, it’s time to sleep,” Tanya said. “And it’s cold too.” She took his arm. “Thank you, Andryusha, for coming. We have boring acquaintances, and not many of them. All we have is the garden, the garden, the garden,” and nothing else. “Standard, half-standard,” she laughed, “Aport, ranet, Borovinka, budding, grafting… Our whole life, all of it, has gone into the garden. I don’t even ever dream about anything except apple trees and pear trees. Of course, that’s good, it’s useful, but sometimes you want something else for a change. I remember when you used to come to visit us on vacation or just like that, the house would somehow feel fresher and brighter, as if the covers had been taken off the chandelier and the furniture. I was a little girl then, and still I understood.”

     She spoke for a long time and with deep feeling. For some reason, it suddenly occurred to him that over the course of the summer he might become attached to this small, frail, talkative creature, grow fascinated, and fall in love, and in both of their situations that was so possible and natural! The thought touched and amused him; he bent toward the dear, worried face and sang softly:

     Onegin, I will not pretend,

     I madly love Tatyana…

     When they came home, Yegor Semyonich was already up. Kovrin didn’t feel like sleeping, so he talked with the old man and went back into the garden with him. Yegor Semyonich was tall, broad-shouldered, with a big belly and shortness of breath, but he always walked so fast that it was hard to keep up with him. He looked extremely worried, was always hurrying somewhere, and with such an expression that if he were even one minute late, everything would be ruined!

     “So, brother, here’s a story…” he began, stopping to catch his breath. “On the surface of the earth, as you can see, it’s freezing, but if you raise a thermometer on a stick about two sazhens above the ground, it’s warm there… How come?”

     “Honestly, I don’t know,” said Kovrin and laughed.

     “Hm… One can’t know everything, of course… No matter how broad the mind is, you can’t fit everything in there. You’re mostly into philosophy, right?”

     “Yes. I read psychology, and generally study philosophy.”

     “And it doesn’t bore you?”

     “On the contrary, it’s the only thing I live for.”

     “Well, God bless you…” Yegor Semyonich said, thoughtfully stroking his gray sideburns. “God bless you… I’m very glad for you… glad, brother…”

     But suddenly he listened intently and, making a terrifying face, ran off to the side and soon disappeared behind the trees, in clouds of smoke.

     “Who tied the horse to the apple tree?” his desperate, soul-rending cry was heard. “What villain and scoundrel dared tie the horse to the apple tree? My God, my God! It’s all been ruined, frozen, spoiled, dirtied! The garden is lost! The garden is destroyed! My God!”

     When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted, offended.

     “Well, what can you do with this accursed lot?” he said in a crying voice, throwing up his hands. “Stepka hauled manure at night and tied the horse to the apple tree! The rascal wound the reins around it so tight that the bark got rubbed in three places. Can you imagine! I tell him, and he just stands there like a fool and blinks! Hanging’s too good for him!”

     After calming down, he embraced Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

     “Well, God bless you… God bless you…” he muttered. “I’m very glad you came. So indescribably glad… Thank you.”

     Then, with that same quick stride and worried face, he walked through the whole garden and showed his former pupil all the greenhouses, hothouses, cold frames, and his two apiaries, which he called a wonder of our century.

     While they were walking, the sun came up and brightly lit the garden. It grew warm. Anticipating a bright, cheerful, long day, Kovrin remembered that it was only the beginning of May and that the whole summer lay ahead, just as bright, cheerful, and long, and suddenly a joyful youthful feeling stirred in his chest, the same one he had felt in childhood when he ran through this garden. And he himself embraced the old man and tenderly kissed him. Moved, the two of them went into the house and began drinking tea from old porcelain cups, with cream, with rich, buttery pretzels, and these little things reminded Kovrin again of his childhood and youth. The beautiful present and the impressions of the past awakening in him merged together; they made his soul feel cramped, but good.

     He waited,When Tanya woke up, and with her he drank coffee, took a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read attentively, made notes, and from time to time raised his eyes to look at the open windows or at the fresh flowers, still wet with dew, standing in vases on the table, then lowered his eyes to the book again, and it seemed to him that every little vein in him was trembling and playing with pleasure.

II

In the village he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city. He read and wrote a lot, studied Italian, and, when he walked, happily thought that soon he would sit down to work again. He slept so little that everyone was amazed. If by chance he fell asleep for half an hour during the day, then he would not sleep all night afterward and, after a sleepless night, as if nothing had happened, felt cheerful and lively.

He talked a lot, drank wine, and smoked expensive cigars. The Pesotskys were often visited, almost every day, by the young ladies neighbors, who played the piano and sang together with Tanya. Sometimes a young man, a neighbor, came over, and he played the violin well. Kovrin listened to the music and singing greedily and was exhausted by them, and this was physically expressed in his eyelids growing heavy and his head drooping to one side.

One day after evening tea he was sitting on the balcony and reading. In the drawing room at that time Tanya, soprano, one of the young ladies, contralto, and the young man on the violin were rehearsing Braga’s famous serenade. Kovrin listened to the words, they were in Russian, and could not make sense of them at all. At last, setting the book aside and listening carefully, he understood: a girl, sick with imagination, had heard in the garden at night some mysterious sounds so beautiful and strange that she had to recognize them as a sacred harmony, incomprehensible to us mortals, and therefore flying back up into the heavens. Kovrin’s eyes began to grow heavy. He stood up and, exhausted, walked through the drawing room, then through the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya by the arm and went out onto the balcony with her.

“I’ve been thinking about one legend since this morning,” he said. “I can’t remember whether I read it somewhere or heard it, but it’s some strange, inconsistent legend. To begin with, it isn’t clear. A thousand years ago some monk, dressed in black, was walking through the desert, somewhere in Syria or Arabia… A few miles from where he was walking, fishermen saw another black monk moving slowly across the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend apparently does not recognize, and listen further. Another mirage came from the first mirage, then a third from the second, so that the image of the black monk kept being passed endlessly from one layer of the atmosphere to another. He was seen now in Africa, now in Spain, now in India, now in the Far North… At last he left the limits of the earth’s atmosphere and now wanders through the whole universe, still never quite getting into those conditions in which he could fade away. Perhaps he is now seen somewhere on Mars or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the real point of the legend, is that exactly a thousand years after the monk walked through the desert, the mirage will again enter the earth’s atmosphere and appear to people. And supposedly that thousand years is already running out… According to the meaning of the legend, we are supposed to expect the black monk any day now.”

“A strange mirage,” Tanya said, not liking the legend.

“But what’s most amazing,” Kovrin laughed, “is that I can’t remember at all where this legend got into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Hear it? Or perhaps I dreamed of the black monk? I swear to God, I don’t remember. But the legend fascinates me. I’ve been thinking about it all day today.”

After letting Tanya go back to the guests, he left the house and, deep in thought, walked around the flower beds. The sun was already setting. The flowers, because they had just been watered, gave off a damp, irritating smell. Singing started again in the house, and from a distance the violin gave the impression of a human voice. Straining his mind to remember where he had heard or read the legend, Kovrin headed slowly toward the park and, almost without noticing it, reached the river.Along the path that ran along the steep bank past the exposed roots, he went down to the water, startled some sandpipers there, and frightened away two ducks. On the grim pines here and there the last rays of the setting sun still glinted, but on the surface of the river it was already true evening. Kovrin crossed to the other side over the floodgates. Before him now lay a wide field covered with young rye, not yet in bloom. No human dwelling, no living soul in the distance, and it seemed that the path, if one followed it, would lead to that same unknown, mysterious place where the sun had just gone down and where the evening glow was blazing so broadly and majestically.

     "How spacious, free, quiet it is here!" thought Kovrin, walking along the path. "And it seems the whole world is looking at me, hiding and waiting for me to understand it…"

     But then waves ran through the rye, and a light evening breeze gently touched his bare head. A minute later another gust of wind, but stronger, the rye rustled, and behind him came a dull murmur of pines. Kovrin stopped in amazement. On the horizon, like a whirlwind or a tornado, a tall black column was rising from the earth to the sky. Its outlines were indistinct, but in the first instant it was possible to understand that it was not standing still, but moving with terrible speed, moving straight here, прямо at Kovrin, and the closer it came, the smaller and clearer it became. Kovrin darted aside into the rye to make way for it, and barely managed to do so…

     A monk in black clothes, with a gray head and black eyebrows, his arms crossed on his chest, swept past… His bare feet did not touch the ground. After rushing past for about three sazhens, he looked back at Kovrin, nodded, and smiled at him kindly and at the same time slyly. But what a pale, terribly pale, gaunt face! Growing again, he flew across the river, silently struck the clay bank and the pines, and passing through them, vanished like smoke.

     "Well, there you are…" Kovrin muttered. "So the legend was true."

     Without trying to explain the strange phenomenon to himself, satisfied just with having managed to see so closely and so clearly not only the black clothes, but even the monk's face and eyes, pleasantly excited, he went home.

     In the park and garden people were walking peacefully, in the house they were playing music, so he alone must have seen the monk. He very much wanted to tell Tanya and Egor Semyonych everything, but he realized that they would certainly take his words for delirium, and that would frighten them; better to keep quiet. He laughed aloud, sang, danced the mazurka, he was cheerful, and everyone, the guests and Tanya, found that today his face was somehow special, radiant, inspired, and that he was very interesting.

     

III

     

     After dinner, when the guests had left, he went to his room and lay down on the sofa: he wanted to think about the monk. But a minute later Tanya came in.

     "Here, Andryusha, read Father's articles," she said, handing him a bundle of pamphlets and reprints. "Excellent articles. He writes very well."

     "Well, excellent indeed!" said Egor Semyonych, coming in after her and laughing forcedly; he was ashamed. "Don't listen, please, don't read! Though if you want to fall asleep, then by all means read: excellent sleeping potion."

     "In my opinion, they are splendid articles," Tanya said with deep conviction. "Please read them, Andryusha, and persuade Papa to write more often. He could write a full course in gardening."

     Egor Semyonych gave a strained laugh, blushed, and began saying the sort of things that usually embarrassed authors say. At last he began to give in.

     "In that case, read Goshé's article first and these Russian little pieces here," he muttered, sorting the pamphlets with trembling hands, "otherwise you won't understand. Before reading my objections, you have to know what I'm objecting to. Anyway, nonsense… boring stuff. And it's probably time for sleep."

     Tanya went out. Egor Semyonych sat down beside Kovrin on the sofa and gave a deep sigh.

     "Yes, my dear fellow…" he began after a while of silence. "There you have it, my most esteemed magistr. So I write articles, take part in exhibitions, and get medals… They say Pesotsky's apples are the size of a head, and they say Pesotsky made his fortune from gardening."In a word, Kochubey is rich and famous. But the question is, what is all this for? The garden really is beautiful, exemplary... It is not a garden, but a whole institution of great state importance, because it is, so to speak, a step into a new era of Russian agriculture and Russian industry. But what for? What is the goal?

     – The thing speaks for itself.

     – I don’t mean that. I want to ask: what will happen to the garden when I die? As it is now, in the form you see it, it won’t last a single month without me. The whole secret of success is not that the garden is large and there are many workers, but that I love the work – do you understand? – perhaps I love it more than myself. Just look at me: I do everything myself. I work from morning till night. I do all the grafting myself, the pruning – myself, the planting – myself, everything – myself. When people help me, I get jealous and irritated to the point of rudeness. The whole secret is in love, that is, in a sharp master’s eye, in master’s hands, and in that feeling when you go somewhere to visit for an hour, sit there, and your heart is not at ease, you’re not yourself: you’re afraid something may happen in the garden. And when I die, who will watch over it? Who will work? The gardener? The workers? Eh? So here’s what I’ll tell you, my dear friend: the first enemy in our work is not a hare, not a cockchafer, and not frost, but an outsider.

     – And Tanya? – Kovrin asked, laughing. – She can’t be more harmful than a hare. She loves and understands the work.

     – Yes, she loves and understands it. If after my death the garden goes to her and she becomes the mistress of it, then of course one couldn’t wish for anything better. But what if, God forbid, she gets married? – whispered Yegor Semyonych, looking at Kovrin in alarm. – That’s it exactly! She gets married, children come along, and then there’s no time left to think about the garden.

     – What I’m most afraid of is this: she’ll marry some fellow, and he’ll get greedy and rent the garden out to market women, and everything will go to hell in the very first year! In our line, women are a scourge from God!

     Yegor Semyonych sighed and was silent for a while.

     – Maybe it is selfishness, but I’ll say it openly: I don’t want Tanya to marry. I’m afraid! There’s this dandy who comes here with a violin and fiddles away; I know Tanya won’t marry him, I know that well, but I can’t stand seeing him! In general, brother, I’m quite a strange fellow. I admit it.

     Yegor Semyonych stood up and paced the room in agitation, and it was clear that he wanted to say something very important, but didn’t dare.

     – I’m very fond of you and I’ll speak frankly with you, – he decided at last, putting his hands in his pockets. – On some delicate questions I’m straightforward and say plainly what I think, and I can’t stand so-called hidden thoughts. I’ll say it straight: you’re the only person I wouldn’t be afraid to give my daughter to. You’re intelligent, you have a heart, and you wouldn’t let my beloved work perish. And the main reason is that I love you like a son... and I’m proud of you. If you and Tanya somehow ended up having a romance, then – well? I’d be very glad and even happy. I’m saying this plainly, without affectation, like an honest man.

     Kovrin laughed. Yegor Semyonovich opened the door to leave and stopped on the threshold.

     – If you and Tanya had a son, I’d make him a gardener, – he said after thinking. – Though this is but idle dreaming... Good night.

     Left alone, Kovrin settled himself more comfortably and began reading the articles. One had the title: “On Intermediate Culture,” another: “A Few Words on Mr. Z.’s Note About Replowing the Soil for a New Garden,” a third: “More on Budding with a Dormant Eye” – and so on. But what an uneasy, uneven tone, what a nervous, almost painful ardor! Here is an article, apparently with the most peaceful title and indifferent content: it speaks of the Russian Antonovka apple tree. But Yegor Semyonych begins it with “audiatur altera pars” 1 and ends – “sapienti sat” 2, and between these sayings there is a whole fountain of various poisonous words aimed at “the learned ignorance of our certified gentlemen gardeners, observing nature from the heights of their chairs,” or Mr. Gosha, “whose success was created by laymen and dilettantes,” and right there, awkwardly and out of place, a strained and insincere regret,that men who steal fruit and break trees in the process can no longer be whipped with rods.

     "It's a lovely, beautiful, healthy thing, but even here there is passion and war," thought Kovrin. "Apparently, everywhere and in every field, people of ideas are nervous and highly sensitive. Probably, that is how it must be."

     He thought of Tanya, who liked Yegor Semyonych's articles so much. Short, pale, thin, with the collarbones showing; her eyes wide open, dark, intelligent, looking somewhere intently and searching for something; her walk, like her father's, small, hurried. She talks a lot, likes to argue, and at the same time accompanies every even insignificant phrase with expressive facial expressions and gestures. She must be extremely nervous.

     Kovrin went on reading, but understood nothing and gave up. The pleasant excitement, the same one with which he had recently danced the mazurka and listened to music, now tormented him and stirred up many thoughts in him. He got up and began pacing the room, thinking about the black monk. It occurred to him that if he alone had seen this strange, supernatural monk, then he was ill and had already reached hallucinations. This thought frightened him, but not for long.

     "But I feel fine, and I am doing no one any harm; so there is nothing bad in my hallucinations," he thought, and he felt fine again.

     He sat down on the sofa and held his head in his hands, restraining the incomprehensible joy filling his whole being, then paced again and sat down to work. But the thoughts he was reading from the book did not satisfy him. He wanted something gigantic, boundless, overwhelming. Toward morning he undressed and reluctantly got into bed: he had to sleep!

     When he heard Yegor Semyonych's footsteps going out into the garden, Kovrin rang and ordered the valet to bring wine. He drank several glasses of Lafite with delight, then covered himself up head to toe; his consciousness grew hazy, and he fell asleep.

     

IV

     

     Yegor Semyonych and Tanya often quarreled and said unpleasant things to each other.

     One morning they had a falling out over something. Tanya cried and went to her room. She did not come out for lunch or tea. At first Yegor Semyonych walked around pompous and puffed up, as if wanting to show that for him the interests of justice and order were above everything in the world, but soon he lost his nerve and was downcast. He wandered sadly around the park and kept sighing, "ah, my God, my God!" and did not eat a crumb at lunch. At last, guilty and tormented by conscience, he knocked on the locked door and called timidly:

     "Tanya! Tanya?"

     And in reply, from behind the door, there came a weak voice, exhausted from tears and at the same time resolute:

     "Leave me, I beg you."

     The owners' distress was reflected throughout the whole house, even among the people working in the garden. Kovrin was absorbed in his interesting work, but in the end even he became bored and uneasy. To somehow dispel the general foul mood, he decided to intervene and, toward evening, knocked on Tanya's door. He was let in.

     "Oh my, how shameful!" he began jokingly, looking in surprise at Tanya's tear-stained, red-spotted, sorrowful face. "Is it really that serious? Oh my!"

     "But if you only knew how he torments me!" she said, and tears, burning, abundant tears, burst from her large eyes. "He has tormented me!" she continued, wringing her hands. "I didn't say anything to him... nothing... I only said there was no need to keep... extra workers, if... if one can, whenever needed, have day laborers. After all... after all, the workers have done nothing for a whole week... I... I only said that, and he started shouting and said to me... many hurtful, deeply insulting things. Why?"

     "There, there," Kovrin said, smoothing her hair. "You quarreled, you cried, and that's that. You can't stay angry for long, that's not good... especially since he loves you endlessly."

     "He has ruined my whole life," Tanya went on, sobbing. "All I hear are insults and... and wrongs. He thinks I'm unnecessary in his house. Well? He's right. Tomorrow I'll leave here, become a telegraph operator... Let him..."

     "Now, now, now... Don't cry, Tanya. Don't cry,"Darling... you’re both hot-tempered, after all

Kovrin spoke gently and persuasively, while she kept crying, trembling her shoulders and wringing her hands, as if a terrible misfortune had truly befallen her. He felt sorry for her all the more because her grief was not serious, yet she suffered deeply. What trivial things were enough to make this creature unhappy for an entire day, and perhaps for her whole life! Consoling Tanya, Kovrin thought that, apart from this girl and her father, you would not find people in the whole world, not with a candle to help you look, who would love him as one of their own, as family; if it were not for these two people, then perhaps he, having lost his father and mother in early childhood, would never know until his dying day what sincere affection was, or that naive, unreasoning love that is felt only for very close blood relatives. And he felt that his half-sick, frayed nerves, like iron to a magnet, responded to the nerves of this crying, trembling girl. He would never again have been able to love a healthy, sturdy, rosy-cheeked woman, but pale, weak, unhappy Tanya appealed to him.

     And he gladly stroked her hair and shoulders, shook her hands and wiped away her tears… At last she stopped crying. She kept complaining for a long time about her father and about her hard, unbearable life in this house, begging Kovrin to understand her position; then she gradually began to smile and sigh that God had given her such a bad character, and finally, laughing loudly, called herself a fool and ran out of the room.

     When, a little later, Kovrin went out into the garden, Yegor Semyonych and Tanya were already strolling side by side along the alley as if nothing had happened, and both were eating rye bread with salt, since both were hungry.

     

V

     

     Pleased that he had played the peacemaker so well, Kovrin went into the park. Sitting on a bench and thinking, he heard the clatter of carriages and women’s laughter - the guests had arrived. When the evening shadows began to fall in the garden, the sounds of a violin and singing voices came faintly, and this reminded him of the black monk. Where is that optical absurdity wandering now, in what country or on what planet?

     Hardly had he remembered the legend and pictured in his mind that dark apparition he had seen in the rye field when, from behind a pine tree right opposite, there emerged silently, without the slightest rustle, a man of medium height with an uncovered gray head, dressed all in dark and barefoot, like a beggar, and on his pale, almost dead face the black eyebrows stood out sharply. Nodding his head amiably, this beggar or wanderer noiselessly came up to the bench and sat down, and Kovrin recognized in him the black monk. For a minute they looked at each other - Kovrin in amazement, the monk gently and, as before, a little slyly, with a look of self-assurance.

     - But you are a mirage, Kovrin said. - So why are you here and sitting in one place? That does not fit the legend.

     - It does not matter, the monk replied after a moment, in a quiet voice, facing him. - The legend, the mirage, and I, all of this is a product of your excited imagination. I am a phantom.

     - So you do not exist? Kovrin asked.

     - Think what you like, the monk said and smiled faintly. - I exist in your imagination, and your imagination is part of nature, so I exist in nature too.

     - You have a very old, wise, and extremely expressive face, as if you really had lived more than a thousand years, Kovrin said. - I did not know that my imagination was capable of creating such phenomena. But why are you looking at me with such delight? Do you like me?

     - Yes. You are one of the few who are rightly called God's chosen ones. You serve eternal truth. Your thoughts, intentions, your amazing science, and your whole life bear the divine, heavenly seal, because they are devoted to what is rational and beautiful, that is, to what is eternal.

     - You said: eternal truth… But is eternal truth accessible to people, and do they need it, if there is no eternal life?

     - Eternal life exists, said the monk.

     - Do you believe in human immortality?

     - Yes, of course. You, humanity, are awaiting a great, brilliant future. And the more people like you there are on earth, the sooner this future will come true. Without you,If humanity were dedicated servants of the highest principle, living consciously and freely, it would be insignificant; developing in the natural order, it would have to wait a long time yet for the end of its earthly history. But you will lead it into the kingdom of eternal truth several thousand years earlier, and that is your great merit. You embody the blessing of God that has rested upon mankind.

     – And what is the purpose of eternal life? asked Kovrin.

     – The same as the purpose of every life, enjoyment. True enjoyment lies in knowledge, and eternal life will offer countless and inexhaustible sources of knowledge, and in this sense it is said: In my Father’s house are many mansions.

     – If you knew how pleasant it is to listen to you! said Kovrin, rubbing his hands in pleasure.

     – Very glad.

     – But I know, when you leave, I will be troubled by the question of your nature. You are a ghost, a hallucination. So, I am mentally ill, abnormal?

     – Even if so. Why be upset? You are ill because you worked beyond your strength and tired yourself out, and that means you sacrificed your health to an idea, and the time is near when you will give that idea your very life as well. What could be better? This is what all noble natures endowed from above strive for.

     – If I know that I am mentally ill, can I trust myself?

     – And why do you know that the brilliant people whom the whole world trusts have not also seen ghosts? Scientists now say that genius is akin to madness. My friend, only ordinary, herd-like people are healthy and normal. Worries about the nervous age, overwork, degeneration, and the like can seriously trouble only those who see the purpose of life in the present, that is, herd-like people.

     – The Romans said: mens sana in corpore sano 3.

     – Not everything the Romans or Greeks said is true. Elevated mood, excitement, ecstasy, all that distinguishes prophets, poets, and martyrs for an idea from ordinary people, is hostile to the animal side of man, that is, to his physical health. I repeat: if you want to be healthy and normal, go into the herd.

     – Strange, you are repeating what often comes into my own head, said Kovrin. – It is as if you have spied on and overheard my innermost thoughts. But let us not talk about me. What do you mean by eternal truth?

     The monk did not answer. Kovrin looked at him and could not make out his face. His features were blurred and indistinct. Then the monk’s head and hands began to disappear; his body merged with the bench and the evening twilight, and he vanished altogether.

     – The hallucination is over! said Kovrin and laughed. – A pity.

     He walked back to the house cheerful and happy. The little that the black monk had said to him flattered not his vanity, but his whole soul, his whole being. To be chosen, to serve eternal truth, to stand among those who will make humanity worthy of the kingdom of God several thousand years earlier, that is, free people from several unnecessary thousand years of struggle, sin, and suffering, to give everything to an idea – youth, strength, health, to be ready to die for the common good, what a lofty, what a happy destiny! His past flashed through his memory, pure, chaste, full of work, he remembered what he had studied and what he had taught others, and decided that there was no exaggeration in the monk’s words.

     Tanya was coming toward him through the park. She was already wearing a different dress.

     – You here? she said. – And we have been looking for you, looking... But what’s wrong with you? she asked in surprise, looking at his rapturous, shining face and at his eyes full of tears. – How strange you are, Andryusha.

     – I am content, Tanya, said Kovrin, putting his hands on her shoulders. – More than content, I am happy! Tanya, dear Tanya, you are an extraordinarily lovely creature. Dear Tanya, I am so glad, so glad!

     He kissed both her hands warmly and went on:

     – I have just lived through bright, wonderful, unearthly moments. But I cannot tell you everything, because you will call me mad or not believe me. Let us talk about you. Dear, splendid Tanya! I love you and have already grown used to loving you. Your closeness, our meetings ten times a day, have become a need of my soul. I do not know,how am I to manage without you when I leave to go back home.

     “Well!” Tanya laughed. “You’ll forget us in two days. We’re little people, and you’re a great man.”

     “No, let’s speak seriously!” he said. “I’ll take you with me, Tanya. Yes? Will you come with me? Do you want to be mine?”

     “Well!” Tanya said and wanted to laugh again, but no laugh came, and red patches appeared on her face.

     She began breathing quickly and walked fast#fast, but not toward the house, farther into the park.

     “I wasn’t thinking about it... I wasn’t thinking!” she said, as if wringing her hands in despair.

     And Kovrin walked after her and said with the same radiant, ecstatic face:

     “I want a love that would take hold of me completely, and only you, Tanya, can give me that love. I’m happy! Happy!”

     She was stunned, bent over, shrank in on herself, and seemed to grow ten years older at once, while he found her beautiful and loudly expressed his delight:

     “How lovely she is!”

     

VI

     

     After learning from Kovrin that not only was the romance going well, but that there would even be a wedding, Yegor Semyonych walked for a long time from corner to corner, trying to hide his agitation. His hands started to tremble, his neck swelled and turned purple, and he ordered the trotting carriage harnessed and drove off somewhere. Tanya, who saw how he struck the horse and how he pulled his cap down deeply, almost to his ears, understood his mood, locked herself in her room, and cried the whole day.

     The peaches and plums were already ripe in the greenhouses; packing and sending this delicate and capricious cargo to Moscow required a great deal of attention, labor, and trouble. Because the summer was very hot and dry, each tree had to be watered, which took a lot of time and manpower, and caterpillars appeared in great numbers, which the workers and even Yegor Semyonych and Tanya, to Kovrin’s great disgust, crushed with their bare fingers. At the same time, they already had to accept autumn orders for fruit and trees and carry on a large correspondence. And at the very hottest time, when it seemed no one had a free minute, the field work began, taking away more than half the workers from the garden; Yegor Semyonych, badly tanned, exhausted, and angry, kept galloping from the garden to the field and back, shouting that he was being torn to pieces and that he would put a bullet through his head.

     And then there was the fuss over the dowry, which the Pesotskys attached no small importance to. The clatter of scissors, the hum of sewing machines, the stifling heat from irons, and the whims of the dressmaker, a nervous, touchy lady, made everyone in the house dizzy. And as if on purpose, guests came every day, and they had to be entertained, fed, and even put up overnight. But all this hard labor passed unnoticed, like in a fog. Tanya felt as if love and happiness had caught her by surprise, although since the age of fourteen she had for some reason been sure that Kovrin would marry her. She was amazed, bewildered, and could not believe herself... At one moment such joy would rush over her that she wanted to fly above the clouds and pray to God there, and at another she would suddenly remember that in August she would have to leave her native nest and leave her father, or, heaven knows from where, the thought would come that she was insignificant, small, and unworthy of such a great man as Kovrin, and she would go to her room, lock the door, and cry bitterly for hours on end. When there were guests, she would suddenly feel that Kovrin was extraordinarily handsome and that all women were in love with him and envied her, and her soul would fill with delight and pride, as if she had conquered the whole world, but as soon as he smiled kindly at some young lady, she would already be trembling with jealousy, go to her room, and there would be tears again. These new sensations took complete hold of her, she helped her father mechanically and did not notice either the peaches or the caterpillars or the workers, or how quickly time was passing.

     Something almost the same was happening with Yegor Semyonych. He worked from morning till night, was always hurrying somewhere, losing his temper, getting irritated, but all of it in some kind of magical half-sleep. It was as if two people were already living in him: one was the real Yegor Semyonych, who, listening to the gardener Ivan Karlych as he reported the disorder to him, would be outraged and in despair clutch his head, and the other,not real, definitely half-drunk, who would suddenly break off a business conversation in mid-sentence, touch the gardener on the shoulder, and begin to mutter:

     "What can you say, blood means a lot. His mother was an amazing, noblest, wisest woman. It was a pleasure to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel's. She drew beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang… Poor thing, may she rest in peace, she died of consumption.

     The not real Yegor Semyonych sighed and, after a pause, went on:

     "When he was a boy and growing up with me, he had the same angelic face, clear and kind. His look, his movements, and his speech are as gentle and graceful as his mother's. And his mind? He always amazed us with his intelligence. And besides, he is a magister for a reason! For a reason! Just wait, Ivan Karlych, what he will be like in ten years! You won't reach him with your hand!

     But then the real Yegor Semyonych, remembering himself, made a terrible face, grabbed his head, and shouted:

     "Devils! Spoiled it, ruined it, fouled it, froze it over! The garden is lost! The garden is ruined!

     And Kovrin kept working with his usual diligence and did not notice the commotion. Love only added fuel to the fire. After each meeting with Tanya, he, happy and ecstatic, went back to his room and, with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and declared his love to her, took up his book or his manuscript. What the black monk said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, the bright future of mankind, and so on, gave his work a special, extraordinary meaning and filled his soul with pride, with a sense of his own greatness. Once or twice a week, in the park or in the house, he met the black monk and talked with him for a long time, but this did not frighten him, on the contrary, it delighted him, since he was already firmly convinced that such visions visit only the chosen, outstanding people who have devoted themselves to serving an idea.

     One day the monk appeared during dinner and sat by the window in the dining room. Kovrin was delighted and very skillfully started a conversation with Yegor Semyonych and Tanya about things that might interest the monk. The black guest listened and nodded kindly, while Yegor Semyonych and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, not suspecting that Kovrin was not speaking to them, but to his hallucination.

     The Dormition Fast came and went unnoticed, and soon after that came the wedding day, which, at Yegor Semyonych's insistent request, was celebrated "with a bang," that is, with a pointless revel that lasted two days. They ate and drank about 3,000 rubles' worth, but because of the bad hired musicians, the shouty toasts, and the footmen running about, because of the noise and the crowding, no one could appreciate either the expensive wines or the amazing appetizers brought in from Moscow.

     

VII

     

     One long winter night Kovrin was lying in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, whose head often ached in the evenings from not being used to city life, had long since fallen asleep and now and then, in her delirium, uttered some incoherent phrases.

     The clock struck three. Kovrin blew out the candle and lay down. He lay for a long time with his eyes closed, but could not fall asleep because, as it seemed to him, the bedroom was very hot and Tanya was delirious. At half past four he lit the candle again, and at that moment he saw the black monk sitting in the armchair by the bed.

     "Hello," said the monk, and after a short pause asked, "What are you thinking about now?"

     "About fame," Kovrin replied. "In the French novel I was just reading, there is a man, a young scholar, who does foolish things and wastes away from longing for fame. That longing is incomprehensible to me."

     "Because you are intelligent. You are indifferent to fame, like a toy that does not interest you."

     "Yes, that's true."

     "Fame does not smile on you. What is flattering, amusing, or instructive in the fact that your name will be carved on a gravestone and then time will erase that inscription along with the gilding? And besides, fortunately, there are too many of you for weak human memory to hold on to your names."

     "I understand," Kovrin agreed. "And why remember them at all? But let's talk about something else. For example, about happiness. What is happiness?"When the clock struck five, he was sitting on the bed, with his legs hanging down over the rug, and speaking to the monk:

– In ancient times, one fortunate man eventually became afraid of his own happiness, so great was it! And, in order to appease the gods, he sacrificed his favorite ring to them. You know? I, like Polycrates, am beginning to be a little troubled by my happiness. It seems strange to me that from morning till night I feel nothing but joy, it fills my whole being and drowns out all my other feelings. I do not know what sadness, grief, or boredom are. Here I am, not sleeping, I have insomnia, and yet I am not bored. I am speaking seriously: I am beginning to be puzzled.

– But why? the monk was astonished. – Is joy an unnatural feeling? Shouldn't it be the normal state of a human being? The higher a person is in intellectual and moral development, the freer they are, the more pleasure life gives them. Socrates, Diogenes, and Marcus Aurelius experienced joy, not sorrow. And the apostle says: Rejoice constantly. So rejoice and be happy.

– But what if the gods get angry? Kovrin joked and laughed. – If they take away my comfort and make me shiver and go hungry, I doubt I would like that.

Meanwhile Tanya had woken up and was looking at her husband with astonishment and horror. He was speaking to the armchair, gesturing and laughing: his eyes were shining, and there was something strange in his laughter.

– Andryusha, who are you talking to? she asked, grabbing the hand he had stretched out toward the monk. – Andryusha! Who?

– Huh? Who? Kovrin said, embarrassed. – With him... He's sitting there, – he said, pointing at the black monk.

– There is no one here... no one! Andryusha, you are ill!

Tanya embraced her husband and pressed close to him, as if shielding him from visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

– You are ill! she sobbed, trembling all over. – Forgive me, darling, dear one, but I have long noticed that your soul is upset by something... You are mentally ill, Andryusha...

Her trembling passed to him too. He looked once more at the armchair, which was already empty, suddenly felt weakness in his arms and legs, became frightened, and began to dress.

– It's nothing, Tanya, nothing... – he muttered, trembling. – Really, I am a little unwell... it's time to admit it.

– I noticed it long ago... and papa noticed it, – she said, trying to hold back her sobs. – You talk to yourself, smile somehow strangely... don't sleep. Oh, my God, my God, save us! – she said in horror. – But don't be afraid, Andryusha, don't be afraid, for God's sake, don't be afraid...

She, too, began to dress. Only now, looking at her, did Kovrin understand the full danger of his situation, understand what the black monk and the talks with him meant. It was now clear to him that he was insane.

Both of them, not knowing why themselves, dressed and went into the drawing room: she in front, he behind her. There, awakened by sobbing, stood Yegor Semyonych in a robe and with a candle in his hand, the man who was staying with them.

– Don't be afraid, Andryusha, – Tanya said, trembling as if with a fever, – don't be afraid... Papa, this will all pass... it will all pass...

Kovrin was too excited to speak. He wanted to say to his father-in-law in a joking tone:

– Congratulations, I seem to have gone mad, – but only moved his lips and smiled bitterly.

At nine o'clock in the morning they put on him an overcoat and fur coat, wrapped him in a shawl, and took him by carriage to the doctor. He began treatment.

VIII

Summer came again, and the doctor ordered him to go to the country. Kovrin had already recovered, had stopped seeing the black monk, and all that remained was to rebuild his physical strength. Living with his father-in-law in the country, he drank a lot of milk, worked only two hours a day, did not drink wine, and did not smoke.

On the evening of St. Elijah’s Day, an all-night vigil was held in the house. When the deacon handed the priest the censer, the old enormous hall smelled exactly like a cemetery, and Kovrin grew bored. He went out into the garden. Not noticing the luxurious flowers, he walked around the garden, sat on a bench, then strolled through the park; reaching the river, he went down and stood there in thought, looking at the water. The grim pines with shaggy roots, which the year before had seen him here so young, cheerful, and vigorous, now were not whispering but stood motionless and silent, as if they did not recognize him. And indeed, his head was cropped, his long beautiful hair was gone, his walk was sluggish, and compared with last summer his face had grown fuller and paler.

     He crossed over on the footbridges to the other bank. Where rye had grown the year before, there now lay rows of cut oats. The sun had already set, and on the horizon a broad red glow was blazing, foretelling windy weather tomorrow. It was quiet. Gazing in the direction where the black monk had appeared for the first time the year before, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes until the evening glow began to fade…

     When he returned home, languid and dissatisfied, the all-night vigil was already over. Yegor Semyonych and Tanya were sitting on the terrace steps and drinking tea. They were talking about something, but when they saw Kovrin they suddenly fell silent, and he could tell from their faces that they had been talking about him.

     “It seems to me it’s already time for you to drink milk,” Tanya said to her husband.

     “No, not yet…” he replied, sitting on the very bottom step. “Drink it yourself. I don’t want any.”

     Tanya exchanged a worried glance with her father and said in a guilty voice:

     “You yourself can see that milk is good for you.”

     “Yes, very good!” Kovrin sneered. “Congratulations: since Friday I’ve gained another pound. He tightly clasped his head with both hands and said sadly, “Why, why did you cure me? Bromides, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear over every sip, every step, all this will eventually drive me to idiocy. I was going crazy, I had delusions of grandeur, but I was cheerful, vigorous, and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I’ve become more sensible and solid, but I’m like everyone else: I’m mediocre, and life is boring to me… Oh, how cruelly you treated me! I had hallucinations, but who was hurt by them? I ask you, who was hurt by them?”

     “God knows what you’re saying!” Yegor Semyonych sighed. “It’s boring even to listen.”

     “Then don’t listen.”

     The presence of people, especially Yegor Semyonych, now irritated Kovrin. He answered him curtly, coldly, and even rudely, and looked at him only mockingly and with hatred, while Yegor Semyonych felt awkward and coughed guiltily, though he felt no guilt at all. Not understanding why their once kind, good-natured relations had changed so sharply, Tanya clung to her father and looked into his eyes anxiously; she wanted to understand and could not, and for her it was clear only that things were getting worse and worse every day, that her father had lately grown much older, while her husband had become irritable, capricious, exacting, and dull. She could no longer laugh and sing, ate nothing at lunch, and did not sleep for whole nights, waiting for something terrible, and was so exhausted that once she lay unconscious from lunch until evening. During the all-night vigil it seemed to her that her father was crying, and now, as the three of them sat on the terrace, she was making an effort not to think about it.

     “How lucky Buddha and Mohammed or Shakespeare were that kind relatives and doctors did not treat them for ecstasy and inspiration!” said Kovrin. “If Mohammed had taken potassium bromide for his nerves, worked only two hours a day, and drank milk, then just as little would have remained of that remarkable man as of his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will eventually make humanity dull, mediocrity will be considered genius, and civilization will perish. If only you knew,” Kovrin said irritably, “how grateful I am to you!”

     He felt a strong irritation, and, so as not to say too much, quickly got up and went into the house. It was quiet,and tobacco and jalap were wafting in from the garden through the open windows. In the vast dark hall, moonlight lay in green patches on the floor and on the piano. Kovrin remembered the raptures of the previous summer, when it had smelled the same way of jalap and the moon shone in the windows. To bring back last year’s mood, he quickly went to his study, lit a strong cigar, and ordered the valet to bring wine. But the cigar made his mouth bitter and disgusting, and the wine did not taste the same as it had the year before. And what does it mean to get out of the habit! The cigar and two swallows of wine made him dizzy and set his heart pounding, so he had to take potassium bromide.

     Before going to sleep, Tanya said to him:

     “Father adores you. You’re angry with him about something, and it’s killing him. Look: he’s aging by the hour. I beg you, Andryusha, for God’s sake, for your poor father’s sake, for my peace of mind, be kind to him!”

     “I can’t and I won’t.”

     “But why?” Tanya asked, beginning to tremble all over. “Explain to me why?”

     “Because I don’t like him, that’s all,” Kovrin said carelessly, and shrugged, “but let’s not talk about him: he’s your father.”

     “I can’t, I can’t understand!” Tanya said, pressing her temples and staring fixedly. “Something incomprehensible, terrible is happening in our house. You’ve changed, you’re not like yourself… You, a smart, extraordinary person, get irritated over trifles, meddle in squabbles… Such little things upset you that sometimes one simply wonders and can’t believe it: is that really you? Well, well, don’t be angry, don’t be angry,” she went on, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. “You’re smart, kind, noble. You’ll be fair to Father. He’s such a kind man!”

     “He’s not kind, he’s good-natured. Vaudeville uncles like your father, with their well-fed, good-natured faces, extraordinarily hospitable and odd, used to move me and make me laugh in stories, in vaudeville, and in life, but now they disgust me. They’re selfish to the bone. What I hate most is their well-fedness and that stomachish, purely bull-like or boar-like optimism.”

     Tanya sat down on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

     “It’s torture,” she said, and from her voice it was clear that she was already extremely tired and that speaking was hard for her. “Since winter, not a single peaceful minute… It’s horrible, my God! I’m suffering…”

     “Yes, of course, I’m Herod, and you and your dear papa are the infants of Egypt. Of course!”

     His face seemed ugly and unpleasant to Tanya. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. She had also noticed before that something was already missing from his face, as if the face itself had changed since he cut his hair. She wanted to say something hurtful to him, but at once she caught herself feeling unfriendly, was frightened, and left the bedroom.

     

IX

     

     Kovrin received an independent professorship. The inaugural lecture was set for December 2, and a notice about it was posted in the university corridor. But on the appointed day he informed the student inspector by telegram that he would not be lecturing because of illness.

     He was coughing up blood. He spat blood, but twice a month or so it would pour out heavily, and then he would become extremely weak and fall into a sleepy state. This illness did not frighten him much, since he knew that his late mother had lived with exactly the same illness for ten years, even more; and the doctors assured him it was not dangerous, advising only that he should not get excited, should lead a regular life, and should talk as little as possible.

     In January the lecture again did not take place for the same reason, and in February it was already too late to begin the course. It had to be postponed until the following year.

     He was no longer living with Tanya, but with another woman, who was two years older than him and took care of him like a child. His mood was peaceful, submissive: he readily obeyed, and when Varvara Nikolayevna, which was the name of his companion, was about to take him to the Crimea, he agreed, although he had a presentiment that nothing good would come of the trip.

     They arrived in Sevastopol in the evening and stayed at a hotel,to rest and go to Yalta tomorrow. Both were tired from the journey. Varvara Nikolaevna drank tea, went to bed, and soon fell asleep. But Kovrin did not lie down. Back home, an hour before leaving for the station, he had received a letter from Tanya and had not dared to open it, and now it was lying in his side pocket, and the thought of it was unsettling him. Deep down, honestly, he now considered his marriage to Tanya a mistake, was glad that he had finally broken with her, and the memory of that woman, who in the end had turned into walking living relics, and in whom, it seemed, everything was already dead except for her large, fixed, intelligent eyes, the memory of her stirred in him only pity and self-reproach. The handwriting on the envelope reminded him how, about two years earlier, he had been unfair and cruel, how he had taken out on innocent people his inner emptiness, boredom, loneliness, and dissatisfaction with life. It also reminded him how once he had torn his dissertation and all the articles written during his illness into tiny pieces, and how he had thrown them out the window, and the scraps, blown by the wind, had caught on the trees and flowers. In every line he saw strange claims based on nothing, light-minded arrogance, audacity, megalomania, and it made such an impression on him as if he were reading a description of his own vices. But when the last notebook had been torn up and flown out the window, he suddenly felt irritated and bitter for some reason, went to his wife, and said many unpleasant things to her. My God, how he tormented her! Once, wanting to hurt her, he told her that her father had played an unattractive role in their romance by asking him to marry her. Yegor Semyonych overheard this by accident, rushed into the room, and in his despair could not utter a single word, only shuffled in place and made a strange mooing sound, as if he had lost the use of his tongue, while Tanya, looking at her father, cried out in a heartbreaking voice and fainted. It was vile.

All this came back to him at the sight of the familiar handwriting. Kovrin went out onto the balcony. The weather was calm and warm, and it smelled of the sea. The marvelous bay reflected the moon and the lights, and had a color that is hard to name. It was a delicate, soft blend of blue and green. In places the water looked the color of blue vitriol, and in places it seemed that moonlight had thickened and, instead of water, filled the bay. Altogether, what harmony of colors, what a peaceful, calm, and elevated feeling!

On the lower floor, under the balcony, the windows were probably open, because women’s voices and laughter could be heard clearly. Apparently, there was a party there.

Kovrin made an effort, opened the letter, and, going into his room, read:

“Just now my father died. I owe this to you, since you killed him. Our garden is dying, strangers have already taken charge of it, that is, the very thing poor father was so afraid of is happening. I owe this to you too. I hate you with all my soul and wish for you to perish as soon as possible. Oh, how I suffer! My soul is burning with unbearable pain… Damn you. I took you for an extraordinary person, for a genius, I loved you, but you turned out to be insane…”

Kovrin could not read any further, tore up the letter, and threw it away. A restlessness like fear took hold of him. Behind the screens Varvara Nikolaevna was sleeping, and he could hear her breathing. Women’s voices and laughter came from the lower floor, but he had such a feeling as if there were no living souls in the whole hotel except him. Because the unhappy, grief-stricken Tanya was cursing him in her letter and wishing for his ruin, he felt haunted, and he kept glancing at the door, as if afraid that once again the room might be entered and taken over by that unknown force which in just a couple of years had caused so much destruction in his life and in the lives of those close to him.

From experience he already knew that when the nerves were acting up, the best remedy for them was work. He needed to sit down at the table and force himself, no matter what, to focus on some single thought. He took from his red briefcase the notebook on which there was a rough outline of a short compilatory work,devised by him in case Crimea should turn out boring without anything to do. He sat down at the table and took up this outline, and it seemed to him that his calm, submissive, indifferent mood was returning. The notebook with the outline even set him thinking about worldly vanity. He thought about how much life takes for those trifling or quite ordinary blessings it can give a person. For example, in order to get a chair before forty, to be an ordinary professor, to expound ordinary and also borrowed thoughts in a dull, tedious, heavy style, in short, to reach the position of a mediocre scholar, he, Kovrin, had to study for fifteen years, work day and night, endure a severe mental illness, survive an unhappy marriage, and go through many sorts of foolish and unjust things it would be pleasant not to remember. Kovrin now clearly realized that he was mediocre, and he gladly made peace with that, since, in his opinion, everyone should be content with what they are.

     The outline had almost calmed him completely, but the torn letter lay white on the floor and kept him from concentrating. He got up from the table, picked up the scraps of the letter, and threw them out the window, but a light wind blew in from the sea, and the scraps scattered across the windowsill. Anxiety, like fear, took hold of him again, and it began to seem that in the whole hotel, besides him, there was not a single soul… He went out onto the balcony. The bay, as if alive, looked at him with many blue, dark blue, turquoise, and fiery eyes and beckoned to him. In fact, it was hot and stifling, and it would not hurt to go for a swim.

     Suddenly, on the lower floor beneath the balcony, a violin began to play, and two tender female voices started singing. It was something familiar. The romance they were singing downstairs told of some girl, sick with imagination, who heard mysterious sounds at night in the garden and decided that it was sacred harmony, incomprehensible to us mortals… Kovrin caught his breath, and his heart clenched with sorrow, and a marvelous, sweet joy, long forgotten by him, trembled in his chest.

     A tall black column, like a whirlwind or a tornado, appeared on the far shore of the bay. Moving with terrible speed across the bay toward the hotel, it grew smaller and darker, and Kovrin barely had time to step aside to make way… The monk, with his gray head uncovered and black eyebrows, barefoot, arms crossed over his chest, swept past and stopped in the middle of the room.

     “Why didn’t you believe me?” he asked reproachfully, looking at Kovrin kindly. “If you had believed me then that you were a genius, these two years would not have passed so sadly and so meagerly.”

     Kovrin already believed that he was God’s chosen one and a genius, he vividly remembered all his former conversations with the black monk and wanted to speak, but blood was flowing from his throat straight onto his chest, and, not knowing what to do, he ran his hands over his chest, and his cuffs became wet with blood. He wanted to call Varvara Nikolaevna, who was sleeping behind the screens, made an effort and said:

     “Tanya!”

     He fell to the floor and, rising on his hands, called again:

     “Tanya!”

     He was calling Tanya, calling the great garden with its luxuriant flowers sprinkled with dew, calling the park, the pines with their shaggy roots, the rye field, his marvelous science, his youth, his courage, his joy, calling life, which was so beautiful. He saw on the floor near his face a large pool of blood and could no longer utter a single word from weakness, but an indescribable, boundless happiness filled his whole being. Down below the balcony they were playing a serenade, and the black monk was whispering to him that he was a genius and that he was dying only because his weak human body had already lost its balance and could no longer serve as a shell for the genius.

     When Varvara Nikolaevna woke up and came out from behind the screens, Kovrin was already dead, and a blissful smile had frozen on his face.

     VOLODYA

     On one of the summer Sundays, at about five in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old youth, ugly, sickly, and timid,Sitting in the gazebo at the Shumikhinas' dacha, he was bored. His unhappy thoughts ran in three directions. First, the next day, Monday, he had to take a math exam; he knew that if he failed to solve the written problem tomorrow, he would be expelled, since he had been in sixth grade for two years and had a yearly algebra mark of 2 3/4. Second, his stay with the Shumikhins, people who were rich and pretended to aristocracy, caused constant pain to his pride. It seemed to him that m-me Shumikhina and her nieces looked at him and his maman as poor relatives and hangers-on, that they did not respect maman and laughed at her. Once he accidentally overheard m-me Shumikhina telling her cousin Anna Fedorovna on the terrace that his maman was still trying to look young and make herself beautiful, that she never paid her losses and had a taste for other people's boots and other people's tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to go to the Shumikhins, described to her what an insulting role she was playing among those people, tried to persuade her, talked insolently, but she, frivolous, spoiled, having lived through two fortunes in her time - her own and her husband's, always drawn to high society, did not understand him, and Volodya had to escort her to the hated dacha two or three times a week. 

Third, the young man could not for a minute get rid of a strange, unpleasant feeling that was completely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with the Shumikhins' cousin and guest, Anna Fedorovna. She was a lively, loud, and laughing lady, about thirty years old, healthy, sturdy, rosy, with round shoulders, a round fat chin, and a constant smile on her thin lips. She was not beautiful and not young - Volodya knew that perfectly well, but for some reason he could not help thinking about her, looking at her when, playing croquet, she shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back, or when, after a long laugh and running up and down the stairs, she collapsed into an armchair and, closing her eyes, breathing hard, pretended that her chest was cramped and stifled. She was married. Her husband, a solid architect, came to the dacha once a week, slept perfectly well, and went back to the city. Volodya's strange feeling began with his suddenly hating this architect for no reason and feeling joy every time he left for the city. 

Now, sitting in the gazebo and thinking about tomorrow's exam and about maman, who was being laughed at, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (that was what the Shumikhins called Anna Fedorovna), to hear her laughter, the rustle of her dress... This desire was not like that pure, poetic love he knew from novels and dreamed of every evening when he went to bed; it was strange, incomprehensible, he was ashamed of it and afraid of it, as of something very bad and unclean, something hard to admit even to himself... 

"This isn't love," he told himself. "No one falls in love with thirty-year-olds and married women... It's just a little affair... Yes, an affair..." 

Thinking about an affair, he remembered his helpless shyness, his lack of a mustache, his freckles, his narrow eyes, and mentally placed himself next to Nyuta - and the pair seemed impossible to him; then he would quickly imagine himself handsome, bold, witty, dressed in the very latest fashion... 

In the thick of his fantasies, when he was sitting hunched over and looking at the ground in a dark corner of the gazebo, light footsteps were heard. Someone was walking slowly along the path. Soon the footsteps died away and something white flashed at the entrance. 

"Is anyone here?" asked a female voice. 

Volodya recognized the voice and raised his head in alarm. 

"Who's there?" Nyuta asked, coming into the gazebo. "Oh, it's you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? And how can you keep thinking, thinking, thinking all the time... you could go crazy that way!" 

Volodya stood up and looked at Nyuta in confusion. She had just returned from the bathhouse. A sheet and a shaggy towel were hanging from her shoulders, and from under the white silk scarf on her head, wet hair peeked out, stuck to her forehead. A damp, cool smell of bathhouse and almond soap came from her. She was out of breath from walking fast.The top button of her blouse was undone, so the yo

Volodya listened and, in heavy, strained thought, scratched his temple. 

     - Only very proud people keep silent and love solitude, - Nyuta continued, pulling his hand away from his temple. - You are a proud man, Volodya. Why are you looking askance? Be so kind as to look me straight in the face! Oh come on, seal! 

     Volodya decided to speak. Wanting to smile, he twitched his lower lip, blinked, and again reached for his temple. 

     - I... I love you! - he said. 

     Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed. 

     - What do I hear?! - she sang, the way opera singers do when they hear something terrible. - What? What did you say? Repeat it, repeat it... 

     - I... I love you! - Volodya repeated. 

     And now, without any participation of his own will, understanding and comprehending nothing, he took half a step toward Nyuta and took her hand above the wrist. His eyes grew dim and tears came to them, the whole world turned into one big shaggy towel smelling of the bathhouse. 

     - Bravo, bravo! - he heard cheerful laughter. - Why are you silent? I want you to talk! Well? 

     Seeing that no one was stopping him from holding her hand, Volodya looked at Nyuta's laughing face and awkwardly, uncomfortably took her by the waist with both hands, so that the hands of both his arms met on her back. He held her by the waist with both hands, and she, with her hands thrown behind her head and showing the dimples at her elbows, was fixing her hair under her scarf and speaking in a calm voice: 

     - One must, Volodya, be nimble, courteous, charming, and one can become that only under the influence of female company. However, what an unpleasant... wicked face you have. You need to talk, laugh... Yes, Volodya, don't be a bore, you are young and there will still be time for philosophizing. Well, let me go, I am leaving. Let me go! 

     She effortlessly freed her waist and, humming something, left the gazebo. Volodya was left alone. He smoothed his hair, smiled, and walked from corner to corner three times, then sat down on the bench and smiled once more. He was unbearably ashamed, so much so that he even wondered that human shame could reach such sharpness and force. From shame he smiled, whispered some incoherent words, and gestured. 

     He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and above all ashamed that he had dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist, although neither by age, nor by his outward qualities, nor by his social position did he, as it seemed to him, have any right to do so. 

     He jumped up, left the gazebo, and, without looking back, went deeper into the garden, farther from the house. 

     "Oh, if only I could leave here as soon as possible! - he thought, grabbing his head. God, as soon as possible!" 

     The train on which Volodya was supposed to travel with maman left at eight forty. There were about three hours left until the train, but he would have gladly gone to the station right away, without waiting for maman. 

     At eight o'clock he was approaching the house. His whole figure expressed determination: whatever happens, happens! He had resolved to go in boldly, look straight ahead, speak loudly, no matter what. 

     He passed through the terrace, the large hall, the drawing room, and stopped in the last room to catch his breath. From here he could hear tea being drunk in the neighboring dining room. M-me Shumikhina, maman, and Nyuta were talking about something and laughing. 

     Volodya listened. 

     - I assure you! - Nyuta was saying. - I didn't believe my own eyes! When he started declaring his love to me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I didn't recognize him. And you know, he has a manner! When he said he was in love with me, there was something beastlike in his face, like a Circassian. 

     - Really! - maman gasped, bursting into a long laugh. - Really! How much he reminds me of his father! 

     Volodya ran back and dashed out into the fresh air. "And how can they speak about this out loud! - he suffered, wringing his hands and looking at the sky in horror. - They speak out loud, coolly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?" 

     But he had to go into the house, come what may. He walked along the alley three times, calmed down a little, and went into the house.Why don’t you come to have tea on time? asked m-me Shumikhina sternly.

My fault, I... I have to go, he muttered, not raising his eyes. Maman, it’s already eight o’clock!

Go yourself, my dear, said maman languidly, I’m staying overnight with Lily. Goodbye, my friend... Let me bless you...

She crossed her son and said in French, addressing Nyuta:

He looks a little like Lermontov... Doesn’t he?

After saying goodbye as best he could and not looking at anyone’s face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later he was already walking along the road to the station and was glad of it. Now he was neither afraid nor ashamed, and it was easy and free to breathe.

Half a verst from the station he sat down on a stone by the road and began looking at the sun, which had hidden more than halfway behind the embankment. At the station, lights had already been lit here and there, one dim green light flickered, but the train could still not be seen. Volodya was pleased to sit, not move, and listen to how evening was gradually setting in. The dusk of the gazebo, the footsteps, the smell of the bathhouse, laughter and waist, all this appeared in his imagination with astonishing clarity and all this was no longer so frightening and important as before...

"Nonsense... She didn’t pull away her hand and laughed when I held her by the waist," he thought. "That means she liked it. If it had been unpleasant to her, she would have gotten angry..."

And now Volodya felt annoyed that back there, in the gazebo, he had not been brave enough. He regretted leaving so stupidly, and he was already sure that if that incident repeated itself, he would be bolder and would look at things more simply.

And it would not be hard for the incident to repeat itself. At the Shumikhins’, they walk around for a long time after dinner. If Volodya goes for a walk with Nyuta in the dark garden, then there it is, the chance!

"I’ll come back," he thought, "and leave tomorrow on the morning train... I’ll say I missed the train."

And he came back... M-me Shumikhina, maman, Nyuta, and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace playing whist. When Volodya lied to them that he had missed the train, they worried that he might miss the exam tomorrow and advised him to get up earlier. All the time they were playing cards, he sat to one side, gazed greedily at Nyuta, and waited... A plan was already ready in his head: he would go up to Nyuta in the dark, take her by the hand, then hug her; no need to say anything, since both of them would understand everything without words.

But after dinner the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and continued playing cards. They played until one in the morning and then went off to sleep.

"How stupid all this is!" Volodya grumbled as he got into bed. "But never mind, I’ll wait until tomorrow... Tomorrow again in the gazebo. It’s nothing..."

He did not try to fall asleep, but sat in bed with his arms around his knees and thought. The thought of the exam was unpleasant to him. He had already decided that he would be expelled and that there was nothing terrible in that expulsion. On the contrary, everything was very good, even very. Tomorrow he would be free as a bird, put on civilian clothes, smoke openly, come here and court Nyuta whenever he wanted; and he would no longer be a gymnasium student, but a "young man." As for the rest, what is called a career and the future, it was all so clear: Volodya would join the volunteers, become a telegraph operator, and finally work at a pharmacy, where he would rise to pharmacist... were there not plenty of jobs? An hour or two passed, and he was still sitting and thinking...

At three o’clock, when it was already dawning, the door creaked softly and maman came into the room.

Aren’t you asleep? she asked, yawning. Sleep, sleep, I’m just for a minute... I’m only taking the drops...

Why do you need them?

The poor Lily has spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow...

She took a bottle with something from the cabinet, went to the window, read the label, and went out.

Maria Leontyevna, those aren’t the drops! Volodya heard a woman’s voice a minute later. That’s lily of the valley, and Lily is asking for morphine. Is your son asleep? Ask him to find it...

It was Nyuta’s voice. Volodya went cold. He quickly put on his trousers, threw his overcoat over his shoulders, and went to the door.

Do you understand? Morphine! Nyuta explained in a whisper. It should be written in Latin.Wake Volodya up, he’ll find it...

Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was wearing the same blouse she had worn when she went bathing. Her hair was uncombed, falling over her shoulders, her face sleepy, darkened by the dusk...

- Volodya is not asleep... - she said. - Volodya, please look for the morphine in the cabinet, dear! What a nuisance this Lili is... It’s always something with her.

Maman muttered something, yawned, and left.

- Go on, look, - said Nyuta. - Why are you standing there?

Volodya went to the little cabinet, knelt down, and began sorting through bottles and boxes of medicine. His hands were trembling, and in his chest and stomach there was a feeling as if cold waves were running through all his insides. The smell of ether, carbolic acid, and various herbs, which he was grabbing at with trembling hands for no reason at all and which were spilling because of it, made him feel stifled and dizzy.

"It seems maman has gone," he thought. "Good... good..."

- Just now... This seems to be morphine... - Volodya said, reading the word "morph..." on one of the labels. - Here you are!

Nyuta was standing in the doorway so that one of her feet was in the corridor and the other in his room. She was fixing her hair, which was hard to fix because it was so thick and long! - and was looking at Volodya absentmindedly. In the loose blouse, sleepy, with her hair down, in the meager light that came into the room from the white but still unlit sky, she seemed to Volodya enchanting, luxurious... Bewitched, trembling all over and with pleasure remembering how he had embraced that marvelous body in the gazebo, he handed her the drops and said:

- How you are...

She came into the room.

She came back...

"To hell with it, all of it!" he thought.

"Quite right... Ugly duckling."

     - Oh, that means nothing, - said the Frenchman, smiling broadly. - I'll go get the bread myself. Oh, it's nothing!

     - After all, Lily Shumikhina is my relative... - she said. - Her late husband, General Shumikhin, is a cousin of my husband. And she herself was born Baroness Kolb... 

     - Maman, that's not true! - Vladimir said irritably. - Why lie? 

     He knew perfectly well that maman was telling the truth; in her story about General Shumikhin and the born Baroness Kolb there was not a single word of lies, but nevertheless he still felt that she was lying. The lie could be felt in the way she spoke, in her expression, in her gaze, in everything. 

     - You are lying! - Vladimir repeated and struck the table with his fist so hard that all the dishes trembled and maman spilled her tea. - Why are you telling us about generals and baronesses? It's all a lie! 

     The music teacher was flustered and coughed into her handkerchief, pretending that she had choked, and maman burst into tears. 

     "Where should I go?" - Vladimir thought. 

     He was already on the street; it was embarrassing to go to his friends. Again, two little English girls came back to his mind at the wrong moment... He walked from corner to corner of the "common room" and entered Augustin Mikhailych's room. It smelled strongly of essential oils and glycerin soap. On the table, on the windowsills, and even on the chairs there were many bottles, glasses, and little glasses with colored liquids.      - No, Evgenia Andreevna, he is too spoiled! - maman said in a singsong voice. - There is no elder over him, and I am weak and can do nothing. No, I am unhappy!

Taking the barrel out of his mouth, he wiped it on the hem of his overcoat, examined the lock; he had never held a weapon in his hands before in his life... 

     - It seems this should be raised... - he thought. - Yes, it seems so...

Vladimir again put the barrel in his mouth, clenched it with his teeth, and pressed something with his finger. A shot rang out... Something struck Vladimir with terrible force on the back of the head, and he fell onto the table, face first into the glasses and bottles. Then he saw his late father in a top hat with a wide black ribbon, who had worn mourning in Menton for some lady, suddenly wrap him in both arms, and the two of them flew into some very dark, deep abyss.

When in the provincial town of S., newcomers complained of the boredom and monotony of life, the local residents, as if excusing themselves, said that on the contrary, life in S. was very good, that S. had a library, a theater, a club, there were balls, and, finally, there were intelligent, interesting, pleasant families one could become acquainted with. This family lived on the main street, near the governor’s, in their own house. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a stout, handsome brunette with sideburns, organized amateur performances for charity, played old generals himself, and coughed very amusingly while doing so. He knew many anecdotes, charades, proverbs, liked to joke and make sharp remarks, and always had such an expression that it was impossible to tell whether he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a thin, attractive lady in pince nez, wrote stories and novels and gladly read them aloud to her guests. Their daughter, Ekaterina Ivanovna, was a young girl who played the piano. In short, each member of the family had some talent of his or her own. The Turkins received guests warmly and showed them their talents cheerfully, with heartfelt simplicity. Their large stone house was spacious and cool in summer, half the windows looked out onto an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests sat in the house, knives clattered in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onions drifted from the yard, and every time this promised a plentiful and delicious supper.

And Doctor Startsev, Dmitry Ionych, when he had just been appointed zemstvo doctor and settled in Dyalizh, nine versts from S., was also told that as an educated person he needed to make the acquaintance of the Turkins. One winter day in the street he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich; they talked about the weather, the theater, cholera, and an invitation followed. In spring, on a holiday, it was Ascension Day, after seeing patients, Startsev went to town to amuse himself a little and, while at it, buy himself a few things. He walked on foot, unhurriedly (he still had no horses of his own), and all the while he hummed: When I had not yet drunk tears from the cup of existence…

In town he had lunch, took a walk in the garden, then Ivan Petrovich’s invitation somehow came back to mind of its own accord, and he decided to go to the Turkins and see what sort of people they were.

“Welcome,” said Ivan Petrovich, greeting him on the porch. “I am very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Come, I’ll introduce you to my better half. I tell her, Verochka,” he went on, introducing the doctor to his wife, “I tell her that he has no Roman right to sit at home in his hospital, he must give his free time to society. Isn’t that so, my dear?”

“Sit here,” Vera Iosifovna said, seating the guest beside herself. “You may court me. My husband is jealous, he is Othello, but we shall try to behave so that he notices nothing.”

“Oh, you little chick, you little minx…” Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. “You have come just at the right time,” he said again to the guest, “my better half has written a monster of a novel and today she will read it aloud.”

“Jean,” Vera Iosifovna said to her husband, “dites que l'on nous donne du the1.”

Startsev was introduced to Ekaterina Ivanovna, an eighteen-year-old girl, very much like her mother, just as thin and attractive. Her expression was still childlike and her waist slim, delicate; and her virgin, already developed bosom, beautiful and healthy, spoke of spring, real spring. Then they drank tea with jam, honey, sweets, and very tasty cookies that melted in the mouth. As evening approached, guests gradually gathered, and to each of them Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes and said:

“Welcome.”

Then everyone sat in the drawing room, with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began like this: “The frost grew stronger…” The windows were wide open, it was heard how knives clattered in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onions drifted in… In the soft, deep armchairs it was peaceful, the lights flickered so kindly in the dusk of the drawing room; and now, on a summer evening,when voices floated in from the street, with laughter, and the scent of lilac wafted from the yard, it was hard to understand how the frost had grown stronger and how the setting sun lit the snowy plain and the traveler walking alone along the road with its cold rays; Vera Iosifovna read about how a young, beautiful countess set up schools, hospitals, and libraries in her country estate, and how she fell in love with a wandering artist, read about what never happens in life, and yet it was pleasant to listen, comfortable, and such good, calm thoughts kept coming to mind, and one did not want to get up.

     – Not bad... said Ivan Petrovich softly.

     And one of the guests, listening and drifting in thought somewhere very, very far away, said barely audibly:

     – Yes... indeed...

     An hour passed, then another. In the city garden nearby, an orchestra was playing and a choir of singers was singing. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, everyone was silent for about five minutes and listened to Luchinushka, which the choir was singing, and that song conveyed what was not in the novel and what happens in life.

     – Do you publish your works in magazines? asked Starsev Vera Iosifovna.

     – No, she answered, I do not publish anywhere. I write it and hide it in my wardrobe. Why publish it? she explained. We have means.

     And everyone somehow sighed.

     – And now you, Kotik, play something, said Ivan Petrovich to his daughter.

     The lid of the piano was raised, and the sheet music that had been laid out in advance was opened. Yekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands, and then immediately struck again with all her strength, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest trembled, she kept stubbornly hitting the same spot, and it seemed she would not stop until she had driven the keys into the piano. The drawing room filled with thunder; everything was booming, the floor, the ceiling, the furniture... Yekaterina Ivanovna was playing a difficult passage, interesting precisely because it was difficult, long and monotonous, and Starsev, listening, pictured stones tumbling down from a high mountain, tumbling and still tumbling, and he wanted them to stop falling as soon as possible, and at the same time Yekaterina Ivanovna, pink with strain, strong, energetic, with a curl fallen onto her forehead, pleased him very much. After the winter spent in Dyalizh, among the sick and peasants, to sit in the drawing room, look at this young, graceful, and probably pure creature, and listen to these noisy, irritating, but still cultured sounds, was so pleasant, so novel...

     – Well, Kotik, today you played as never before, said Ivan Petrovich with tears in his eyes when his daughter finished and stood up. – You can't do better, even if you died, Denis.

     Everyone surrounded her, congratulated her, marveled, assured her that they had not heard such music for a long time, and she listened in silence, smiling slightly, and triumph was written all over her figure.

     – Wonderful! excellent!

     – Wonderful! said Starsev too, giving in to the general enthusiasm. – Where did you study music? he asked Yekaterina Ivanovna. – At the conservatory?

     – No, I am only planning to go to the conservatory, and for now I studied here, with Madame Zavlovskaya.

     – Did you complete the course at the local gymnasium?

     – Oh no! Vera Iosifovna answered for her. We invited teachers to the house, because in a gymnasium or institute, you will agree, there could be bad influences; while a girl is growing up, she should be under the influence of her mother alone.

     – And yet I will go to the conservatory, said Yekaterina Ivanovna.

     – No, Kotik loves her mama. Kotik will not upset papa and mama.

     – No, I will go! I will go! said Yekaterina Ivanovna, joking and pouting, and stamped her foot.

     And at dinner Ivan Petrovich was already showing his talents. Smiling only with his eyes, he told anecdotes, made witty remarks, proposed funny problems and solved them himself, and all the while spoke in his extraordinary language, developed through long practice in wit and evidently long since become habitual to him: bolshinsky, not bad, you were twisted, thank you...

     But that was not all. When the guests, full and satisfied, gathered in the hall, sorting out their coats and canes, the footman Pavlusha was fussing around them, or,As for his name here, Pava, a boy about fourteen, with a cropped haircut and full cheeks.

	– Come on, Pava, do it! Ivan Petrovich said to him.

	Pava struck a pose, raised his hand high, and said in a tragic tone:

	– Die, unfortunate one!

	And they all burst out laughing.

	“Interesting,” thought Startsev, going out into the street.

	He went into the restaurant once more and drank some beer, then set off on foot to Dyalizh. He walked and sang the whole way: Your voice for me, so gentle and so languid…

	After walking nine versts and then lying down to sleep, he felt not the slightest fatigue, and on the contrary, it seemed to him that he would gladly walk another twenty versts.

	“Not bad…” he remembered as he fell asleep, and laughed.

II

	Startsev kept planning to visit the Turkins, but there was so much work at the hospital that he simply could not find a free hour. More than a year passed like this in labor and loneliness; but then a letter arrived from the city in a blue envelope…

	Vera Iosifovna had long suffered from migraines, but recently, when Kotik kept threatening every day that she would leave for the conservatory, the attacks had started recurring more and more often. All the city doctors had been to the Turkins’; at last it was the zemstvo doctor’s turn. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter, asking him to come and ease her suffering. Startsev came, and after that began visiting the Turkins often, very often… He really did help Vera Iosifovna a little, and she told all her guests that he was an extraordinary, amazing doctor. But by then he was going to the Turkins no longer because of her migraines…

	A holiday. Yekaterina Ivanovna had finished her long, exhausting exercises on the piano. Then they sat for a long time in the dining room drinking tea, and Ivan Petrovich told something funny. But then came the bell; someone had to go to the front hall to greet a guest; Startsev took advantage of the moment of confusion and said to Yekaterina Ivanovna in a whisper, very excited:

	– For God’s sake, I beg you, don’t torment me, let’s go into the garden!

	She shrugged, as if puzzled and not understanding what he wanted from her, but she got up and went.

	– You play the piano for three or four hours, he said, following her, then sit with your mother, and there’s no way at all to talk to you. Give me at least fifteen minutes, I beg you.

	Autumn was approaching, and in the old garden it was quiet, sad, and dark leaves lay along the alleys. It was already getting dark early.

	– I haven’t seen you for a whole week, Startsev went on, and if you only knew what suffering that is! Let’s sit down. Listen to me.

	Both of them had a favorite place in the garden: a bench under an old broad maple tree. And now they sat down on that bench.

	– What do you want? Yekaterina Ivanovna asked dryly, in a businesslike tone.

	– I haven’t seen you for a whole week, I haven’t heard you for so long. I passionately want, I long for your voice. Speak.

	She delighted him with her freshness, the naive expression in her eyes and cheeks. Even in the way her dress sat on her, he saw something extraordinarily charming, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. And at the same time, despite this naivety, she seemed very intelligent to him and advanced beyond her years. With her he could talk about literature, art, anything at all, could complain to her about life, about people, although during serious conversation she would sometimes suddenly start laughing at the wrong moment or run into the house. Like almost all girls of that kind, she read a lot (in general, very little was read in S., and in the local library they even used to say that if it weren’t for the girls and the young Jews, they might as well close the library); Startsev liked this endlessly, and every time he would ask her with excitement what she had been reading lately, and, enchanted, would listen when she told him.

	– What did you read this week while we weren’t seeing each other? he asked now. Tell me, please.

	– I read Pisemsky.

	– What exactly?

	– A Thousand Souls, Kotik answered. And how funny Pisemsky’s first name was: Aleksey Feofilaktych!

	– Where are you going? Startsev cried in horror when she suddenly stood up and went toward the house. I need to talk to you.I must explain myself... Stay with me for at least five minutes! I beg you!

     She stopped, as if she wanted to say something, then awkwardly slipped a note into his hand and ran into the house, and there sat down at the piano again.

     "Today, at eleven o'clock in the evening," read Startsev, "meet at the cemetery by Demetti's monument."

     "Well, that is really not very smart," he thought, coming to himself. "What does the cemetery have to do with it? What for?"

     It was clear: Kotik was teasing. Who, really, would seriously think of arranging a meeting at night, far outside the city, in a cemetery, when this could easily be arranged on the street, in the city garden? And was it fitting for him, a zemstvo doctor, a sensible, solid man, to sigh, receive little notes, trudge around cemeteries, do foolish things at which even gymnasium boys now laughed? Where would this romance lead? What would his colleagues say when they found out? That was what Startsev thought, wandering around the club near the tables, and at half past ten he suddenly got up and went to the cemetery.

He already had his own pair of horses and the coachman Panteleimon in a velvet vest. The moon was shining. It was quiet, warm, but warm in an autumn way. In the suburb, near the slaughterhouses, dogs were howling. Startsev left the horses on the edge of town, in one of the alleys, and went to the cemetery on foot. "Everyone has their own oddities," he thought. "Kotik is odd too and, who knows, maybe she is not joking, maybe she will come," and he surrendered himself to this weak, empty hope, and it intoxicated him.

     He walked half a verst across the field. The cemetery could be seen in the distance as a dark strip, like a forest or a large garden. A white stone fence appeared, then the gates... In the moonlight, one could read on the gates: "The hour is coming when..." Startsev went through the little gate, and the first thing he saw was white crosses and monuments on both sides of the broad alley and black shadows from them and from the poplars; and all around, far off, white and black could be seen, and the sleepy trees bent their branches over the white ground. It seemed that it was brighter here than in the field; the maple leaves, like paws, stood out sharply against the yellow sand of the alleys and on the slabs, and the inscriptions on the monuments were clear. At first Startsev was struck by what he now saw for the first time in his life and what, probably, he would never see again: a world unlike anything else, a world where the moonlight is so lovely and soft, as if this were its cradle, where there is no life, no and no, but in every dark poplar, in every grave, one can feel the presence of a mystery promising a quiet, beautiful, eternal life. From the slabs and withered flowers, together with the autumn smell of leaves, there breathes forgiveness, sorrow, and peace.

     All around was silence; the stars looked down from the sky in deep humility, and Startsev's steps sounded so sharply and out of place. And only when the clocks began to strike in the church and he imagined himself dead, buried here forever, did it seem to him that someone was looking at him, and for a minute he thought that this was not peace and not silence, but the dull anguish of nonexistence, suppressed despair...

     Demetti's monument was in the form of a chapel, with an angel on top; once, an Italian opera had passed through S., one of the singers had died, she was buried, and this monument was erected. In the city, no one remembered her anymore, but the lamp above the entrance reflected the moonlight and seemed to be burning.

     There was no one there. And who would come here at midnight? But Startsev waited, and, as if the moonlight were heating his passion, he waited with passion and pictured kisses, embraces in his imagination. He sat by the monument for about half an hour, then walked along the side alleys, hat in hand, waiting and thinking about how many women and girls were buried here in these graves, women and girls who were beautiful, charming, who loved, who burned with passion at night, surrendering themselves to caresses. How badly mother nature jokes with a person in essence, how insulting it is to realize that! Startsev thought this, and at the same time he wanted to shout that he wanted, that he was waiting for love at any cost; before him there were no longer pieces of marble, but beautiful bodies, he saw forms that shyly hid in the shadows of the trees, felt warmth, and this longing was becoming painful...

     And as if a curtain had fallen, the moon went under the clouds,and suddenly everything went dark all around. Starsev barely found the gate, it was already dark as on an autumn night, then for an hour and a half he wandered, looking for the lane where he had left his horses.

     I’m tired, I can barely stand on my feet, he said to Panteleimon.

     And, settling into the carriage with pleasure, he thought:

     Oh, I mustn’t get fat!

     

III

     

     The next day in the evening he went to the Turkins’ to make his proposal. But that proved inconvenient, since Yekaterina Ivanovna was having her hair done in her room by the barber. She was getting ready to go to the club for a dance evening.

     He had to sit for a long time again in the dining room and drink tea. Ivan Petrovich, seeing that his guest was thoughtful and bored, took some notes from his vest pocket, read a funny letter from the German manager about how all the locks on the estate had gone bad and the shyness had fallen apart.

     They must be giving a considerable dowry, Starsev thought, listening absently.

     After a sleepless night he was in a dazed state, as if he had been drugged with something sweet and soporific; his soul was hazy but joyful, warm, and at the same time some cold, heavy little piece in his head reasoned:

     Stop now, before it is too late! Is she the right match for you? She is spoiled, capricious, sleeps till two in the afternoon, and you are a church deacon’s son, a zemstvo doctor…

     So what? he thought. Let it be.

     Besides, if you marry her, the little piece continued, her relatives will make you quit zemstvo service and live in the city.

     So what? he thought. In the city, then in the city. We’ll get the dowry, set up a household…

     At last Yekaterina Ivanovna came in in a ball dress, with a low neckline, pretty, neat, and Starsev was charmed and so delighted that he could not say a single word, and only looked at her and laughed.

     She began to say goodbye, and he, there was no reason for him to stay any longer, got up, saying it was time for him to go home: the patients were waiting.

     Nothing to be done, said Ivan Petrovich, go on then, you’ll give Kotik a ride to the club while you’re at it.

     It was drizzling in the yard, it was very dark, and only Panteleimon’s hoarse coughing could tell where the horses were. The carriage top was put up.

     I walk on the carpet, you go on while you lie, said Ivan Petrovich, putting his daughter into the carriage, he goes on while he lies… Off you go! Goodbye then!

     They set off.

     I was at the cemetery yesterday, Starsev began. How ungenerous and unmerciful of you…

     You were at the cemetery?

     Yes, I was there and waited for you until nearly two o’clock. I suffered…

     Then suffer, if you don’t understand jokes.

     Yekaterina Ivanovna, pleased that she had so cleverly teased the man in love and that she was loved so much, burst out laughing and suddenly cried out in alarm, because at that very moment the horses were turning sharply into the club gate and the carriage tilted. Starsev put his arm around Yekaterina Ivanovna’s waist; startled, she pressed herself against him, and he could not restrain himself and kissed her passionately on the lips, on the chin, and hugged her tighter.

     Enough, she said dryly.

     And in an instant she was no longer in the carriage, and the policeman near the lit entrance of the club was shouting in an awful voice at Panteleimon:

     Why are you standing there, you crow? Move on!

     Starsev went home, but soon returned. Dressed in someone else’s tailcoat and a stiff white tie, which kept sticking up and wanted to slip off his collar, he was sitting in the club parlor at midnight and speaking to Yekaterina Ivanovna with enthusiasm:

     Oh, how little those who have never loved know! It seems to me that no one has yet described love truly, and it is hardly possible to describe this tender, joyful, tormenting feeling, and whoever has experienced it even once will not convey it in words. What is the use of prefaces, descriptions? What is the use of needless eloquence? My love is boundless… Please, I beg you, Starsev finally said, be my wife!

     Dmitry Ionych, said Yekaterina Ivanovna with a very serious expression after thinking for a moment. Dmitry Ionych, I am very grateful to you for the honor, I respect you, but…

She stood up and kept standing, and said, excuse me, I cannot be your wife. Let us speak seriously. Dmitry Ionych, you know, above all in life I love art, I am madly in love with music, I adore it, I have devoted my whole life to it. I want to be an artist, I want fame, success, freedom, and you want me to keep living in this town, to keep living this empty, useless life, which has become unbearable to me. To become a wife, oh no, excuse me! A person must strive for a higher, brilliant goal, and family life would bind me forever. Dmitry Ionych (she smiled a little, because in saying Dmitry Ionych she remembered Alexey Feofilaktych), Dmitry Ionych, you are a kind, noble, intelligent person, you are the best of all... tears came to her eyes, I sympathize with you with all my soul, but... but you will understand...

And, so as not to cry, she turned away and left the drawing room.

Starcev’s heart stopped pounding anxiously. After leaving the club and going out into the street, he first of all tore off his stiff tie and drew a deep breath. He was a little ashamed and his pride was hurt, he had not expected a refusal, and he could not believe that all his dreams, yearnings, and hopes had led him to such a silly end, as if in a little play at an amateur performance. And he felt sorry for his feeling, for this love of his, so sorry that he seemed ready either to burst into tears or to hit Panteleimon’s broad back with his umbrella with all his strength.

For about three days everything slipped through his fingers, he did not eat, did not sleep, but when he heard the rumor that Ekaterina Ivanovna had gone to Moscow to enter the conservatory, he calmed down and resumed his former life.

Later, whenever he remembered how he had wandered around the cemetery or how he had ridden all over town looking for a tailcoat, he would lazily stretch and say:

- What a lot of trouble, though!

 

IV

 

Four years passed. In town, Starcev already had a large practice. Every morning he hurriedly saw patients at home in Dyalizh, then went off to see city patients, no longer in a pair, but in a troika with bells, and returned home late at night. He had grown stout, become comfortable, and walked on foot reluctantly, as he suffered from shortness of breath. Panteleimon had also grown stout, and the broader he grew, the more sadly he sighed and complained about his bitter lot: driving had worn him out!

Starcev visited different houses and met many people, but did not become close to anyone. The townspeople, with their talk, their views on life, and even their appearance, irritated him. Experience gradually taught him that while you are playing cards with a townsman or having a bite with him, he is a peaceful, good-natured, and even not stupid person, but as soon as you start talking to him about something inedible, for example, politics or science, he gets confused or starts up such a philosophy, so dull and evil, that you can only wave your hand and walk away. When Starcev tried to talk even with a liberal townsman, for example, about how humanity, thank God, is moving forward and that in time it will do without passports and without the death penalty, the townsman would look at him askance and distrustfully and ask, "So then anyone can cut anybody down in the street?" And when Starcev, in company, at dinner or tea, said that people need to work, that one cannot live without labor, everyone took it as a reproach and began to get angry and argue pesteringly. In spite of all this, the townspeople did nothing, absolutely nothing, and were interested in nothing, and there was no way to think of what to talk to them about. So Starcev avoided conversation and only ate and played vint, and when he found himself at some house on a family holiday and was invited to have a meal, he would sit down and eat in silence, looking into his plate; and everything that was said at such times was uninteresting, unfair, stupid, he felt irritated, grew agitated, but kept silent, and because he always kept sternly silent and looked into his plate, they nicknamed him in town the puffed-up Pole, although he had never been Polish.

He avoided entertainments like theater and concerts, but he played vint every evening, for about three hours, with pleasure. He had one more amusement,into which he had slipped unnoticed, little by little, was this: in the evenings to take from his pockets the papers earned by practice, and, it happened, the papers, yellow and green, smelling of perfume, vinegar, incense, and blubber, had stuffed all his pockets with about seventy rubles; and when he had collected several hundred, he took them to the Mutual Credit Society and put them there in a checking account.

     During all four years after Yekaterina Ivanovna left, he was at the Turkins' only twice, at Vera Iosifovna's invitation, as she was still being treated for migraine. Every summer Yekaterina Ivanovna came to visit her parents, but he never saw her once; somehow it never happened.

     But now four years had passed. On one quiet, warm morning a letter was brought to the hospital. Vera Iosifovna wrote to Dmitry Ionych that she missed him very much, and asked him to come to her without fail and relieve her suffering, and besides, today was her birthday. At the bottom was a postscript: “I join Mama's request. K.”

     Startsev thought it over and went to the Turkins in the evening.

     “Ah, hello there!” Ivan Petrovich greeted him, smiling only with his eyes. “Bonjourte.”

     Vera Iosifovna, already quite aged, with white hair, shook Startsev's hand, sighed affectatiously, and said:

     “You, doctor, do not want to take care of me, you never come to see us, I am already too old for you. But now the young one has come, perhaps she will be happier.”

     And Kotik? She had grown thinner, paler, more beautiful and slimmer; but now she was Yekaterina Ivanovna, not Kotik; there was no longer the former freshness and the look of childish naïveté. In her gaze and her manner there was something new, timid and guilty, as if here, in the Turkins' house, she no longer felt at home.

     “How many years, how many winters!” she said, offering Startsev her hand, and it was clear that her heart was beating anxiously; and, looking intently and curiously into his face, she went on: “How you have filled out! You've tanned, matured, but in general you have changed little.”

     And now he liked her, liked her very much, but something was already missing in her, or something was superfluous, he himself could not have said what exactly, but something was already keeping him from feeling as before. He did not like her pallor, her new expression, her weak smile, her voice, and after a little while he no longer liked the dress, the armchair in which she sat, something in the past, when he had nearly married her. He remembered his love, the dreams and hopes that had stirred him four years ago, and he felt awkward.

     They drank tea with sweet pie. Then Vera Iosifovna read a novel aloud, reading about what never happens in real life, and Startsev listened, looking at her gray, beautiful head and waiting for her to finish.

     “Talentless,” he thought, “not the one who cannot write stories, but the one who writes them and cannot hide it.”

     “Not bad,” Ivan Petrovich said. Then Yekaterina Ivanovna played the piano loudly and for a long time, and when she finished, she was thanked and admired for a long time.

     “It's a good thing I didn't marry her,” Startsev thought.

     She looked at him and seemed to be waiting for him to suggest going into the garden, but he said nothing.

     “Well, let's talk,” she said, coming up to him. “How have you been? What's new with you? How? I kept thinking about you all these days,” she went on nervously, “I wanted to send you a letter, I wanted to come myself to see you in Dyalyzh, and I had already decided to go, but then I changed my mind, God knows how you now feel about me. I waited for you today with such excitement. For God's sake, let's go into the garden.” They went into the garden and sat there on a bench under the old maple, just as four years ago. It was dark.

     “And how have you been living?” Yekaterina Ivanovna asked.

     “Nothing, we're living little by little,” Startsev replied.

     And he could think of nothing else. They were silent.

     “I am upset,” Yekaterina Ivanovna said, covering her face with her hands, “but don't pay attention. I feel so good at home, I'm so glad to see everyone, and I can't get used to it. So many memories! It seemed to me that we would talk nonstop, until morning.Now he saw her face up close, her shining eyes, and here, in the darkness, she seemed younger than in the room, and it even seemed as if her former childlike expression had returned. And indeed, she was looking at him with naive curiosity, as if she wanted to examine more closely and understand the man who once loved her so ardently, so tenderly, and so unhappily; her eyes thanked him for that love. And he remembered everything that had been, every last detail, how he used to wander through the cemetery, how later toward morning, exhausted, he would return home, and suddenly he felt sad and sorry for the past. A little flame flickered in his soul.

     – Do you remember when I saw you off to the club for an evening? he said. Then it was raining, it was dark...

     The little flame kept growing in his soul, and he already wanted to talk, to complain about life...

     – Eh! he said with a sigh. You ask how I’m doing. How are we doing here? Not at all. We grow old, put on weight, and sink lower. Day and night, and the days are gone, life passes dimly, without impressions, without thoughts... By day, making money, and in the evening, the club, a society of gamblers, alcoholics, and wheezers whom I can’t stand. What’s good about that?

     – But you have work, a noble purpose in life. You used to love talking about your hospital. Back then I was some kind of strange girl, imagining myself a great pianist. Now all young ladies play the piano, and I played too, like everyone else, and there was nothing special in me; I’m just as much a pianist as mama is a writer. And of course I didn’t understand you then, but later, in Moscow, I often thought about you. I thought only about you. What happiness it is to be a zemstvo doctor, to help sufferers, to serve the people. What happiness! repeated Yekaterina Ivanovna in excitement. When I thought about you in Moscow, you seemed to me so ideal, so exalted...

     Startsev remembered the banknotes he used to take out of his pockets in the evenings with such pleasure, and the little flame in his soul went out.

     He stood up to go to the house. She took his arm.

     – You are the best of all the people I have known in my life, she continued. We will see each other, talk, won’t we? Promise me. I’m not a pianist, I don’t deceive myself anymore, and I won’t play for you or talk about music.

     When they entered the house and Startsev saw in the evening light her face and her sad, grateful, searching eyes turned toward him, he felt uneasy and thought again:

     “It’s a good thing I didn’t get married then.”

     He began to say goodbye.

     – You have no Roman right to leave without supper, Ivan Petrovich said, seeing him off. That is extremely perpendicular of you. Come on, perform! he said, turning in the hall to Pava.

     Pava, no longer a boy but a young man with a mustache, struck a pose, raised his hand and said in a tragic voice:

     – Die, unhappy one!

     All this irritated Startsev. As he got into the carriage and looked at the dark house and garden, which had once been so dear and precious to him, he remembered everything at once – Vera Iosifovna’s novels, Kotik’s noisy playing, Ivan Petrovich’s wit, and Pava’s tragic pose, and thought that if the most talented people in the whole city were so untalented, then what must the city itself be like.

     Three days later Pava brought a letter from Yekaterina Ivanovna.

     “You are not coming to see us. Why? she wrote. I’m afraid you have changed toward us; I’m afraid, and I’m frightened by the mere thought of it. So reassure me, come and say that everything is fine.

     I need to talk to you. Yours, E. T.”

     He read the letter, thought for a moment, and said to Pava:

     “Tell them, my dear fellow, that today I can’t come, I’m very busy. I’ll come, tell them, in about three days.”

     But three days passed, then a week passed, and he still did not come. Once, while passing by the Turkins’ house, he remembered that he ought to stop by for at least a minute, but he thought it over and... did not stop.

     And after that he never visited the Turkins again.

     

V

     

     Several more years passed. Startsev grew even fatter, became obese, breathes heavily, and already walks with his head thrown back. When he, plump and red, rides in a troika with bells and Panteleimon,also plump and red, with a fleshy nape, sitting on the box, stretching out straight, almost wooden arms, and shouting at passersby, “Prra-vda hold!” then the picture is impressive, and it seems that it is not a man driving, but a pagan god. He has a huge practice in town, no time to breathe, and he already has an estate and two houses in town, and he is eyeing a third, a better one, and when at the Mutual Credit Society they tell him about some house scheduled for auction, he goes into that house without ceremony and, going through all the rooms, paying no attention to the unclothed women and children who look at him with amazement and fear, he pokes all the doors with his stick and says:

     – Is this an office? Is this a bedroom? And what’s here?

     And all the while he breathes heavily and wipes sweat from his brow.

     He has a lot of worries, but still he does not give up his zemstvo post; greed has gotten the better of him, and he wants to manage both here and there. In Dyalizh and in town they already call him simply Ionych. – “Where is Ionych going?” or: “Shouldn’t we invite Ionych to the consultation?”

     Probably because his throat has grown fat, his voice has changed and become thin and sharp. His character has changed too: he has become heavy and irritable. When seeing patients, he is usually angry, taps his stick impatiently on the floor, and shouts in his unpleasant voice:

     – Answer only the questions! No talking!

     He is alone. Life is boring for him, nothing interests him.

     During all the time he has lived in Dyalizh, his love for Kotik was his only joy and, probably, his last. In the evenings he plays vint at the club and then sits alone at the big table and has dinner. He is served by Ivan, the oldest and most respected footman, they bring him Lafite No. 17, and now everyone, both the club elders and the cook and the footman, knows what he likes and what he does not like, try their utmost to please him, otherwise he may suddenly get angry and start banging his stick on the floor.

     While dining, he occasionally turns around and joins in some conversation:

     – What are you talking about? Huh? Who?

     And when it happens that nearby at some table people start talking about the Turkins, he asks:

     – Which Turkins are you talking about? The ones whose daughter plays the piano?

     That is all that can be said about him.

     And the Turkins? Ivan Petrovich has not grown old, has not changed a bit, and as before keeps cracking jokes and telling anecdotes; Vera Iosifovna still gladly reads her novels to guests, with heartfelt simplicity. And Kotik plays the grand piano every day for about four hours. She has noticeably aged, is ailing, and every autumn goes with her mother to Crimea. Seeing them off at the station, Ivan Petrovich, as the train starts moving, wipes away tears and shouts:

     – Goodbye please!

     

Alarm

     

     Mashenka Pavletskaya, a young woman, just out of the institute, returning from a walk to the Pushkin house, where she lived as a governess, found an extraordinary commotion. Mikhailo, the porter who opened the door for her, was agitated and red as a lobster.

     Noise came from upstairs.

     “Probably the mistress has had a fit...,” thought Mashenka, “or she quarreled with her husband...”

     In the foyer and in the corridor she met the maids. One maid was crying. Then Mashenka saw the master himself, Nikolai Sergeich, come running out of her room, a short, not yet old man with a puffy face and a large bald spot. He was red. He was twitching... Not noticing the governess, he passed her by and, raising his hands, cried out:

     – Oh, how terrible this is! How tactless! How stupid, how wild! Disgusting!

     Mashenka entered her room, and there for the first time in her life she had to experience in all its sharpness a feeling so familiar to dependent people, silent people, people living off the bread of the rich and the notable. Her room was being searched. The mistress, Fedosya Vasilyevna, a plump broad-shouldered lady with thick black eyebrows, bareheaded and angular, with barely noticeable mustaches and red hands, face, and manner resembling a simple peasant cook, was standing by her desk and putting balls of wool, scraps, papers back into the work bag... Evidently the governess’s appearance was unexpected to her, because,glancing back and seeing her pale, astonished face, she grew a little embarrassed and muttered:

     – Pardon,1 I... I spilled it by accident... caught it on my sleeve...

     And, saying something more, Madame Kushkina rustled out, her train swishing. Mashenka looked around her room with astonished eyes and, understanding nothing, not knowing what to think, shrugged, turned cold with fear... What was Fyodosiya Vasilievna looking for in her bag? If in fact, as she says, she accidentally caught it with her sleeve and spilled it, then why did Nikolai Sergeich rush out of the room, so red and agitated? Why was one of the desk drawers slightly pulled out? The savings box where the governess kept kopecks and old stamps was unlocked. It had been unlocked, but they had not managed to lock it again, although they had scratched up the whole lock. The bookcase, the desk surface, the bed, all bore fresh signs of a search. And in the laundry basket too. The laundry was folded neatly, but not in the order in which Mashenka had left it when she went out of the house. So there had really been a search, the real thing, but what for, why? What happened? Mashenka remembered the doorman's agitation, the commotion that was still going on, the tearful maid; was all this connected with the search that had just been carried out in her room? Was she involved in some terrible business? Mashenka went pale and, cold all over, sank down onto the laundry basket.

     The maid came into the room.

     – Liza, do you know why they... searched me? asked the governess.

     – The lady has lost a brooch worth two thousand... said Liza.

     – Yes, but why search me?

     – They searched everyone, miss. They searched me all over too... They stripped us all naked and searched us... And I, miss, swear before God... Not that I went anywhere near their brooch, but I didn't even go near the dressing table. I'll say the same in the police station too.

     – But... why search me? continued the governess in confusion.

     – I told you, the brooch was stolen... The lady herself rummaged through everything with her own hands. They even searched doorman Mikhail themselves. A complete disgrace! Nikolai Sergeich just watches and clucks like a hen. And you, miss, are trembling for nothing. They didn't find anything on you. If you didn't take the brooch, then you have nothing to be afraid of.

     – But this, Liza, is vile... insulting! said Mashenka, gasping with indignation. – It's wicked, despicable! What right did she have to suspect me and rummage through my things?

     – You live in other people's house, miss, sighed Liza. – Even if you are a young lady, still... like a servant... It's not the same as living with your papa and mama...
