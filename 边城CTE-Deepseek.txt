Paragraph [1]: Foreword to "Border Town"

I harbor an inexpressible tender affection for farmers and soldiers, a feeling that can be seen everywhere in all my works. I have never concealed this feeling. I grew up in the kind of small country town described in my writing. My grandfather, father, and brothers all were in the military: those who died did so in the line of duty, and those who survived would inevitably spend their entire lives in service. Based on the side of the world I have encountered, I narrate their loves, hates, joys, and sorrows. Even if this pen is clumsy, it may not stray too far from the subject. Because they are upright and honest, some aspects of their lives are extremely great, others extremely ordinary; some aspects of their temperaments are extremely beautiful, others extremely trivial—when I began to write about them, to make them more human, closer to human feelings, I naturally wrote honestly and plainly. But because of this, this work might inevitably become a kind of futile endeavor.

Given the current trends, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are very likely to develop unpleasant feelings toward such works. The former claim to "not be behind the times," telling people that China does not need this kind of work; the latter are "too afraid of falling behind" and are unwilling to read such works now. This is naturally true. What is "falling behind"? A somewhat rational person might never be able to understand it, but who among the majority is not afraid of "falling behind"? I have something to say: "This book of mine is not written for this majority." People who have read three or five foreign books on literary theory and criticism, or perhaps also a heap of classic and modern world masterpieces, often lack the life experience to allow them, beyond their "erudition," to know a little about another place and another kind of thing in China. Therefore, even if this work conforms to some literary theory and critics heap various praises on it, such criticism still inevitably becomes an insult to the author. Since they do not wish to understand this nation's true loves, hates, joys, and sorrows, they cannot explain the merits and demerits of this work—this book is not written for them. As for literary enthusiasts, they might be college students or high school students, distributed in the more densely populated cities of the country, often honestly and naively spending a portion of their precious time reading newly published literary books in China. They are controlled and dominated by a custom created through the joint efforts of some theorists, critics, clever publishers, and literary gossips accustomed to lying and fabricating rumors. Moreover, their lives are truly too far removed from the world mentioned in this work. They do not need this kind of work, and this book does not seek to gain them. Theorists have literary theories from various countries' publications for reference and need not worry about having nothing to say; critics have writers and works with whom they have minor personal grievances, enough for them to praise or condemn for a lifetime. Most readers, regardless of their taste or beliefs, have works to read. Isn't it precisely because they care about the reading public that many people are said to be forever spinning like a top for the reading public? Even if the publication of this book is not abandoned by the leading theorists and critics, and the led majority of readers do not completely give it up, the author of this book has long since intended to abandon this "majority."

This book of mine is only prepared for those "who have themselves left school, or have never been able to approach school, still know some Chinese characters, are in positions beyond the reach of literary theory, literary criticism, and lying and rumors, live in that society, and are deeply concerned with all the good and evil of the entire nation in space and time." They truly know what the current countryside is like, want to know what the past countryside was like, and they will surely also be willing to learn from this book about the countryside and soldiers in a small corner of the world. The world I write about may be entirely unfamiliar to them, yet their tolerance, their enthusiasm for seeking comfort and knowledge from a book, will certainly enable them to read this book calmly. I will not stop there; I also prepare for them an opportunity for comparison. In another work, I will mention the two decades of civil war, how the farmers who bore the brunt of it, their character and soul pressed by great force, lost their original model of simplicity, diligence, peace, and uprightness, and became a new kind of thing. How impoverished and lazy they became under the poison of exorbitant taxes and opium! I will make a simple narrative of the anxieties of some small characters in the midst of change when this nation is pushed by history toward an unknowable destiny, and their concepts and desires of "surviving" and "how to survive" arising from malnutrition. My readers should be rational, and this rationality is based on caring about the changes in contemporary Chinese society, understanding the nation's past greatness and present depravity, each person quietly dedicated to the great cause of national rejuvenation. This work may only give them a touch of nostalgic melancholy, or only a bitter smile, or perhaps a nightmare, but at the same time, who knows, it might also give them some courage and confidence!

Paragraph [2]: Chapter 1

There is an official road leading from Sichuan to Hunan, going east. When this road approaches the border of western Hunan and reaches a small mountain town called "Chadong," there is a stream. Beside the stream stands a white pagoda, and below the pagoda lives a solitary family. This family consists only of an old man, a young girl, and a yellow dog.

The stream flows down, winding around the mountain, and after about three li it merges into the main river of Chadong. If one crosses the stream and climbs over the small hill, it is only one li to the edge of Chadong town. The stream is like the back of a bow, and the mountain path like the bowstring, so there is a slight difference in distance. The stream is about twenty zhang wide, with a riverbed made of large stones. Although the still water might be too deep for a single boat pole to touch the bottom, it remains clear and transparent, and the fish swimming in the river can be counted. Since the stream is a main thoroughfare between Sichuan and Hunan, the water level often rises and falls. Limited by financial resources, they could not build a bridge, so they set up a square-headed ferryboat. This ferryboat can carry about twenty passengers, with their goods and horses, across the river at one time; when there are more people, it goes back and forth. A small bamboo pole is erected at the bow of the ferryboat, hanging a movable iron ring. A section of worn cable is stretched between the wooden gutters on both banks of the stream. When someone needs to cross, they hang the iron ring on the worn cable, and the boatman pulls on the cable with his hands, slowly towing the boat across to the opposite bank. As the boat is about to reach the shore, the ferryman, shouting "Slow down, slow down," suddenly jumps ashore, pulls the iron ring, and then the people, goods, cattle, and horses all go ashore, disappear over the small hill. The ferry is public property, so people crossing do not have to pay. If someone feels uneasy and throws a handful of coins onto the boat deck, the ferryman will pick them up one by one and stuff them back into that person's hand, with a seriousness as if arguing: "I have my rations, three pecks of rice, seven hundred cash, enough! Who wants this?"

But that won't do; people want to feel at ease. Who would be comfortable receiving labor without reward? No matter what, someone always leaves money. The boatman, unable to refuse, and for his own peace of mind, would entrust this money to someone to buy tea and pipe tobacco in Chadong. He would hang the high-quality pipe tobacco from Chadong, bundle by bundle, on his belt, and generously offer it to anyone crossing who needed it. Sometimes, judging from the expression of a traveler who showed particular interest in the pipe tobacco on his person, he would tie a small bundle of tobacco to that person's bundle, saying, "Don't smoke this? It's good, it's fine, the flavor is excellent, suitable for giving as a gift!" In June, he would put the tea leaves into a large vat, brew it with boiling water, and offer it to passersby to quench their thirst.

The man in charge of this ferry was the old man living under the pagoda. He had lived for seventy years and had been guarding this stream since he was twenty. For fifty years, he had ferried countless people across. Although he was so old, and should have rested, Heaven did not allow him to rest, as if he could not part from this life. He never pondered the meaning of his duty for himself, but just quietly and faithfully lived on. Replacing Heaven, enabling him to feel the power of life when the sun rose, and when the sun set, preventing him from thinking about dying along with the sun, was the young girl who accompanied him. His only friends were a ferryboat and a yellow dog, his only relative was that young girl.

The young girl's mother, the old ferryman's only daughter, fifteen years earlier had had a clandestine relationship with a soldier from Chadong, secretly, behind the back of her honest father. After she became pregnant, this garrison soldier wanted to elope with her downstream. But judging by the act of running away, one would violate his military duty, and the other would have to leave her lonely father. After some thought, the soldier saw that she lacked the courage to run far away and he himself found it inconvenient to destroy his honor as a soldier, so he thought: Since they could not be together in life, no one could stop them from dying together. He first took poison. The woman, concerned for the child in her womb, could not bear it and could not make a decision. The matter had already come to the knowledge of her father, the ferryman, but he did not add a single weighty word, acting as if he had never heard of it, continuing to spend his days peacefully. The daughter, filled with both shame and pity, stayed by her father's side. After the child in her womb was born, she went to the stream, drank a lot of cold water, and died. Through something close to a miracle, this orphan grew up. In the blink of an eye, she was thirteen years old. Because the mountains on both sides of their dwelling were covered with tall bamboo, their green color pressing in upon one, the old ferryman casually picked a nearby name for this poor orphan, calling her "Cuicui."

Cuicui grew up in the wind and sun, her skin turned dark. Her eyes were fixed on green hills and clear waters, and her eyes were as bright as crystal. Nature not only raised her but also taught her. She was naturally innocent and lively, everywhere like a small wild creature. She was also so gentle, like a yellow deer on the mountain, never thinking of cruel things, never worrying, never getting angry. Usually, when she encountered a stranger on the ferry who took notice of her, she would stare at the stranger with her bright eyes, looking as if she might at any moment flee into the deep mountains. But once she understood that the person had no ill intent, she would again play leisurely by the water.

The old ferryman, whether in rain or shine, always stayed at the boat's bow. When someone needed to cross, he would bend slightly, grasp the bamboo cable with both hands, and pull the boat across the stream. Sometimes, when tired, he would lie on a large rock by the stream and fall asleep. When someone on the opposite bank waved and shouted to cross, Cuicui would not let her grandfather get up; she would jump into the boat and nimbly ferry the traveler across the stream for her grandfather, handling everything smoothly and expertly, never causing trouble. Sometimes, she and her grandfather and the yellow dog would be on the boat together, and when crossing, they would work together. Just as the boat neared the shore and her grandfather was greeting the passenger, "Slow down, slow down," the yellow dog, holding the rope in its mouth, would be the first to leap ashore, and seeming to understand how to do its duty, it would tightly grip the boat rope and pull the boat to shore.

On days when the weather was fine and clear, with no one crossing and the entire day leisurely, Grandfather and Cuicui would sit on the large rock in front of their door and sun themselves. Sometimes, they would throw a piece of wood from a high place into the water, urging the yellow dog to jump down from the high rock and bring the wood back. Or Cuicui and the yellow dog, both pricking up their ears, would listen to Grandfather tell stories of war in the town from many years ago. Or Grandfather and Cuicui, each holding a small bamboo vertical flute to their lips, would play wedding tunes. When a traveler came to cross, the old ferryman would put down his bamboo flute, go alone to the boat, and ferry the person across the stream. The one on the rock, seeing the boat set off, would shout sharply:

"Grandpa, Grandpa, listen to me play, sing!"

Grandpa, in the middle of the stream, would happily start to sing. His hoarse voice and the sound of the bamboo flute vibrated in the still air, making the stream seem a bit livelier. (In fact, the back-and-forth of the singing only made everything even quieter.)

Sometimes what crossed were small cattle from eastern Sichuan heading to Chadong, or sheep, or a bridal sedan chair. Cuicui would insist on being the ferryman, standing at the bow, lazily pulling the cable, letting the boat cross slowly. After the cattle, sheep, and bridal sedan chair went ashore, Cuicui would follow them, stand on a small hilltop, watch them until they were far away, then return to the boat and pull it back to the shore near home. And she would softly imitate the calls of the lambs and the cows, or pick a handful of wildflowers and tie them in her hair, pretending to be a bride.

The mountain town of Chadong was only one li from the ferry crossing. When she needed to buy oil or salt, or on holidays when Grandfather wanted a drink of wine, Grandfather would not go to town; instead, the yellow dog would accompany Cuicui into town to get supplies. At the general store, there were large bundles of vermicelli, big vats of white sugar, firecrackers, and red candles—all left a deep impression on Cuicui. When she returned to her grandfather, she would talk about these things for a long time. There were also many boats going upstream near the riverbank, with hundreds of boatmen busy loading and unloading all kinds of goods. These boats were much larger and much more interesting than the ferryboat.Cuicui was not easily forgotten either.

The town of Chadong was built against the river and nestled against the mountains. On the side near the mountains, the city wall stretched like a long serpent climbing up the slopes. On the side facing the river, space was left outside the city wall along the riverbank to set up docks, where small awning boats were moored. Boats heading downstream carried tung oil, green salt, and dyed gallnuts. Those heading upstream transported cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, miscellaneous goods, and seafood. Connecting the docks was a river street, where most houses stood half on land and half over the water. Because land was limited, every house was built on stilts. When the spring floodwaters rose and gradually seeped into the street, the families along the river street would each take long ladders, placing one end on the eaves and the other on the city wall. Everyone would curse and shout as they carried bundles, bedding, and rice urns up the ladders into the city, only coming out through the city gate once the waters receded. In years when the flood was particularly fierce, one or two of the stilted houses along the river would inevitably be swept away by the torrent, and everyone on the city wall would stare blankly. Those who had suffered losses also gazed dumbly, as if they had nothing to say about their misfortune, much like the helplessness felt when witnessing other irreparable disasters under nature's arrangement. During the floods, from the city wall, one could also see the suddenly widened river surface, the vast and mighty currents, and houses, oxen, sheep, and large trees floating down from the upstream mountains. Then, in places where the current slowed, in front of the customs revenue barge, people would often steer small sampans. As soon as they spotted a livestock animal, a piece of wood, or an empty boat drifting in the river, with a woman or child crying out on board, they would hastily row over, intercept the target downstream, tie it with a long rope, and then row back to shore. These honest and brave people, like most locals, loved profit but also upheld righteousness. Whether rescuing people or salvaging goods, they acted with the same swiftness and courage in a thrilling adventure, earning cheers from all who witnessed it.

That river was historically known as the You River, now renamed the Bai River. After the Bai River flows downstream to Chenzhou and merges with the Yuan River, it becomes slightly murky, as if emerging from mountain springs. But if one travels upstream, deep pools of thirty or fifty feet are crystal clear to the bottom. Under the sunlight, every small white pebble and patterned agate stone on the riverbed is distinctly visible. The fish swimming to and fro in the water seem to float in the air. High mountains line both banks, covered with slender bamboo suitable for papermaking, perpetually a deep, eye-catching green. Homes near the water are often nestled among peach and apricot blossoms. In spring, one need only notice that wherever there are peach blossoms, there are houses, and wherever there are houses, one can buy wine. In summer, the dazzling purple cotton clothes and trousers dried in the sun serve as banners marking where people live. When autumn and winter arrive, houses perched on cliffs or by the water are all clearly visible. The yellow mud walls and black tiles are always perfectly positioned, harmonizing beautifully with the surroundings, leaving a very pleasant impression on anyone who approaches. A traveler with even a slight interest in poetry or painting, curled up in a small boat on this river for a thirty-day journey, would surely not grow bored, for there are wonders everywhere—nature's boldness and delicacy, each inspiring fascination and admiration.

The source of the Bai River originates from the border of Sichuan. Small boats traveling upstream on the Bai River can reach Xiushan in Sichuan when the spring waters rise. But within Hunan territory, Chadong is the last river dock. The river surface at Chadong is about half a li wide, but when the water level drops in autumn and winter, the flowing portion of the riverbed is less than twenty zhang, with the rest being a stretch of green stones. Once small boats reach here, they cannot proceed further upstream, so all import and export goods from eastern Sichuan are loaded and unloaded at this spot. Export goods are carried on the shoulders of porters using fir wood carrying poles, while import goods are also bundled and carried here by human labor.

In this town, only one battalion of garrison soldiers, reorganized from the old Green Standard Army's屯丁, was stationed, along with about five hundred households. (Among these households, aside from a few small capitalists who owned some mountain fields and oil presses, or lent out屯 oil,屯 grain, and屯 cotton yarn, most were descendants of military personnel who had been stationed here.) There was also a likin tax bureau, whose office was located in a small temple below the river street outside the city, often flying a long streamer. The bureau chief lived in the city. The battalion of soldiers was stationed in the old Assistant General's yamen. Except for the bugler who went up the city wall every day to play his bugle, reminding people that troops were still stationed here, the other soldiers seemed almost nonexistent. On winter days, when one went into the city, one would only see clothes and green vegetables drying in front of every house. Sweet potatoes, often with their vines attached, hung under the eaves. Bags made of palm fiber, filled with chestnuts, hazelnuts, and other hard-shelled fruits, also hung under the eaves. In every corner of the houses, chickens of all sizes clucked and played. Occasionally, a man could be seen sawing wood on his doorstep or splitting wood with an axe, stacking the firewood in the open yard into pagoda-like piles. Or one might see a few middle-aged women, dressed in blue cloth clothes starched stiff, with white cloth embroidered aprons tied at the chest, bent over, talking and working in the sunlight. Everything was always so quiet, and every day for all the people passed in this simple solitude. This increased one's contemplative power over "human affairs" and enriched one's dreams. Everyone living in this small town, in their allotted days, must have harbored inevitable expectations of love and hatred for human affairs. But what were they thinking? Who knows. For those living in the higher parts of the city, a stand at the doorstep offered a view of the opposite shore and the scenery on the river. When boats arrived, countless trackers could be seen from afar on the opposite beach. Some trackers, coming from downstream, brought fine pastries and foreign sugar, which they would take into the city to exchange for money when they landed. When boats arrived, children's imaginations would be focused on the boat trackers. As for adults, hatching a brood of chicks, raising two pigs, asking a downstream boatman to bring back a pair of gold earrings, two bolts of official blue cloth, a jar of good soy sauce, or a double-thick Meifu lamp shade—these took up most of a housewife's thoughts.

Although this small town was so peaceful and tranquil, because it was a hub for commercial transactions from eastern Sichuan, the small river street outside the city was a bit different. There were inns for merchants to stay in and barbershops that never moved. In addition, restaurants, general stores, oil firms, salt warehouses, and cotton cloth shops each had their place, adorning this river street. There were also shops selling檀木活车, bamboo cables, and pots and pans for boats, as well as families who made a living by introducing sailors to jobs. In front of small restaurants, on long tables, there were often fried golden-brown carp with tofu, decorated with red chili shreds, lying in shallow bowls. Beside the bowls, large bamboo tubes held big handfuls of red chopsticks. Anyone willing to spend a little money could sit down at the long table by the door, pull out a pair of chopsticks, and a woman with finely plucked eyebrows and powdered face would come over and ask, "Elder brother, officer, sweet wine or liquor?" A man with a bit of a hot temper, a sense of humor, or a slight interest in the landlady would pretend to be angry and say, "Sweet wine? Am I a child, asking me if I want sweet wine!" Then, strong liquor would be ladled from a large vat with a bamboo tube into an earthen bowl, and it would be placed on the side table in an instant. General stores sold Meifu oil and Meifu oil lamps, as well as incense, candles, and paper. Oil firms stockpiled tung oil. Salt warehouses stored green salt from the fire wells. Cotton cloth shops sold white cotton yarn, large cloth, cotton, and black crepe silk for headscarves. Stores selling boat supplies displayed a wide variety of items, everything available, and sometimes heavy iron anchors weighing over a hundred jin were left outside on the roadside, waiting for customers to inquire about the price. Those who specialized in introducing sailors and made a living by the docks kept their doors wide open all day in their homes on the river street. Boat owners in black satin mandarin jackets and rough-and-tumble sailors frequently came and went. The place was like a teahouse but didn't sell tea, and it wasn't an opium den but allowed smoking. Although those who came here talked about business on the boats, the rules for going upstream and downstream, rowing, and tracking were mostly fixed, so there was no need for numerical discussion. Most of them came here for "socializing." With the "dragon head manager" at the center, they discussed local current events, the business situation between the two provinces, and "news from downstream." Fundraising meetings were mostly held here, and dice-throwing to determine who would be the rotating leader also took place here. Two things often became their business talk: buying and selling boats, and buying and selling wives.

Just as a metropolis produces certain parasites with the development of commerce, due to the needs of merchants and sailors, this small border town's river street also managed to gather a group of such people, congregating in some of the stilted houses. These women were either brought from nearby villages or were women who had followed the Sichuan army to Hunan and ended up stranded. They wore fake foreign silk clothes and printed calico trousers, plucked their eyebrows into thin lines, and applied strongly scented, vulgar oil to their large buns. During the day, when they had nothing to do, they would sit at the door making shoes, embroidering double phoenixes on the toes with red and green silk threads, or embroidering flowered pouch belts for their sailor lovers, while watching passersby to while away the long hours. Or they would lean by the river-facing window, watching sailors unload goods and listening to them sing as they climbed the masts. At night, they would take turns receiving merchants and sailors, earnestly fulfilling their duties as prostitutes.

Due to the simple customs of the border region, even prostitutes were always so honest. When dealing with strangers, they would collect the money first, then close the door and do as they pleased. Once they were familiar with someone, the money became optional. Most prostitutes relied on Sichuan merchants for their livelihood, but their deepest affections were often reserved for the sailors. When feelings were strong, they would bite each other's lips and necks, making vows, and agree that "neither shall fool around after parting." For forty or fifty days, the one drifting on the boat and the one left on shore would both endure this period, binding their hearts tightly to a distant person. Especially the women, whose emotions were so sincere, almost indescribably foolish. If the man didn't return by the agreed time, in their dreams, they would often see the boat docking, the man swaying as he came ashore over the gangplank, running straight towards them. Or if they had suspicions during the day, in their dreams, they would see the man singing on the mast in another direction, ignoring them. The weaker ones would then dream of drowning themselves in the river or taking opium, while the stronger ones would dream of grabbing a kitchen knife and rushing straight at the sailor. Although their lives were so distant from mainstream society, tears and joy, mixed into their lives through gains and losses of love and hate, were similar to those of young souls in other lands. Their entire being was soaked in that love and hate, feverish and cold, forgetting everything. If there was any difference, it was that these people were more genuine, and also more foolish. Short-term contracts, long-term marriages, temporary lock-ins—these transactions over a woman's body, due to the simple folkways, were not considered base or shameful by those involved, nor did onlookers criticize or despise them through the lens of a scholar. These people valued righteousness over profit and could keep their promises and self-discipline. Even as prostitutes, they were often more trustworthy than the moral and shame-conscious city dwellers.

The master of the docks was named Shunshun, a man who had spent his days in the military ranks during the Qing Dynasty. During the revolution, he served as a squad leader in the famous 49th Standard of the Army. Other squad leaders either became great figures due to the revolution or were beheaded and dismembered. But he, with foot rheumatism contracted from youthful recklessness, returned to his hometown. With his meager savings, he bought a six-oar white wooden boat, rented it to a poor boat owner, and had it carry goods between Chadong and Chenzhou. With good luck, the boat didn't meet with any mishaps for half a year. With the money he earned, he married a fair-skinned, black-haired young widow with some property. A few years later, he owned four boats of various sizes, a shop, and two sons on this river.

But this generous and free-spirited man, though his career was very smooth, could not become as wealthy as the oil merchants because he loved making friends, was generous, and helped those in urgent need. Having spent time in the army, he understood the hardships of travelers and the feelings of the down-and-out. So, any boat owner who had gone bankrupt due to a shipwreck, passing discharged soldiers, or wandering scholars—anyone who came to this place and sought help upon hearing his name—would receive his utmost assistance. He made money from the water and freely spent it this way. Although he had a slight limp and could still swim, and walked unevenly, he was fair and selfless. Matters on the river were originally very simple, all governed by custom. If one boat collided with another, or if one boat harmed the interests of another person or boat, it was always resolved according to customary methods. But to mediate everything using these customs and rules, a respected and virtuous central figure was needed. One autumn, the original person in charge died, and Shunshun became his replacement. At that time, he was only fifty years old. He was reasonable, upright, and peace-loving, and not greedy for money, so no one questioned his age.

Nowadays, his eldest son is eighteen and his youngest is sixteen. Both young men are as sturdy as young bulls, capable of steering boats, swimming, and walking long distances. There is nothing that young people from small towns can do that they cannot, and they excel at everything they do. The elder one, like his father, is bold and open-minded, unconcerned with trivialities. The younger one takes after his fair-skinned, black-haired mother, is quiet, yet has strikingly handsome eyebrows and eyes.At first glance, one could tell he was intelligent and full of emotion.

Since the two brothers had grown up, they needed training in various aspects of life. Their father took turns sending the two children on travels. When going downstream by boat, they would serve as crew on their own vessels, sharing hardships with others. When rowing, they chose the heaviest oar; when towing, they took the lead ropes. They ate dried fish, chili peppers, and sour pickled vegetables, and slept on the hard cabin boards. When traveling upstream by land, they followed Sichuan-bound merchants through Xiushan, Longtan, and Youyang for trade, regardless of cold or heat, rain or snow, always wearing straw sandals and pressing on by stages. They carried short knives, and when necessary, they would swiftly draw their blades, stand in an open space, and face their opponent for a close-quarters fight. The gang’s ethos was, “To deal with enemies, one must use a knife; to bond with friends, one must also use a knife.” So when a knife was needed, they never missed the chance. They learned trade, social etiquette, how to live in a new place, and how to use a knife to protect their bodies and honor. The goal of their education seemed to be teaching the two children the courage and loyalty needed to be a person. As a result, they grew as sturdy as tigers, yet they were gentle and approachable, not arrogant, lazy, flashy, or bullying. Thus, when the father and two sons were mentioned in the borderlands of Chadong, everyone respected those names.

When the two sons were very young, their father realized the elder one was much like himself, but he secretly doted on the younger. Due to this unconscious favoritism, he named the elder son Tianbao and the younger son Nuosong. This meant that what heaven protects might inevitably face conflicts in human affairs, while what the Nuo gods send must not be slighted, according to local custom. Nuosong was very handsome. The boat people of Chadong, clumsy at praising such beauty, merely nicknamed him “Yue Yun.” Although no one had seen Yue Yun in person, his general impression, from the young warrior role on stage, gave him a similar air.

In the border area of three provinces, the local military authorities maintained peace and stability for over ten years, handling affairs well without any incidents. Water and land trade neither halted due to war nor suffered from bandits. Everything was orderly, and the people were content and law-abiding. Except for the profound grief caused by the death of a cow, a capsized boat, or some other major misfortune, the people of this border town seemed never to feel the tragic struggles elsewhere in China.

The busiest days of the year in the border town were the Dragon Boat Festival, the Mid-Autumn Festival, and New Year’s Day. How these three festivals excited the locals fifty or thirty years ago remains unchanged, still being the most significant days for the residents.

On the Dragon Boat Festival, local women and children all wore new clothes and drew the character “王” (king) on their foreheads with realgar wine. Every household would eat fish and meat that day. Around 11 AM, all of Chadong would have lunch. After eating, those living in town would lock their doors and head out of town to the riverbank to watch the boat races. Those with acquaintances on River Street could watch from the stilted houses there; otherwise, they stood at the customs house entrance or at various docks. The dragon boats on the river raced from the Longtan area to the customs house. That day, military officers, tax officials, and local dignitaries all gathered at the customs house to watch. The rowers had prepared days in advance, each group selecting strong and agile young men to practice advancing and retreating in the pool. The boats were quite different from ordinary wooden boats—long, narrow, with high, curved ends, painted with vermilion lines. Usually stored in dry caves by the river, they were dragged into the water when needed. Each boat could hold twelve to eighteen rowers, a leader, a drummer, and a gong player. Each rower used a short oar, paddling in time with the drum’s rhythm. The leader, with a red cloth wrapped around his head and two small command flags, directed the boat’s movement. The drummer and gong player, usually seated in the middle, beat a simple rhythm to guide the rowers. The old ferryman tired from work had fallen asleep, and Cuicui, exhausted from crying, had also slept. Cuicui could not forget what her grandfather had spoken of; in her dream, her soul seemed lifted by a wondrously beautiful song, floating gently here and there, up to the white pagoda, down to the vegetable garden, onto the boat, and then flying back past the middle of the cliff—to do what? To pick tiger-ear plants! During the day when pulling the boat, she had often looked up at those plump tiger-ear plants on the cliff and knew them well. The cliff wall was thirty or fifty feet high, usually impossible to reach, but at this moment she could choose the largest leaves for an umbrella.

Everything seemed like the story her grandfather told; Cuicui just lay dazedly on the straw mat under the coarse linen bed curtain, thinking this dream was exceptionally beautiful and sweet. But grandfather lay awake in bed, pricking up his ears to listen to someone singing half the night on the high cliff across the stream. He knew who was singing; he knew it was Tianbao, the eldest son from River Street, making his first move on the "horse path." He listened with a mix of worry and joy. Since Cuicui was sound asleep, exhausted from crying during the day, he did not disturb her.

At dawn the next day, Cuicui and her grandfather got up, washed their faces with stream water to break the taboo of recounting dreams in the morning. Cuicui hurried to tell her grandfather about her dream the night before.

"Grandfather, you talked about singing, and last night I heard the most beautiful singing in my dream, so soft and lingering. I felt like I followed that sound flying everywhere, flew to the middle of the cliff across the stream, and picked a big handful of tiger-ear plants. Once I got the tiger-ear plants, I don't know who I gave them to. I slept so well, the dream was so interesting!"

Grandfather smiled a gentle, pitying smile but did not tell Cuicui the truth about the previous night.

Grandfather thought to himself: "It would be better to dream forever; some people even become prime ministers or top scholars in their dreams."

The old ferryman still thought the singer from the night before was Tianbao, the eldest son. In recent days, he had Cuicui mind the boat while he found an excuse to go to town to deliver medicine and gather information. On River Street, he spotted the eldest son, grabbed the young man, and said cheerfully:

"Eldest son, you, riding both the carriage path and the horse path—what a sly one!"

But the old ferryman had made a mistake, confusing the singer from the night before. These two brothers had both gone to Green Creek Hill the previous night. Because the elder brother had taken the carriage path first, he absolutely refused to start singing, insisting that the younger brother sing first. Once the younger brother began, the elder knew he was no match and could not bring himself to sing. The singing Cuicui and her grandfather heard that night was entirely from Nuosong, the second son. When the elder brother accompanied his younger brother home, he decided to leave Chadong and take the family's new oil boat downstream to forget everything upriver. At that moment, he was heading to the river to see the new boat being loaded. Seeing his cold expression, the old ferryman did not understand his intention. He made a funny gesture with his eyes and brows, indicating he knew the eldest son's coldness was put on, and that he had news to share.

He patted the eldest son and said softly:

"You sang very well; someone in their dream heard your song and was carried far away by it, walking a long way! You're number one, the number one singer around here."

The eldest son looked at the old ferryman's shameless, wrinkled face and said softly:

"Forget it, just give your precious daughter to the songbird who can sing."

The old ferryman had no idea what this meant. The eldest son walked down to the river through a passageway between stilted houses, and the old ferryman followed. At the riverbank, he saw the new boat being loaded, with many oil baskets stacked on the shore. A boatman was tying bundles of reeds to use as wave breakers along the gunwales, while someone else was greasing oars with tallow. The old ferryman asked the boatman sitting in the scorching sun tying the reed bundles when the boat would go downstream and who would be in charge. The boatman pointed at the eldest son. The old ferryman rubbed his hands and said:

"Eldest son, listen to me seriously: that matter of yours, taking the carriage path, isn't right; but on the horse path, you have a chance!"

The eldest son pointed toward a window and said: "Uncle, look over there. If you want a songbird for a grandson-in-law, the songbird is right there!"

The old ferryman looked up and saw the second son arranging a fishing net by the window.

When he returned to the ferryboat at Green Creek Hill, Cuicui asked:

"Grandfather, who did you quarrel with? Your face looks so awful!"

Grandfather smiled faintly but did not tell Cuicui a single word about his trip to town.

The eldest son took the new oil boat and headed downstream, leaving Nuosong, the second son, at home. The old ferryman, still believing the singing from the previous night belonged to the second son, expected to hear that song again in the following days. In the evening, he deliberately steered the conversation to other things to get Cuicui to pay attention to the nighttime singing. After dinner, the two sat in the house; as the house fronted the water, long-legged mosquitoes began buzzing at dusk. Cuicui lit a bundle of mugwort and wormwood, waving it into every corner of the room to drive away the mosquitoes. After waving it around, once she figured the whole room was filled with the smoke, she placed it on the floor by the bedside, then sat on a small stool to listen to her grandfather talk. From one story, they gradually moved to singing, and grandfather spoke cleverly. Finally, he asked:

"Cuicui, the song in your dream could make you climb the high cliff to pick those tiger-ear plants. If someone actually came to sing for you on the high cliff across the stream, what would you do?" Grandfather said this as a joke.

Cuicui answered jokingly: "If someone sings, I'll listen; I'll listen as long as he sings!"

"What if he sings for three years and six months?"

"If it sounds good, I'll listen for three years and six months."

"That's not fair, is it?"

"Why isn't it fair? Wouldn't the one singing for me be very willing for me to listen to his song for so long?"

"Logically speaking: cooking needs someone to eat, singing needs someone to listen. But if someone sings for you, he wants you to understand the meaning in his song!"

"Grandfather, what meaning is in the song?"

"Of course, it's his genuine desire to be close to you! If you don't understand that thought, isn't it the same as listening to a songbird?"

"And if I understand his heart, then what?"

Grandfather thumped his leg heavily with his fist and laughed: "Cuicui, you're clever, but your grandfather is very dull, and I don't speak gently. Don't be angry. I'm just rambling, telling you a joke. You should take it as a joke. Tianbao, the eldest son from River Street, took the carriage path and sent a matchmaker to propose. I told you about that; you didn't seem willing, right? But, if that person had a younger brother, who took the horse path, singing for you to propose, what would you say?"

Cuicui was startled and lowered her head. She didn't know how much of the joke was true, or who had made it up.

Grandfather said: "Tell me, which one would you choose?"

Cuicui smiled softly and, with a hint of pleading, said:

"Grandfather, don't tell this joke." Cuicui stood up.

"What if what I'm saying is true?"

"Grandfather, you really are a..." Cuicui said as she walked out.

Grandfather said: "I was joking. Are you angry with me?"

Cuicui dared not be angry with her grandfather. As she reached the threshold, she changed the subject: "Grandfather, look at the moon in the sky, so big!" Saying this, she went outside and stood still in the clear moonlight. After a moment, grandfather also came out of the house. Cuicui then sat on the rocks heated during the day by the strong sun; the stones were still radiating the stored warmth. Grandfather said: "Cuicui, don't sit on the hot stones, or you'll get sores." But after feeling them himself, he also sat down on the rocks.

The moonlight was very soft, and a thin layer of white mist floated over the stream. At this moment, if someone on the opposite bank were singing, answered from across the stream, it would have been truly beautiful. Cuicui still remembered grandfather's earlier joke. She wasn't deaf; grandfather's words were very clear—a younger brother on the horse path, singing away such a night, what did it mean? She seemed to wait for such a song, silent for a long time.

She sat in the moonlight for a while, her heart indeed wishing to hear someone sing. After a long time, there was nothing from across the stream except the repeated clear sounds of grass insects. Cuicui walked back into the house, felt for the reed pipe by the door, brought it out, and began to play it herself in the moonlight. Feeling she played poorly, she handed it to grandfather to play. The old ferryman put the reed pipe to his lips and played a long tune, softening Cuicui's heart.

Cuicui leaned against her grandfather and asked him:

"Grandfather, who was the first person to make this little pipe?"

"It must have been the happiest person, because he gives so much happiness to others; but it also seems like it was made by the unhappiest person, because it can also make people unhappy!"

"Grandfather, are you unhappy? Are you angry with me?"

"I'm not angry with you. With you by my side, I'm very happy."

"What if I ran away?"

"You won't leave your grandfather."

"What if something like that happened, grandfather, what would you do?"

"If something like that happened, I would take this ferryboat and go find you."

Cuicui giggled. "Phoenix Rapid, Thistle Rapid aren't fierce; further down there's Twisting Chicken Cage; Twisting Chicken Cage is also easy to go down, but Green Wave Rapid has waves as big as houses. Grandfather, can your ferryboat go down Phoenix Rapid, Thistle Rapid, and Green Wave Rapid? Didn't you say the water in those places is like a madman?"

Grandfather said: "Cuicui, by then I really would be like a madman; would I still be afraid of big water and big waves?"

Cuicui, appearing quite serious, thought for a moment, then said: "Grandfather, I definitely won't leave. But, will you leave? Could someone take you away somewhere else?"

Grandfather was silent. He thought of things like being taken away by death.

The old ferryman pondered what would happen after death took him, staring blankly at a star in the southern sky, thinking: "Shooting stars only appear in the seventh and eighth months; will people also die in the seventh and eighth months?" Then he recalled the conversation with the eldest son on River Street during the day, thought of the mill that the middle village man was giving as a dowry, thought of the second son, thought of a whole bunch of things, and felt somewhat unsettled.

Cuicui suddenly said: "Grandfather, sing me a song, will you?"

Grandfather sang ten songs. Cuicui, nestled beside her grandfather, listened with closed eyes. When grandfather fell silent, Cuicui murmured to herself: "I've picked another handful of tiger-ear plants."

The songs grandfather sang were the ones he had heard that night.

The second son had the chance to sing but never came to Green Creek Hill to sing again. The fifteenth passed, the sixteenth also passed. By the seventeenth, the old ferryman could not bear it anymore. He went to town, heading for River Street to find that young man. Just as he reached the city gate and was about to enter River Street, he met Horseman Yang, who had been the matchmaker for the eldest son. Yang was leading a mule and preparing to leave the city. Seeing the old ferryman, he grabbed him:

"Uncle, I have something to tell you; what luck that you came to town!"

"What is it?"

"Tianbao, the eldest son, took a downstream boat to Thistle Rapid and had an accident. The man fell into the whirlpool below the rapid and drowned. Shunshun's family got the news this morning; I hear the second son rushed off at dawn."

This news hit him like a heavy slap; he couldn't believe it was true. Feigning calm, he said:

"Tianbao, the eldest son, drowned? Never heard of a water duck drowning!"

"But that water duck still had such an accident... I agree with your wise decision not to let that boy have things too easy on the carriage path."

From the horseman's words, the old ferryman was still very doubtful about this news, but from the horseman's expression, he saw clearly that it was true. He said sadly:

"What wisdom do I have? This is Heaven's will! Everything is Heaven's will..." The old ferryman spoke with deep emotion.

To verify how reliable the horseman's words were, the old ferryman parted with him and hurried to River Street. At the gate of Shunshun's house, someone was burning spirit money, and many people were gathered around talking. Drawing near to listen, they were discussing the very matter Horseman Yang had mentioned. But as soon as someone noticed the old ferryman behind them, everyone changed the subject, deliberately talking about the rise and fall of oil prices downstream. Feeling very uneasy, the old ferryman looked for a relatively friendly boatman to talk to.

Soon, Fleetmaster Shunshun returned from outside, looking heavy-hearted. This hearty, upright middle-aged man seemed to be struggling to get up after being knocked down by misfortune. As soon as he saw the old ferryman, he said:

"Old uncle, the matter we discussed is off. Tianbao, the eldest son, is gone; you know, right?"

The old ferryman's eyes were red; he rubbed his hands. "How? Is this true? Was it yesterday, or the day before?"

Another man, who seemed to have rushed along the road with the news, chimed in: "On the morning of the sixteenth, the boat ran aground on a reef; the bow took on water. The eldest son tried to fend off with a pole, and he was thrown into the water."

The old ferryman said: "Did you see him go into the water?"

"I went into the water with him!"

"Did he say anything?""There's no time to say anything! He hasn't spoken a word these past few days!"  

The old ferryman shook his head and cast a timid glance at Shunshun. Fleetmaster Shunshun, as if sensing his unease, said, "Uncle, it's all fate. Let it be.  

I have some good strong liquor sent by someone from Daxingchang. Take some and drink it." A boatman used a bamboo tube to scoop out some liquor, covered the opening with a fresh tung tree leaf, and handed it to the old ferryman.  

The old ferryman took the liquor and, reaching River Street, walked with his head down toward the docks. He went to see the place where Tianbao had boarded his boat three days ago. Horseman Yang was still there, letting his horse roll in the sand while he sat under a willow tree enjoying the shade. The old ferryman walked over and invited the horseman to try the Daxingchang liquor. After they had both drunk a little, their spirits seemed to lift. The old ferryman then told Horseman Yang about the night of the fourteenth, when No. 2 had crossed to Green Creek Hill to sing.  

When the horseman heard this, he said:  

"Uncle, do you think Cuicui should be with No. 2, and that he’s the one meant for her…"

"No. 2, No. 2, come here! I have something to say to you!"  

No. 2 stopped, looking very displeased, and asked the horseman, "What do you have to say?" The horseman glanced at the old ferryman and then said to No. 2, "Come here! I have something to tell you!"  

"What is it?"  

"I heard you'd already left—come over here, I won't eat you!"  

The dark-faced, broad-shouldered, vigorous Nuosong forced a smile and came under the willow tree. The old ferryman, trying to lighten the mood, pointed to the new mill upstream and said, "No. 2, I hear that mill will be yours someday! If it is, would you let me tend it?"  

No. 2 seemed irritated by the implication of the question and said nothing. Sensing the tension, Horseman Yang said, "No. 2, what’s the matter? Are you heading downstream?" The young man nodded, said nothing more, and walked off.

"Cuicui, something new happened in town today. Tianbao, No. 1, took an oil boat down to Chenzhou, but had bad luck and drowned in the Citan Rapids."  

Cuicui didn't quite understand and seemed unmoved by the news at first. Grandfather continued:  

"Cuicui, it's true. Horseman Yang, who came here as a matchmaker last time, said I was wise not to agree to the marriage earlier!"

When the boat reached home, Grandfather gave an awkward smile and walked inside. Cuicui stayed to guard the boat but, hearing nothing from him for a long time, went back to check. She found him sitting on the doorstep weaving straw sandal straps.

"Grandfather, what's wrong?"  

"Tianbao really is dead! No. 2 is angry with us, thinking we had something to do with what happened to his family!"  

Someone shouted from the stream for the ferry, and Grandfather hurried out. Cuicui sat on a pile of straw in the corner of the room, her mind in a turmoil. When he still hadn't returned after a while, she began to cry.

Seventeen  

Grandfather seemed angry with someone, smiling less and paying less attention to Cuicui. Cuicui felt as if he no longer doted on her, though she didn't understand why. But it didn't last long; as time passed, things got better. They still lived their lives rowing the boat, everything the same, except that life seemed to have an invisible gap somewhere that could never be filled. When Grandfather went to River Street, Fleetmaster Shunshun still treated him kindly, but it was clear the fleetmaster hadn't forgotten the cause of the deceased's death. No. 2 had traveled six hundred miles down the North River to Chenzhou, searching along the riverbanks for his poor brother's remains, but found nothing. He posted notices at various tax stations and returned to Chadong. Not long after, he went to East Sichuan to buy goods. When crossing the ferry, he saw the old ferryman. The old ferryman, noticing that the young man seemed to have completely forgotten the past, spoke to him.

"No. 2, my Cuicui said one night in the fifth month, she had a dream…" He looked at No. 2 again, saw that he was neither surprised nor annoyed, and continued, "It was a strange dream. She said someone's voice floated her up in her dream, up to a cliff where she picked a tiger-ear plant!"

"But, but…"

No. 2 waited a while, glanced at the old ferryman, said nothing, smiled, and strode off with a porter carrying loads of vermicelli and white sugar.

"Nuosong, No. 2, that ferryman seems to like you a lot!"  

No. 2 said nothing, so the man continued:  

"No. 2, he asked if you want the mill or the ferryboat. Are you really planning to be his grandson-in-law and take over his ferry?"  

No. 2 laughed, and the man added:  

"No. 2, if it were up to me, I'd take the mill. A mill brings in seven sheng of rice and three dou of bran a day."

Eighteen  

A month passed uneventfully. The pain in everyone's hearts seemed to heal under the long, sunny days. The weather was especially hot, and people busied themselves sweating and cooling off with fermented rice soup in cold water, with little to worry about. Worries couldn't linger in such a life. Cuicui napped daily in the shade of the white pagoda, where it was cool, and the bamboo birds and other fowl in the mountains sang so soothingly that in her dreams, she was borne along by their songs, often having the most absurd dreams.  

This wasn't anyone's fault. Poets write entire volumes from a small incident, sculptors carve lifelike figures from stone, painters dash a bit of green, a bit of red, a bit of gray to create magical paintings—all because they're thinking of a smile's shadow or a frown's trace. Cuicui couldn't use words, stone, or color to transfer the love and hate in her heart to something else; instead, she let her heart roam over the most absurd things. From this secrecy, she often felt both startled and thrilled. The unknowable future shook her emotions intensely, and she couldn't entirely hide her foolishness from her grandfather.  

As for Grandfather, it could be said he knew everything. But in truth, he understood nothing. He knew Cuicui didn't dislike No. 2, but he didn't know how the young man felt. He had met with rebuffs from both the fleetmaster and No. 2, but he didn't lose heart.  

"Things need to be arranged properly to make sense. Everything is fated!" Thinking this, he felt matters were becoming more complicated. Awake, he dreamed even more wildly and expansively than his granddaughter Cuicui. He asked every local ferry passenger about No. 2 and his father, caring for them as if they were his own family. Yet strangely, this made him afraid to see the fleetmaster and No. 2. When he saw them, he didn't know what to say and would just rub his hands together in his old habit, losing his composure entirely. No. 2 and his father both understood his intentions, but the deceased had left a mournful impression in their hearts, so they both acted as if they didn't understand the old ferryman's meaning, passing the days as they did.  

Even though he clearly hadn't dreamed that night, when speaking to Cuicui in the morning, Grandfather would say:  

"Cuicui, Cuicui, I had a terrifying dream last night!"  

Cuicui would ask, "What terrifying dream?"  

Pretending to recall his dream, he would study Cuicui's small face and long eyebrows and tell her some wonderful dream he had made up while awake. Needless to say, those dreams weren't really frightening.  

All rivers must flow to the sea. No matter how far he started, he always ended up talking about the matter that made Cuicui blush. When Cuicui seemed displeased and showed signs of being a little embarrassed, the old ferryman would grow a bit scared, quickly explaining and using casual talk to cover the original intent of his words.  

"Cuicui, I didn't mean it that way. I'm old and muddled. Full of silly jokes."  

But sometimes Cuicui would quietly listen to Grandfather's silly, muddled jokes, even smiling to herself until the very end.  

Cuicui would suddenly say:  

"Grandfather, you really are a bit muddled!"  

Hearing this, Grandfather would fall silent. He would think, "I have a lot on my mind," but before he could say it, a ferry passenger would call him away.  

The weather was hot. A ferry passenger came from afar, carrying a seventy-pound load on his shoulder. When he reached the stream, seeking relief from the heat, he didn't rush to cross but crouched by the tea jar under a rock, drank some cold tea, swapped his "blow-pipe" tobacco pipe with a companion, and chatted with the ferryman.Many baseless rumors were spoken from this spot, and the old ferryman overheard them. Sometimes, because the stream was so clear, those crossing would linger to wash their feet or bathe by the bank, sitting longer and talking more. Grandfather would pass these talks on to Cuicui, and so she also learned many things—how the prices of goods rose and fell, the cost of riding a sedan chair or taking a boat, how the men steering rafts maneuvered their dozen-odd large oars when shooting the rapids, and how the big-footed women burned opium on the little tobacco boats... nothing was left out.

Nuosong, the Second Young Master, had returned to Chadong with goods from Eastern Sichuan. It was nearly dusk, and the stream was very still. Grandfather and Cuicui were in the vegetable garden checking on the radish seedlings. Cuicui had napped a bit too long during the day and felt a little lonely. Hearing what seemed like someone hoarsely calling for a ferry, she hurried down to the stream ahead of the others. As she descended the bank, she saw two men standing by the dock, their backs clearly visible in the slanting sunlight. It was none other than Nuosong, Second Young Master, and his family’s hired hand! Startled, like a small wild creature spotting a hunter, Cuicui turned and fled back into the bamboo grove on the hill. The old ferryman heard it all clearly but remained crouched in the garden counting his vegetables, finding it amusing. He had seen Cuicui run off and knew she must have recognized who was crossing. The people wanting to cross couldn’t get her to attend to them and had no choice but to keep shouting hoarsely for the ferry. While they waited quietly by the stream, the old ferryman in the garden thought to himself, “Could it be the Second Young Master?” As if afraid of upsetting Cuicui, he still stayed crouched and motionless.

Making wild guesses, the old ferryman ran down to the stream. When he got there, he saw the two men were already on the boat, and one of them was indeed the Second Young Master. “Ah, Second Young Master, you’re back!”

“Crossed! Without you on the boat, who would dare cast off?” said the hired hand, as a water bird skimmed across the stream. “The kingfisher’s gone back to its nest. We still need to get home for supper!”

“It’s early, plenty of time to reach River Street,” said the old ferryman, jumping onto the boat. And he thought to himself, “Isn’t it this boat you want to inherit?” As he pulled on the rope, the boat left the shore.

“Second Young Master, the journey must have been tiring!...”

The old ferryman spoke, but the Second Young Master listened without showing any feeling or giving any reply. When the boat reached the shore, the young man and his family’s hired hand shouldered their loads and crossed over the hill. That indifferent impression lingered in the old ferryman’s mind, so he clenched his fist and shook it three times behind their backs, giving a soft growl, then pulled the boat back. Nineteen

Cuicui had run into the bamboo grove, and the old ferryman took a long time to get off the boat. From Nuosong’s, the Second Young Master’s, perspective, this didn’t bode well for the future. Although the old ferryman’s every word hinted that “there’s hope in this matter,” his timid explanations were highly inappropriate. Thinking of his elder brother, the Second Young Master twisted the meaning of things. He felt somewhat resentful, somewhat angry. On the third day after returning home, someone from Middle Stockade came to sound him out. Staying at Shunshun’s house on River Street, this person asked Shunshun if the Second Young Master was still interested in that new mill. Shunshun then turned to ask the Second Young Master himself.

The Second Young Master said, “Father, if you think it’s for you—having an extra mill and an extra person in the house to make you happy—then you can agree. But if it’s for my sake, I need to think it over carefully. Let’s talk about it another day. I still don’t know whether I should have a mill or a ferry boat. Maybe fate only allows me to run a ferry!”

The man sounding things out committed this to memory and went back to Middle Stockade to report. When crossing at Green Creek Hill, he met the old ferryman. Remembering what the Second Young Master had said, he couldn’t help but smile. The old ferryman, learning he was from Middle Stockade, asked him what business he had in Chadong.

The man from Middle Stockade, knowing how to be discreet, said:

“No particular business. Just went to sit for a while at Fleetmaster Shunshun’s house on River Street.”

“One doesn’t visit the Temple of the Three Treasures without a reason! If you sat, there must have been talk!”

“Well, a few words were exchanged.”

“What was said?” The man said nothing more, but the old ferryman continued, “I hear you people from Middle Stockade want to give that big mill by the river, along with your family’s daughter, to Shunshun on River Street. Has there been any progress on that matter?”

The man from Middle Stockade laughed. “It’s settled. I asked Shunshun. He’s quite willing to become in-laws with someone from Middle Stockade. Then I asked the young man...”

“What did the young man think?”

“He said: I have a mill before me, and a ferry boat. I wanted the ferry at first, but now I’ve decided on the mill. The ferry is movable, not as fixed as the mill. That boy knows how to calculate.”

This man was a rice market broker; his words carried weight. He knew perfectly well what the “ferry boat” referred to, but he didn’t let on. Seeing the old ferryman’s lips twitch as if he wanted to speak, the man cut in:

“Everything is fate, not a bit up to us. Poor Shunshun’s eldest son, so handsome and dignified, yet he drowned in the river!”

These words stabbed the old ferryman’s heart, and he swallowed the question he was about to ask. After the man from Middle Stockade had gone ashore and left, the old ferryman stood gloomily at the prow of the boat, dazed for a long time. Recalling the Second Young Master’s aloof demeanor during the recent crossing, he felt deeply troubled.

Cuicui was playing happily under the tower. She went to the high bank by the stream, wanting her grandfather to sing. When he didn’t respond, she went down to the stream, complaining all the way. Only when she reached the bank did she see how dejected her grandfather looked, though she couldn’t understand why. Seeing Cuicui’s cheerful, dark face, the old ferryman gave a rough laugh. Someone was crossing with goods, so he said nothing, silently pulling the boat into the stream. Once in the middle, he burst into loud song. After ferrying the man across, he jumped onto the dock and approached Cuicui, still laughing roughly, and put his hand to his forehead.

Cuicui said:

“Grandfather, what’s wrong? Are you having sunstroke? Lie down in the shade and rest. I’ll take care of the boat!”

“You take care of the boat? Fine, this boat is yours to manage!”

As if he really had sunstroke, feeling tight in the chest, the old ferryman put on a strong front in front of Cuicui. But after going back into the house alone, he found some broken porcelain, scratched his arms and legs a few times to let out some dark blood, and then lay down on the bed to sleep.

Cuicui tended the boat herself, but felt a strange joy, thinking: “If Grandfather won’t sing for me, I’ll sing myself!”

She sang many songs. Lying in bed with his eyes closed, the old ferryman listened to every line, his heart in turmoil. But he knew this wasn’t a serious illness that could knock him down; he would still get up tomorrow. He thought about going into town the next day, to check on things at River Street, and many other things crossed his mind.

But the next day, though he got out of bed, his head was still heavy. Grandfather was indeed sick. Cuicui seemed more grown-up. She brewed him a pot of strong medicine and made him drink it, and also picked garlic sprouts from the garden behind the house to pickle in rice soup. While tending the boat, she kept finding time to run back to check on her grandfather, asking this and that. But grandfather said nothing, only suffering from a secret pain. After lying in bed for three days, he finally recovered. He walked around the house a bit, his bones still strong, but preoccupied by one thing, he prepared to go into town to River Street. Cuicui couldn’t see what urgent business he had that required going to town that very day and begged him not to go.

The old ferryman rubbed his hands together, wondering if he should tell her the reason. Cuicui’s dark, oval face and her limpid eyes made him sigh.

He said, “I have something important. I have to go today!”

Cuicui said with a wry smile, “How important could it be? Isn’t it just...”

Knowing Cuicui’s temper, and sensing her displeasure, the old ferryman stopped insisting on leaving. He put down the bamboo tube and embroidered waist pouch he had prepared to take, and said with a somewhat ingratiating smile, “Alright, I won’t go. If you’re worried I’ll fall and hurt myself, I won’t go. I thought it wouldn’t be too hot in the morning, and I could finish my business in town and come back. But if I can’t go today, I’ll go tomorrow!”

Cuicui said gently, in a soft voice, “Going tomorrow is fine. Your legs are still weak. Rest another day before getting up.”

The old ferryman seemed unwilling to accept this. Spreading his hands, he walked out, nearly tripping over a mallet for weaving straw sandals by the doorstep. Once he steadied himself, Cuicui said with a wry smile, “Grandfather, see? Still not convinced!” The old ferryman picked up the mallet and threw it into a corner of the room, saying, “Your grandfather is old! Just wait a few days, I’ll hunt a leopard for you to see!”

In the afternoon, there was a passing rain shower. The old ferryman, having discussed it properly with Cuicui, still went into town. Cuicui couldn’t accompany him, so she sent the yellow dog along. In town, an old acquaintance detained the ferryman for a long talk about the price of salt and rice. Then he went to the garrison headquarters to see the newly purchased mules and horses, before finally heading to Shunshun’s house on River Street. There, finding Shunshun playing cards with three others, he didn’t want to interrupt. He stood behind them, watching the game for a while. Later, Shunshun offered him a drink, but he declined, using his recent illness as an excuse. The card game continued, and the old ferryman didn’t want to leave just yet. Shunshun didn’t seem to grasp that he was waiting to speak, focusing only on his hand. Eventually, someone else noticed the old ferryman’s demeanor and asked if he had something to discuss. Awkwardly rubbing his large hands together in his usual manner, the old ferryman said he had nothing else, just wanted to have a word with the fleetmaster.

Only then did the fleetmaster understand why the old man had been watching the cards for so long. He turned and smiled at the old ferryman.

“Why didn’t you say so earlier? If you hadn’t, I’d have thought you were studying my plays!”

“It’s nothing, just a few words. I didn’t want to spoil your fun, so I didn’t dare speak up.”

The fleetmaster tossed his cards onto the table, laughed, and walked towards the back room. The old ferryman followed.

“What is it?” the fleetmaster asked, his expression seeming to already know what was coming, with a touch of pity.

“I heard from someone in Middle Stockade that you’re planning to become in-laws with the militia captain there. Is it true?”

Seeing the old ferryman’s eyes fixed on his face, seeking a satisfactory answer, the fleetmaster said, “There’s something to it.” His tone, however, implied: “So what if it is?”

The old ferryman said, “Is it true?”

The other replied coolly, “It’s true.” But the implication was still: “True, so what?”

Feigning composure, the old ferryman asked, “And the Second Young Master?”

The fleetmaster said, “The Second Young Master took a boat down to Taoyuan days ago!”

As it turned out, the Second Young Master had even argued with his father before leaving for Taoyuan. Although the fleetmaster was extremely open-minded by nature, he was understandably unwilling to have the girl indirectly responsible for his first son’s death become his second son’s wife. According to local custom, such matters were considered children’s affairs, beyond the adults’ interference. And the Second Young Master truly liked Cuicui, and Cuicui loved him too. He didn’t oppose such a love-match marriage. But somehow, the old ferryman’s concern over this matter had caused some misunderstanding between the father and son towards him. Thinking of the recent family tragedy, the fleetmaster felt it was all related to this old, meddlesome ferryman. Though he showed no sign of it, a knot had formed in his heart.

Before the old ferryman could speak again, the fleetmaster said, somewhat more bluntly:

“Uncle, let it be. Our mouths are only for drinking now, not for singing our children’s songs! I understand your intentions perfectly; you mean well. But I also ask you to understand mine. I think we should only discuss matters within our own domain, not meddle in the affairs of the young.”

Struck down by this blunt blow, the old ferryman still wanted to say a few words, but the fleetmaster wouldn’t give him the chance, pulling him back to the card table.

Having nothing more to say, the old ferryman looked at the fleetmaster. Though he was smiling and telling jokes, his heart seemed heavy; he threw his cards forcefully onto the table. Without a word, the old ferryman put on his conical hat and left.

It was still early. Feeling very displeased, the old ferryman went back into town to find Horseman Yang. The horseman was drinking. Although the old ferryman used his illness as an excuse, he couldn’t avoid having three or five cups. Returning to Green Creek Hill, he was overheated from the walk and washed himself with stream water. Feeling very tired, he told Cuicui to tend the boat.Went home to sleep by himself.  

The weather was oppressive at dusk, with red dragonflies darting all over the creek. Clouds had gathered in the sky, and the hot wind rustled the bamboo groves on both mountains with such force that it seemed sure to rain heavily by night. Cuicui stayed by the ferry boat, watching the dragonflies flit back and forth over the creek, her heart in turmoil. Seeing the worried look on her grandfather's face, she grew anxious and hurried back home. At first, she thought her grandfather must have gone to bed early, but instead found him sitting on the doorstep weaving straw sandals!  

"Grandfather, how many pairs of sandals do you need? Aren't there still fourteen pairs by the head of the bed? Why don't you lie down and rest?"  

The old ferryman said nothing, but stood up, raised his head to look at the sky, and said softly:  

"Cuicui, there's going to be a heavy rain and loud thunder tonight! Later, let's tie our boat under the cliff—this rain will be fierce."  

Cuicui said, "Grandfather, I'm really scared!" What Cuicui feared didn't seem to be the coming thunderstorm at night.  

The old ferryman seemed to understand her meaning and said, "What's there to fear? Whatever must come will come—there's no need to be afraid!"

That night, heavy rain indeed fell, accompanied by terrifying thunderclaps. When lightning flashed across the ridge of the roof, a deafening thunderbolt followed immediately. Cuicui trembled in the dark. Her grandfather woke up, knowing she was afraid, and worried she might catch cold, so he got up and draped a cotton sheet over her. Grandfather said:  

"Cuicui, don't be afraid!"  

Cuicui said, "I'm not afraid!" She wanted to add, "With you here, Grandfather, I'm not afraid!" Another thunderbolt crashed, followed by a deep, rumbling sound of collapse that drowned out the rain. Both thought it must be the cliff by the creek bank caving in, fearing that the ferry boat might be crushed under the rocks.  

The grandfather and granddaughter lay silently in bed, listening to the sound of rain and thunder.  

But no matter how heavy the rain, before long, Cuicui fell asleep anyway. When she woke up, it was already dawn, and the rain had stopped at some unknown hour, leaving only the sound of water rushing from the mountain gullies on both sides into the creek. Cuicui got up, saw that her grandfather still seemed to be sleeping soundly, opened the door, and went outside. In front of the door, a ditch had formed, with a stream of water gurgling from behind the pagoda and cascading straight down from the front cliff. Temporary water channels crisscrossed everywhere. The vegetable garden by the house had been washed away by the mountain torrent, the seedlings buried in coarse sand and mud. Walking further to look at the creek, she saw that it had swollen greatly, flooding the dock, with the waterline nearly reaching the tea jar. The path down to the dock was like a small river, gushing with yellow muddy water. The cable that stretched across the creek for the ferry was submerged, and the boat moored under the cliff was nowhere to be seen.  

Cuicui saw that the cliff in front of the house hadn't collapsed, so she didn't immediately notice the loss of the ferry boat. But after a while, when she searched high and low for it and couldn't find it, she turned around casually and saw that the white pagoda behind the house was gone. Shocked beyond measure, she hurried to the back of the house and found that the pagoda had indeed collapsed, with a huge pile of bricks and stones scattered in disarray. Terrified and at a loss, Cuicui screamed for her grandfather. He didn't get up or respond, so she rushed back inside, shook him by the bed for a long time, but still he made no sound. The old man had passed away as the thunderstorm was dying down.  

Cuicui burst into loud sobs.  

After a while, a courier traveling from Chadong to eastern Sichuan reached the creek bank and called for the ferry from across the stream. Cuicui was in the kitchen, crying as she heated water to bathe her deceased grandfather.  

The man, thinking the ferryman's family was still asleep and eager to cross, threw small stones across the creek when no one answered, hitting the roof. Cuicui came out, her face a mess of tears and snot, and stood on the high bank by the creek.  

"Hey, it's late! Row the boat over!"  

"The boat's gone!"  

"What's your grandfather doing? He's in charge of the boat—it's his responsibility!"  

"He's been in charge of the boat, for fifty years—he's dead!"  

Cuicui sobbed as she shouted to the man across the stream. Realizing the old ferryman was dead, the man knew he had to go into town to report the news. He said:  

"Really dead? Don't cry, I'll go back and let them know, have them bring a boat and supplies!"  

When the man returned to Chadong, he told acquaintances the news, and soon everyone inside and outside the town knew. The boat chief, Shunshun, on River Street, sent someone to find an empty boat, bring a plain wooden coffin, and immediately row to Bixiju. Meanwhile, Horseman Yang of the town, along with an old soldier, rushed to Bixiju, cut down dozens of large bamboo poles, and wove them into a raft with kudzu vines to serve as a temporary ferry. After finishing the raft, they poled it to the bank near Cuicui's house. The old soldier stayed to ferry people across, while Horseman Yang went to Cuicui's house to see the deceased, his eyes glistening with tears. He touched the stiff old friend lying on the bed, then hurriedly attended to necessary tasks. Later, helpers arrived, and the coffin came by boat from the big river. The old Daoist priest from town also came, carrying many ritual tools, an old linen robe, and a large rooster, to perform the rites of chanting and purification for free, crossing over on the raft. People came and went in the house, while Cuicui sat on a low stool by the stove, sobbing quietly.  

By noon, Boat Chief Shunshun arrived as well, accompanied by a man carrying a sack of rice, a jar of wine, and a leg of pork. Seeing Cuicui, he said:  

"Cuicui, I know your grandfather has passed away. Old people must die—don't worry, I'll take care of everything!" After looking around, he left.  

By afternoon, the body was encoffined, and some helpers went home. By evening, only the old Daoist priest, Horseman Yang, and two young laborers sent by Shunshun's family remained. Before dusk, the Daoist priest cut some flowers from red and green paper and made candle stands from yellow clay. After dark, yellow nine-grade candles were lit on the small table before the coffin, incense was burned, and small candles were placed around the coffin. The old priest donned the blue linen robe and began the funeral ritual of circling the coffin. The priest led the way with a small paper banner, followed by the "filial daughter" Cuicui, with the horseman bringing up the rear, slowly circling the lonely coffin. The two laborers stood in the empty space by the stove, banging gongs and cymbals haphazardly. The priest walked with closed eyes, chanting and humming to comfort the departed soul. When he mentioned the Western Paradise of eternal bliss where flowers bloom in all seasons, the old horseman scattered paper flowers from a wooden tray high over the coffin, symbolizing that realm.  

By midnight, the rites were done, firecrackers were set off, and the candles were nearly spent. Cuicui, her eyes swollen with tears, hurried to the stove to prepare a late-night meal for the helpers. After eating, the old Daoist priest lay down crookedly on the deceased's bed and fell asleep. The remaining few had to keep vigil by the coffin as custom demanded. The old horseman sang funeral songs for everyone, using an empty wooden rice measure as a small drum, tapping it "pat pat pat" as he sang—songs about "Wang Xiang lying on ice" and "Huang Xiang fanning the pillow."  

Cuicui had cried all day and been busy all day, and by now was utterly exhausted. She rested her head against the coffin and dozed off. The two laborers and the horseman, after their meal and a couple of cups of wine, were still full of energy and took turns singing funeral songs. But soon, Cuicui woke again, as if startled from a dream, realized her grandfather was dead, and began to sob softly once more.  

"Cuicui, Cuicui, don't cry—crying won't bring the dead back!"  

Bald-headed Chen Sisi then told a joke about a crying bride, laced with a few coarse words, which made the two laborers chuckle for a long time. The yellow dog barked outside. Cuicui opened the front door and stood outside for a while, hearing insects chirping everywhere, the moonlight beautiful in the sky, and bright stars embedded in the deep blue firmament, serene and gentle. Cuicui thought:  

"Is this real? Is Grandfather truly dead?"  

The old horseman had been following behind her, knowing that a girl's heart can be narrow—she might hide her sorrow like embers buried in ash, and after her grandfather's death, feeling all hope lost, she might jump off a cliff or hang herself to follow him. So he kept a watchful eye on Cuicui.  

Seeing Cuicui standing there in a daze for a long time without turning back, the old horseman coughed and called out:  

"Cuicui, the dew has fallen—aren't you cold?"  

"I'm not cold."  

"The weather is wonderful!"  

"Ah..." A large shooting star made Cuicui cry out softly.  

Then another meteor streaked across the southern sky. An owl hooted from across the creek.  

"Cuicui," the old horseman said, standing shoulder to shoulder with her, very gently, "go inside and sleep—don't let your mind wander!"  

Cuicui silently returned to the front of her grandfather's coffin, sat on the ground, and began to sob again. The two laborers guarding the house had already fallen asleep.  

Horseman Yang said softly, "Don't cry! Don't cry! Your grandfather would be sad too—what good does it do to cry your eyes out and hoarse your throat? Listen to me, I know all about your grandfather's wishes. I'll handle everything. I'll arrange everything properly, to honor your grandfather. I can manage, I can do anything. I'll find someone your grandfather and you would both like to take over this ferry! If it doesn't go our way, old as I am, I can still take up a sickle and fight them. Cuicui, don't worry—I'll take care of everything!..."  

Somewhere far off, a rooster crowed. The old Daoist priest muttered drowsily from the bed, "Is it dawn? Too early!"

Early the next morning, helpers came from town carrying ropes and carrying poles.  

When the old ferryman's small white wooden coffin was carried by six men to the collapsed pagoda's hillside for burial, Boat Chief Shunshun, the horseman, Cuicui, the old Daoist priest, and the yellow dog all followed behind. At the pre-dug pit, the priest first jumped in according to ritual, placing some cinnabar grains and white rice in the four corners and the center, burned some paper money, and climbed out, signaling the coffin bearers to lower it. Cuicui, hoarse from crying, threw herself over the coffin and refused to let go. Only after the horseman forcefully pulled her away could the coffin be moved. Soon, it was lowered into the pit, the ropes were pulled out, the direction adjusted, and fresh earth covered it. Cuicui sat on the ground, sobbing. The old priest returned to town to perform other rites, crossing the stream on the raft. Boat Chief entrusted everything to the old horseman and hurried back to town as well. The helpers washed their hands by the creek, each having their own matters to attend to. Knowing the family's situation, they didn't want to impose further and crossed back home without disturbing the household. Thus, only three remained at Bixiju: Cuicui, the old horseman, and Bald-headed Chen Sisi, temporarily sent by the boat chief's family to help manage the ferry. The yellow dog, having been hit with a stone by the bald man, seemed displeased with him and kept barking softly.  

By afternoon, Cuicui discussed with the old horseman, asking him to return to town to entrust his horse to the garrison men and then come back to Bixiju to stay with her. When the horseman returned to Bixiju, Bald-headed Chen Sisi was sent back to town.  

Cuicui continued to run the ferry herself with the yellow dog, letting the old horseman sit on the high bank by the creek to relax or sing to her in his old, raspy voice.  

Three days later, Boat Chief came to discuss taking Cuicui to live at his home, but Cuicui wanted to guard her grandfather's grave and was unwilling to go to town immediately. She only asked the boat chief to speak to the yamen in town, allowing Horseman Yang to stay with her temporarily. Boat Chief Shunshun agreed and left.  

Horseman Yang, being over fifty, was even better at telling stories than Cuicui's grandfather. Moreover, he was especially attentive, diligent, and tidy, so living with him made Cuicui feel as if she had lost a grandfather but gained an uncle. When people asked about her poor grandfather during crossings, or when she thought of him at dusk, it made Cuicui's heart ache and feel desolate. But as such sorrowful days stretched on, the pain gradually faded. Every dusk and evening, the two would sit on the high bank by the creek in front of the house, talking about the poor grandfather lying in the damp earth—stories Cuicui had never known before, which softened her heart. They also spoke of Cuicui's father, that soldier who cherished both love and honor, and how his green-standard army attire had stirred girls' hearts. They talked of Cuicui's mother, how skilled she was at singing, and how popular her songs had been in those days.  

Times had changed, and everything was naturally different—the emperor no longer ruled the land, let alone ordinary people! The old horseman thought of his younger days as a groom, leading his horse to Bixiju to serenade Cuicui's mother, who paid him no heed. Now, he had become the sole support and confidant of this orphaned girl, and couldn't help but smile bitterly.  

Because they talked every dusk about her grandfather and family matters, they eventually came to discuss everything before the old ferryman's death. Thus, Cuicui learned many things her grandfather had never mentioned in life: Erlao's singing, the death of Shunshun's eldest son, the coldness of Shunshun and his son toward her grandfather, and how the Middle Village family used a mill as a dowry to tempt Nuosong Erlao. Erlao, still haunted by his brother's death, and having received no response from Cuicui, was also being pressured by his family to accept the mill.Still brooding over the ferry crossing, she went down in a huff. How could her grandfather's death be related to Cuicui? ... Everything that Cuicui had failed to understand, she now fully grasped. After realizing the truth, Cuicui cried all night long.

After the seventh week of mourning, Fleetmaster Shunshun sent someone to invite the horseman into town to discuss bringing Cuicui to his home as Erlao's wife. But since Erlao was still in Chenzhou, they decided to put the matter aside for now. Instead, Cuicui would move to a house on River Street and wait until Erlao returned to see his intentions. The horseman thought this decision should be asked of Cuicui. When he came back, he conveyed Shunshun's intentions to Cuicui and then offered his own advice—since her status was not yet settled, it would be inappropriate to live in a stranger's home. It would be better for her to stay at Bixi Ju and wait until Erlao returned with his boat to see what he thought.

After this plan was decided, the old horseman, believing that Erlao would return soon, entrusted his horse to the care of the camp attendants and stayed with Cuicui at Bixi Ju, letting the days pass one by one.

The white pagoda at Bixi Ju was tied to the feng shui of Chadong. Now that it had collapsed, rebuilding it was naturally necessary. Apart from contributions from the town's garrison, tax bureau, merchants, and ordinary folks, people from various large stockades also took registers to collect donations. Since the pagoda was not built for the benefit of any single person but for everyone to accumulate merit and blessings, everyone should have the chance to donate. Thus, a large bamboo tube with knots at both ends and a slit cut in the middle was placed on the ferry for passersby to freely drop in their money. When the tube was full, the horseman would carry it into town to the elders in charge and bring back another tube. When ferry passengers saw that the old boatman was gone and that Cuicui had a white string tied in her braid, they understood that the old man had finished his work and was quietly lying in the earth. They would cast sympathetic glances at Cuicui while fumbling for coins to stuff into the bamboo tube. "May heaven bless you. May the departed go to the Western Paradise, and the living enjoy eternal peace." Cuicui understood the meaning behind the donors' actions, and her heart ached. She quickly turned her back to pull the boat.

By winter, the collapsed white pagoda had been rebuilt. But the young man who had sung under the moonlight, whose songs had gently lifted Cuicui's soul in her dreams, had not yet returned to Chadong.
