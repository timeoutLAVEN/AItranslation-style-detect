Section [1]: Preface to "Border Town"

For farmers and soldiers, I have an ineffable warmth and affection. This feeling can be found everywhere in all my works. I have never concealed this emotion. I grew up in the kind of small rural town described in my works. My grandfather, father, and brothers were all in the military: those who died all died in service, those who did not would surely end their lives in service. Speaking from what I have witnessed, narrating their loves and hates, sorrows and joys, even if this pen is somewhat clumsy, at least it will not stray too far from the topic. That is because they are upright, honest, with some aspects of life that are extremely great, while others are utterly ordinary. Some parts of their nature are extremely beautiful, others are rather trivial—when I set out to write about them, to make them more human and true to life, I naturally wrote in an honest and straightforward way. But because of this, this work may not avoid becoming something useless.

Judging by the current climate, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are easily led to negative feelings by works like this. The former, posing as "progressive," declare that China doesn't need this type of work; the latter, "overly worried about being outdated," do not want to read this kind of work at present. This, of course, is true. What is "outdated"? A slightly rational person might never truly understand, but who among the majority is not afraid of being "outdated"? I want to say this: "I did not write this book for such a majority." Those who have read three to five foreign books on literary theory and criticism, or even read a pile of classical and modern world masterpieces, often do not have enough life experience to know, beyond their "erudition," a little about another side of China, about other happenings elsewhere. So even if this work meets certain literary theories and gets praised by critics, such reviews still end up being an insult to the author. Since they have no desire to understand the real love, hate, sorrow, and joy of this nation, they cannot truly judge the gains and losses of this work—this book was not written for them. As for art lovers, be they university or secondary school students, mostly inhabiting the more densely populated cities, they often honestly and naively spend some precious time reading newly published literary books from within China. Yet they are jointly controlled by literary theorists, critics, clever publishers, and rumor-mongering newsmen of the literary world. Meanwhile, their daily lives are too removed from the world mentioned in this work. They do not need such works, and this book does not seek their approval. Theorists can consult all kinds of literary theory from international publications and are never at a loss for something to say; critics have enough authors and works to praise or denounce as favors and grudges demand, keeping them busy for a lifetime. As for the majority of readers, whatever their tastes or beliefs, there are always works for them to read. Just because we care about the general reader, isn’t it so that many people, claiming to serve the general reader, spin endlessly like a top? Even if this book is not abandoned by the leading theorists and critics, and is not entirely rejected by the majority of led readers, as the author, I have long since made up my mind to give up on this "majority."

This book is meant for those who "have already left school, or never had any chance to attend school, yet can read some Chinese, work in professions untouched by literary theory, criticism, and gossip, live in that kind of society, and truly care about the nation's welfare in space and time." They truly know what the countryside is today, and want to know what it was in the past. They would surely also want to learn through this book something about rural life and soldiers in a small corner of the world. The world I've written about may be completely unfamiliar to them, but their open-mindedness, their zeal to seek comfort and knowledge in a book, will surely let them read this book calmly and at ease. Nor am I stopping here, I also intend to give them grounds for comparison. In another work, I will describe the civil war of the past twenty years, and show how some frontline farmers, with their characters and souls crushed by brute force, lost their original simplicity, thriftiness, peacefulness, and rectitude, and became something entirely new. How, under oppression and the poison of opium, they turned poor and lazy! I will narrate simply the woes of small people swept along by history toward an unknown destiny, and, out of malnutrition, their ideas and yearnings for "survival" and "how to survive." My readers should be rational, their rationality rooted in concern for the changes in Chinese society, knowing where this nation's greatness and present decline lie, each working in solitude for national revival. This work may only offer them a nostalgic melancholy, a bitter smile, or perhaps a nightmare, but at the same time, who knows, it may give them a measure of courage and confidence!

Written on April 24, 1934

Section [2]: Chapter 1

1  

Traveling from Sichuan into Hunan, there's an official road to the east. Near the western border of Hunan, this road arrives at a small mountain town named "Chadong." There, beside a small creek, stands a white pagoda, and beneath it a lone household lives. They are just an old man, a young girl, and a yellow dog.

The creek winds around the mountains and, after about three li, flows into the main river at Chadong. By crossing the creek and going over a small hill, it is only one li to the edge of Chadong town. The creek flows like a drawn bow, and the mountain path is like the bowstring—a small difference in distance is thus made. The creek is about twenty zhang wide, its bed paved with large stone slabs. The water, always still and clear as crystal, is so deep in places that even a pole cannot reach the bottom, yet the swimming fish in the stream can be counted individually. Since this creek is the main passageway between Sichuan and Hunan, the water often rises and falls. But limited by funds and unable to build a bridge, people set up a square-headed ferryboat. The ferry can carry about twenty passengers with their horses across at a time, and when there are more people, it makes multiple trips. At the head of the boat stands a short bamboo pole with a movable iron ring. On both creek banks, a stretch of abandoned cable is fastened inside water troughs. When someone needs to cross, the iron ring is hooked onto the cable, and someone aboard then pulls the boat across slowly by hand along the cable. As the boat draws near the shore, the one managing the ferry shouts "Slowly, slowly" as they jump nimbly ashore, pull the iron ring, and everyone, goods, oxen, and horses all disembark and disappear over the ridge. The ferry is publicly owned, so passengers need not pay. Some, feeling uneasy, toss a handful of coins onto the deck, but the ferryman always picks them up, stuffing them back into the person's hand with the seriousness of a quarrel: "I've got my measure—three dou of rice, seven hundred coins—that's enough. Who wants this!"

But that's not how it works; everyone wants to set their mind at ease, and who can resist offering something for hard work? No matter what, some still leave money. The ferryman, unable to resist, also for his peace of mind, asks someone to buy tea and coarse tobacco from Chadong with the money. He ties up the best coarse tobacco from Chadong in bundles, hanging them at his belt and freely giving to any passengers who need it. Sometimes, if he feels from the way travelers look that they're eyeing his tobacco with interest, he tucks a small bundle into the traveler's pack, saying, "Don't smoke this? This is great, really splendid, tastes wonderful, makes a great gift too!" As for the tea, in June he puts it in a large jar and makes tea with boiled water to quench travelers' thirst.

The one who manages this ferry is the old man living under the pagoda. Having lived for seventy years, he has guarded the creek since he was twenty, and doesn't know how many people he has ferried over the past fifty years. Though of such an age and really should be resting, fate doesn't allow it—he seems unable to part from this life. He never considers the significance of his duty for himself; he simply lives quietly and faithfully as always. It's as if fate itself, instead of letting him die when the sun sets, each sunrise gives him the strength to live. The one who keeps him company, so that sunset does not mean dying along with the daylight, is the girl by his side. His only friends: a ferryboat and a yellow dog; his only family: the girl.

The girl's mother, the old boatman's only daughter, had, fifteen years ago, become secretly involved with a Chadong soldier, hiding it from her honest father. After she gave birth to a child, the garrison soldier wanted to persuade her to flee downriver with him. But in considering escape, one would break military duty, the other would have to leave her lonely father. After much consideration, seeing she lacked the courage to run, and he unwilling to tarnish his name as a soldier, he thought: if we cannot live together, then at least no one can stop us from dying together. He took poison first. The woman, however, could not harden herself against the life in her womb; unable to decide, she hesitated. The whole affair came to the boatman's knowledge, but he never uttered a harsh word, acting as if he had never heard anything, simply carrying on life calmly. The daughter, shamed yet pitiful, still stayed by her father's side, and after the child was born, drank too much cold water by the creek and died. By something like a miracle, the orphan girl grew up, and in the blink of an eye she was thirteen. Since the home was surrounded by green bamboo on both sides, the old boatman casually gave this poor orphan a name from the colors around, "Cuicui."

Cuicui grew up in the wind and sun, her skin tanned dark, everywhere surrounded by green hills and clear water, her eyes bright as crystal. Nature reared and taught her, so she was innocent and lively, like a small animal in every way. She was clever, like a young yellow deer among the hills, never imagining cruel things, never fretting, never losing her temper. When strangers paid attention to her on the boat, she'd stare right back at them with wide eyes, ready to dart deep into the mountains, but when she saw they had no ill intent, she'd return to playing by the water as before.

The old boatman, rain or shine, always guarded the boat. When someone needed to cross, he'd stoop a little, both hands guiding the bamboo cable, pulling the boat across the creek. Sometimes, tired out, he'd fall asleep on the big rock by the bank. If someone on the far side waved and called for a crossing, Cuicui would stop her grandpa from getting up and deftly jump aboard to ferry the traveler herself—she handled everything smoothly and never made a mistake. Sometimes she and her grandpa and the yellow dog were all aboard together, and during the crossing, she'd help him. When the boat neared shore and grandpa was calling, "Slowly, slowly," the yellow dog would be the first to leap out with the rope in its mouth, expertly tugging the boat to land as if it knew exactly how to do its duty.

On clear, mild days when no one was crossing, grandpa and Cuicui would sit on the big rock in front of their door and sun themselves. Sometimes, they'd toss a stick into the water from high up and urge the yellow dog to leap from the rock and fetch it. Sometimes, Cuicui and the dog, both alert, would listen to grandpa telling stories of old war days in the town. Other times, grandpa and Cuicui would each play bamboo flutes, making the tunes for wedding processions and sending off brides. When someone appeared at the ferry, the old boatman would put down the flute and go alone to the boat, ferrying people across. From back on the rock, one could see the boat set off, and in a high voice shout:

"Grandpa, grandpa, listen to me play, you sing!"

Reaching midstream, grandpa would sing with joy, his hoarse voice and the flute's sound echoing through the silent air, making the creek seem a bit livelier. (In fact, the returning echoes made everything seem even quieter.)

Sometimes the passengers were young cattle from eastern Sichuan bound for Chadong, sheep, or brides in wedding sedans, and Cuicui insisted on acting as ferryman, standing lazily at the bow, pulling the cable to let the boat drift slowly across. Once cattle, sheep, or wedding sedan disembarked, Cuicui followed them, standing on the little hill and watching until they disappeared far away before she returned to bring the boat back to the home side. She would softly imitate the bleat of lambs or the low of cows, or pick some wildflowers to tie in her hair, playing at being a bride all by herself.

Chadong was only one li from the ferry. When they needed to buy oil or salt, or for a festive drink, grandpa wouldn't go, but sent Cuicui and the yellow dog into town for the things. At the grocery, there were loads of vermicelli, huge jars of sugar, firecrackers, red candles—all making a huge impression on Cuicui, and on her return, she would spend half the day telling grandpa about it. There by the river also gathered many cargo boats unloading goods—a hundred or more boatmen busy, their boats much bigger and more interesting than the ferry.Cuicui also found it hard to forget.

The town of Chadong was built by the water and upon the mountains, with the city wall on the side closest to the hills winding like a long snake along the slopes. On the waterfront side, there was a space outside the city left to form a dock, where small covered boats were moored. When boats traveled downstream, they carried tung oil, green salt, and dyed wooden sticks. On the way upriver, they carried cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, sundries, and seafood. Connecting the various docks was a river street; most of the houses here were half on land, half on the water. Because space was limited, every house had stilted floors. When the river swelled with spring water, and as water gradually flowed into the street, every household on the river street would use a long ladder, one end placed on the eaves and the other on the city wall. Amid curses and shouts, everyone, carrying their bundles, bedding, and rice jars, would enter the city using ladders and only exit through the city gates once the water had receded. In a year when the flood was especially strong, inevitably a stilted house or two along the river would be swept away, and everyone would watch mutely from atop the city walls. Even those who suffered the loss would only stand and stare, as if there were nothing to say about their misfortune, much like when other irreparable disasters, decreed by nature, occurred before their eyes. During the flood, one could also stand on the city wall and gaze at how the river suddenly widened. Here the current rushed mightily, bringing with it houses, cattle, sheep, and big trees floating and sinking from upstream. So, where the river slowed, in front of the river customs pontoon, people would often set out in small boats. Upon spotting a floating animal, a piece of stray wood, or an empty boat with the cries of a woman or a child aboard, they would quickly paddle out, catching up downstream, tying the object securely with a long rope, and rowing it toward the shore. These honest and brave people, motivated by both profit and righteousness, were just like others of the area. Whether rescuing people or salvaging goods, they moved with agility and courage as though it were an enjoyable adventure, earning the admiration of all.

That river is the historically renowned You River, now called the Bai River. Downstream from Bai River, after merging with the Yuan River at Chenzhou, the water becomes slightly turbid, with the quality of spring water coming from the mountains. If you head upstream, the deep pools, three to five zhang deep, are crystal clear to the bottom. By day, the riverbed and its tiny white stones, patterned agates, are all visible. Fish swimming through the water look as if they’re floating through air. The banks are mostly high mountains, and the mountains are full of slender bamboo suitable for papermaking, their deep green color lasting year-round, dazzling the eyes. Homes by the water are mostly hidden among peach and apricot blossoms. In spring, wherever there are peach blossoms, there are households; wherever there are households, there is wine for sale. In summer, purple floral cloth garments, dazzling in the sunlight, serve as flags marking where houses are. When autumn and winter come, the houses on the cliffs and by the water become all the more distinct. The yellow mud walls and dark roof tiles are always flawlessly positioned, in perfect harmony with the surroundings, so that one's first impression is supremely pleasant. For a traveler with even a slight interest in poetry or painting, spending thirty days curled up in a small boat on this river would never be tedious, because everywhere miracles abound; there’s no end to the boldness and the subtlety of nature here, every detail capturing the heart and stirring the soul.

The Bai River originates from the border of Sichuan. In spring, when the river rises, boats can travel upriver directly to Xiushan in Sichuan. But within Hunan’s borders, Chadong is the last port. The river at Chadong is half a li wide, but in autumn and winter when the water drops, the stream narrows to less than twenty zhang, the rest of the riverbed scattered with green stones. After the boats reach here, they can go no further, so goods bound for eastern Sichuan are loaded and unloaded at this location. Exports are carried on shoulder poles made of fir, shouldered by porters. Imports are also bundled and carried away by hand from here.

Within the town, there is only one company of garrison troops, drawn from the old “Green Standard” battalion, and about five hundred households. (Of these, a portion own some mountain fields and oil presses, or are small capitalists lending money and storing oil, rice, or cotton yarn; the rest are mostly descendants of the old garrison soldiers who settled here with military household registration.) There’s also a local tax office, its administration set in a small temple under the river street outside the city, always hung with a long vertical banner. The bureau chief lives in the city. The company of soldiers are stationed at the old garrison command office; apart from buglers who climb the city walls daily and play their horns, reminding people that soldiers are still stationed here, the rest of the soldiers are almost invisible. In the white days of winter, if you walk into the city, you see clothes and greens drying in front of every household. Sweet potatoes, still on the vines, hang under the eaves. Bags made from palm fiber, stuffed with chestnuts, hazelnuts, and other hard-shelled nuts, are also often hung under the eaves. In every corner, chickens roam and play. Sometimes, men occupy the threshold, sawing wood or splitting logs, stacking the chopped firewood into pagoda-like towers in the yard. Or you might see several middle-aged women, dressed in stiff, freshly laundered blue cotton clothing, white cloth aprons with embroidered blossoms on their chests, bending over as they chat and work in the sunlight. Everything is forever so quiet; every day, the people here pass their lives in this kind of simple solitude. Each bit of tranquility gives one more capacity for reflection on “human affairs”—and for dreaming. Everyone living in this small town must also, in their allotted days, secretly harbor hopes and expectations regarding the loves and hatreds of life. But what are these people thinking? Who knows. From high ground in the city, just stepping out the front door lets you overlook the river and its scenery. When boats approach, one can watch, from across the sands on the far shore, countless towmen hauling the boats up. Some of them have brought small sweets and candies from downstream, which they’ll carry into the city to sell when they dock. When the boats arrive, children's imaginations run to those boat haulers. As for the adults: hatching a brood of chicks, raising two pigs, asking a passing boatman to bring back a pair of gold earrings, two zhang of official blue cloth, a jar of good soy sauce, or a double-wick kerosene lamp shade—these take up most of a housewife’s concerns.

Though the town is so quiet and peaceful, the fact that it's the commercial gateway to eastern Sichuan makes the little river street outside the city a little different. There are guesthouses where merchants stay, stationary barbershops, and also restaurants, general stores, oil shops, salt depots, fabric shops—all lining and decorating the riverside street. There are also shops selling sandalwood pulleys, bamboo cables, iron pots for boat use, and homes that introduce sailors to dock work. In front of the small eateries, there will often be long tables displaying golden fried carp with tofu, garnished with thin slivers of red pepper, laying in shallow bowls, with bundles of red chopsticks stuck into big bamboo tubes nearby. Anyone who wants, after paying a little, can sit by the table, grab a pair of chopsticks, and a woman with thin-plucked eyebrows and powdered face will come over and ask, “Mister, sir, do you want sweet wine? Or spirits?” The bolder, more mischievous men—especially those with an eye for the woman in charge—will act offended and say, “Drink sweet wine? I’m not a child, stop asking about sweet wine!” Then, the strong spirits will be scooped from a large vat with a bamboo ladle and poured straight into a clay bowl, instantly arriving at the table. The general store sells Meifu oil and Meifu oil lamps, along with incense and paper offerings. The oil shop stocks tung oil, the salt depot piles green salt from the well, the fabric shop sells white cotton yarn, large bolts of cloth, cotton, and the black crepe headscarves. Shops for boat supplies sell all sorts of goods, sometimes with iron anchors weighing over a hundred catties laid by the doorway waiting for customers. The homes that introduce sailors to port work always keep their doors open, with boat owners in satin coats and rough-handed sailors coming and going; it looks like a teahouse, but no tea is sold, nor is it a smoking den, though one can freely smoke. Though the talk is supposedly about shipping business, most matters—like boat assignments and labor distribution—follow set rules, and numbers need not be discussed. Most visitors here are actually just “socializing.” With the local syndicate boss as host, they discuss local news, inter-provincial business affairs, and “new happenings” downstream. When organizing clubs or collecting funds, most gatherings are held here; dice games for taking turns as club leader also often take place here. The business affairs that most concern them boil down to just two things: buying and selling boats, and buying and selling wives.

As in big cities, where commerce breeds a certain class of hangers-on necessitated by the needs of merchants and sailors, this little border town’s riverside street also has its own such crowd gathered in houses with stilted floors. These women were either recruited from nearby villages or followed Sichuan soldiers to Hunan and ended up drifting here, dressed in fake silk tops, printed cotton pants, eyebrows plucked into fine lines, with thickly oiled and fragrance-laden hair piled up high. With nothing to do in the daytime, they’d sit at the door making shoes, embroidering twin phoenixes in red and green thread on the toes, or sewing embroidered pouches for their sailor lovers; all the while watching passersby, letting the long days slip past. Or they would lean by the riverside window watching sailors unload goods, listening to their songs as they scurried up and down the masts. Come nightfall, they would take turns entertaining merchants and sailors, wholeheartedly performing the duties expected of a “prostitute.”

Yet, because the local customs are so simple and honest, even as “prostitutes”, they remain guileless. When dealing with strangers, money must be paid first before the door is shut for business, but once they know someone, sometimes money becomes optional. Prostitutes mostly depend on Sichuan merchants to make a living, but real affections are more often tied to the sailors. When feelings run deep, they bite each other’s lips or necks and swear oaths, promising that “after we part, neither of us is allowed to stray.” So, for forty or fifty days—the one afloat on the boat and the one left ashore—each spends these days in a daze, heart bound tightly to a single distant person. Especially, the women’s feelings are so true and passionate it defies description. If the man doesn’t return as promised, she always dreams of the boat pulling up to shore and the man, unsteady, coming down the gangplank and running straight to her. If she’s suspicious in the daytime, then in her dreams she’ll see the man singing at the mast, but ignoring her completely. The weak-willed, next in their dreams might throw themselves in the river or swallow opium; the strong-willed grab a kitchen knife and run straight to the sailor. Though their lives are estranged from regular society, their tears and joys, in the midst of strong love and hate, are woven deep into their lives, making them just like young people elsewhere—body and soul saturated by their passions, feeling heat in winter, forgetting everything else. As for the differences, it’s just that these people are more sincere, and perhaps a bit more muddled. Short-term contracts, long-term marriages, one-off transactions behind closed doors—all these exchanges concerning a woman’s body are, because of the honest customs here, not considered shameful or low by those involved, nor do outsiders judge or despise them with the traditional attitudes of the educated. Here, where honor trumps profit and promises are kept, even prostitutes can be more trustworthy than the so-called moral or self-righteous city folk.

The head of the river port is Shunshun, a man who’s been around since the old imperial days, having once served in the army. During the revolution, he was a squad leader in the famous 49th Infantry Regiment. Others became heroes or were executed after the revolution, but he, because he had gout in his legs from youthful mischief, came back home, used his meager savings to buy a six-oared boat made of white wood, which he rented to a poor boat owner to carry goods between Chadong and Chenzhou. Fortune was on his side; the boat had no mishaps for half a year, and from the money earned, he then married a pale-skinned, black-haired young widow with some property. A few years later, he had four boats of different sizes running the river, a shop, and two sons.

But, open-handed and easygoing as he was, his business never boomed like the oil merchants’ because he loved to make friends and was generous to those in need. Having lived rough himself, he understood travelers’ hardships and the feelings of the unlucky, so whatever down-on-their-luck boat owners, discharged soldiers passing through, itinerant scholars or writers came to him for help, he gave whatever assistance he could. Money earned on the river was given away just as readily. Though he had bad legs, he could still swim; walking was difficult, but as a person, he was upright and fair. On the water, everything was simple and governed by habit: if someone’s boat collided, or if someone’s interests were infringed upon, there were customary ways to resolve it. But to apply these rules and settle matters requires a senior, respected figure. One autumn, the previous man in charge died, and Shunshun took over. At the time, he was only fifty, but was sensible, fair, straightforward, and disinterested in money, so no one questioned his age.

Nowadays, his eldest son is eighteen, the younger sixteen. Both young men are as sturdy as little bulls: they can steer boats, swim, and walk long distances. Of the things young men from small towns can do, they can do them all, and do them well. The older one is, like his father, bold and open-minded, unconcerned with petty formalities. The younger takes after their pale-skinned, black-haired mother—quiet, not given to talking, but with striking brows and features.At a glance, one could tell that he was intelligent and full of emotion.

Now that the two brothers had grown up, as their father, it was necessary to train them for all aspects of life, so he would take turns sending the two children out to travel. When going downstream by boat, they would often work as crew on his own boat, sharing the hardships and joys with the others. When rowing, they would choose the heaviest oar, and when hauling the towrope, they would take the lead rope and the second rope. What they ate was dried fish, chili peppers, and pickled vegetables; what they slept on was the hard, unyielding floorboards of the cabin. When going upstream by land, they would follow merchants from East Sichuan, traveling to Xiushan, Longtan, and Youyang to do business, braving cold, heat, rain, and snow, always wearing straw sandals and hurrying from one post to the next. They also carried short knives, so that in case of necessity, they could swiftly draw their knives, step into an open space, and settle things hand-to-hand with the person opposite. The custom of their group was “to deal with enemies, one must use knives; to bond with friends, one must also use knives,” so whenever there was a need for knives, they never missed the slightest opportunity. They learned trade, social skills, how to make a living in a new place, and also how to use a knife to protect both their bodies and their reputations. The purpose of this education seemed to be to teach the two children courage and loyalty. As a result, the two of them became as sturdy as tigers, yet still gentle and friendly, neither lazy nor frivolous, nor relying on power to bully others. Therefore, whenever the three of them were mentioned on the border of Chadong, everyone would show a certain respect for that family name.

As a father, when the two sons were very young, he knew that everything about the eldest son was like himself, but he would dote a little more on the second son. Because of this unwitting partiality, he named the eldest son Tianbao and the second son Nuosong. The meaning was that, while those blessed by Heaven might still encounter some setbacks in life, those sent by the Nuo deity, according to local custom, could not be taken lightly by others. Nuosong was very handsome, but people of Chadong’s boatmen’s families were not skilled at praising such beauty; they simply gave him the nickname “Yue Yun.” Although nobody had actually seen Yue Yun in person, the general impression came from the character Yue Yun in traditional opera, giving him a similar air. Three

At the junction of two provinces, for more than ten years local military affairs had focused on stability and order and had been handled appropriately, with no incidents occurring. Waterborne and land commerce had not been interrupted by war nor affected by banditry. Everything was in perfect order, and people lived content and law-abiding lives. Other than misfortunes such as losing cattle, capsizing boats, or other fatal accidents in their own families, people felt deep sorrow over such tragedies, but the kinds of suffering and struggles common in other parts of China never seemed to trouble the people of this border town.

The liveliest days of the year in Chadong were Dragon Boat Festival, Mid-Autumn Festival, and Chinese New Year. For three to five decades, the excitement these three festivals brought to the locals had not really changed, and they remained the most meaningful days of the year for the residents there.

On Dragon Boat Festival day, women and children all wore new clothes, with the character "king" painted on their foreheads with realgar dipped in wine. Every household would have fish and meat on this day. Around eleven in the morning, all of Chadong would have already finished lunch. After the meal, those who lived in town would lock their doors and the whole family would go out to the riverside to watch the boat races. If one had friends on River Street, they could watch from the balcony of a stilthouse; if not, they would stand at the customs gate or on the docks to watch. The dragon boats took a certain spot at the upper part of the long pool as their starting point, with the customs post as the finish line for the races. On this day, all the military officers, customs officials, and local dignitaries would gather in front of the customs house to join the festivities. The boatmen had prepared well in the days prior, dividing into groups and selecting some of the strongest and most agile young men to practice maneuvers in the pool. The form of the boats was quite different from the usual wooden boats: they were all long and narrow, with both ends raised high, and the hull painted with long vermilion lines. Normally, they were kept dry in riverside caves, dragged to the water only when needed. Each boat could seat twelve to eighteen rowers, a lead, a drummer, and a gong player. Each rower held a short oar, paddling in time with the slow or fast beat of the drum. Seated at the bow, the leader would wrap their head in a red cloth and wave two small flags to direct the boat. The drummer and gong player usually sat midship; once the boat started, the simple rhythms of the drum and gong would resound, keeping the rowers in sync. Since the boat’s speed depended on the drumbeats, whenever two boats were fiercely competing, the drum would thunder, and with the cheering from both banks, people would be reminded of Lady Liang Hongyu beating the drums during the water battle at the old Stork River, or Niu Gao capturing Yang Yao at water’s edge with battle drums. Any boat that pulled ahead would be rewarded in front of the customs house with a length of red cloth and a small silver plaque, sometimes tied to a crew member’s head, showing off the boat’s collective honor. Soldiers fond of a good time would also set off five hundred-firecracker strings at the water’s edge each time a boat won, in celebration.

After the boat races, the garrison commander in the city, to share the joy with civilians and add to the festive atmosphere, would release thirty green-headed, long-necked drakes into the river, each with a strip of red cloth tied around their necks. That night, sure enough, it rained heavily, accompanied by frightening thunder. When the lightning flashed across the ridge of the roof, there was immediately a deafening clap of thunder.
