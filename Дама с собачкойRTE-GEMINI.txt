Anton Pavlovich Chekhov

The Lady with the Dog

     

I

     

     It was said that a new face had appeared on the embankment: a lady with a dog. Dmitry Dmitrich Gurov, who had already spent two weeks in Yalta and had grown accustomed to the place, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting at Verne's pavilion, he saw a young lady, a fair-haired woman of medium height wearing a beret, walking along the embankment: a white pomeranian was running after her.

     And then he encountered her in the public garden and in the square several times a day. She walked alone, always in the same beret, with the white pomeranian; no one knew who she was, and they called her simply: the lady with the dog.

     "If she is here without a husband and without acquaintances," Gurov reflected, "it would not be superfluous to make her acquaintance."

     He was not yet forty, but he already had a daughter of twelve and two sons in high school. He had been married off early, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed half as old again as he. She was a tall woman, with dark eyebrows, erect, grave, dignified and, as she called herself, a thinking person. She read a great deal, omitted the "hard sign" in her letters, called her husband not Dmitry but Dimitry, while he secretly considered her shallow, narrow-minded, unelegant, was afraid of her and disliked being at home. He had begun to betray her long ago, betrayed her often and, probably for that reason, spoke almost always ill of women, and when they were discussed in his presence, he called them:

     — The lower race!

     It seemed to him that he had been sufficiently taught by bitter experience to call them whatever he liked, but still, he could not have lived even two days without the "lower race." In the company of men he was bored, not himself, with them he was untalkative and cold, but when he was among women, he felt free and knew what to talk about with them and how to behave; even being silent with them was easy for him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature there was something attractive, elusive, which disposed women toward him, lured them; he knew about this, and he himself was also drawn to them by some force.

     Repeated experience, indeed bitter experience, had long ago taught him that every intimacy, which at first so pleasantly diversifies life and appears as a charming and light adventure, inevitably grows, among decent people, especially Muscovites, who are slow to move and irresolute, into a whole task, extremely complex, and the situation in the end becomes burdensome. But at every new meeting with an interesting woman, this experience somehow slipped from his memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

     And so one day toward evening he was dining in the garden, and the lady in the beret was approaching slowly to take the neighboring table. Her expression, gait, dress, and hairstyle told him that she was from decent society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here… There is much untruth in stories about the laxity of local morals, he despised them and knew that such stories are for the most part composed by people who would themselves gladly sin if they knew how, but when the lady sat down at the neighboring table three paces from him, he remembered these stories of easy conquests, of trips to the mountains, and the seductive thought of a quick, fleeting liaison, of a romance with an unknown woman, whose first and last name you do not know, suddenly took possession of him.

     He beckoned the pomeranian to him kindly and, when it approached, shook his finger at it. The pomeranian growled. Gurov shook his finger again.

     The lady looked at him and immediately lowered her eyes.

     — He does not bite, — she said and blushed.

     — May I give him a bone? — And when she nodded affirmatively, he asked amiably: — Have you been in Yalta long?

     — About five days.

     — And I am already dragging through my second week here.

     They were silent for a while.

     — Time goes by quickly, and yet it is so boring here! — she said, not looking at him.

     — It is only a matter of custom to say that it is boring here. A provincial lives in his home somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra — and he is not bored, but he comes here: "Oh, it's boring! Oh, the dust!" One would think he came from Granada.

     She laughed.Then both continued to eat in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked side by side — and a playful, light conversation began, the kind held by people who are free and satisfied, to whom it is all the same where they go or what they talk about. They walked and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was a lilac color, so soft and warm, and a golden stripe from the moon ran across it. They talked about how stifling it was after the hot day. Gurov said that he was a Muscovite, a philologist by education, but served in a bank; he had once prepared to sing in a private opera, but gave it up, and owned two houses in Moscow... And from her he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg, but got married in S., where she had been living for two years, that she would stay in Yalta for another month or so, and perhaps her husband, who also wanted a rest, would come for her. She could not at all explain where her husband served — whether in the provincial government or in the provincial Zemstvo board, and this was funny to her herself. And Gurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

     Then in his room he thought about her, about how tomorrow she would surely meet him. It must be so. Going to bed, he remembered that she had so recently been a schoolgirl, studying, just like his daughter now, he remembered how much shyness and awkwardness there still was in her laughter, in her conversation with a stranger — it must be the first time in her life she was alone, in such a setting, where she is followed, and looked at, and spoken to with only one secret purpose, which she cannot help but guess. He remembered her slender, weak neck, her beautiful, grey eyes.

     "There is something pathetic about her nonetheless," he thought and began to fall asleep.

     

II

     

     A week had passed since their acquaintance. It was a holiday. Inside the rooms it was stifling, and in the streets the dust whirled around, tearing off hats. All day long one felt thirsty, and Gurov went often to the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeyevna now water with syrup, now ice cream. There was nowhere to go.

     In the evening, when it calmed down a little, they went to the pier to watch the steamer come in. On the pier there were many people strolling; they had gathered to meet someone, holding bouquets. And here two peculiarities of the elegant Yalta crowd stood out clearly: the elderly ladies were dressed like young ones, and there were many generals.

     Due to the rough sea the steamer arrived late, after the sun had already set, and, before docking at the pier, turned around for a long time. Anna Sergeyevna looked through her lorgnette at the steamer and at the passengers, as if searching for acquaintances, and when she turned to Gurov, her eyes shone. She talked a lot, and her questions were abrupt, and she herself immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

     The elegant crowd was dispersing, faces were no longer visible, the wind had died down completely, but Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood as if waiting to see if anyone else would get off the steamer. Anna Sergeyevna was already silent and smelling the flowers, not looking at Gurov.

     — The weather has improved toward evening, — he said. — Where shall we go now? Shall we drive somewhere?

     She did not answer anything.

     Then he looked intently at her and suddenly embraced her and kissed her on the lips, and the smell and moisture of the flowers enveloped him, and immediately he looked around fearfully: had anyone seen?

     — Let's go to your place... — he said softly.

     And both walked quickly.

     In her room it was stifling, smelling of the perfume she had bought in a Japanese shop. Gurov, looking at her now, thought: "What encounters there are in life!" From the past he retained a memory of carefree, good-natured women, cheerful from love, grateful to him for happiness, however short; and of those — such as, for example, his wife — who loved without sincerity, with excessive talk, affectedly, with hysteria, with an expression as if it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of those two or three, very beautiful, cold ones, whose faces suddenly flashed a predatory expression, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it can give, and these were women no longer in their first youth, capricious, unreasoning, domineering, not intelligent, and when Gurov cooled toward them,then their beauty excited hatred in him and the lace on their underwear then seemed to him like scales.

But here there was still the same timidity, the awkwardness of inexperienced youth, an uneasy feeling, and there was an impression of bewilderment, as if someone had suddenly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the dog", took what had happened in a peculiar way, very seriously, as if it were her fall, so it seemed, and it was strange and inappropriate. Her features drooped and faded, and her long hair hung mournfully on both sides of her face, she sat lost in thought in a dejected pose, like a sinner in an old painting.

- It is not good, - she said. - You are the first not to respect me now.

On the table in the room was a watermelon. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat without haste. At least half an hour passed in silence.

Anna Sergeyevna was touching, she radiated the purity of a decent, naive woman who had lived little, the solitary candle burning on the table hardly lit her face, but it was clear that she was heavy at heart.

- Why would I stop respecting you? - asked Gurov. - You do not know what you are saying yourself.

- May God forgive me! - she said, and her eyes filled with tears. - This is terrible.

- You seem to be making excuses.

- How can I excuse myself? I am a bad, low woman, I despise myself and do not think of an excuse. I did not deceive my husband, but myself. And not just now, but I have been deceiving for a long time. My husband, perhaps, is an honest, good man, but he is a flunkey! I do not know what he does there, how he serves, I only know that he is a flunkey. When I married him, I was twenty years old, I was tormented by curiosity, I wanted something better, for there must be, - I said to myself, - another life. I wanted to live! To live and live... Curiosity burned me... you do not understand this, but, I swear to God, I could no longer control myself, something was happening to me, I could not be held back, I told my husband I was ill and came here... And here I walked around as if in a daze, like a madwoman... and now I have become a vulgar, worthless woman whom anyone can despise.

Gurov was already bored with listening, he was irritated by the naive tone, this repentance, so unexpected and misplaced, if it were not for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a role.

- I do not understand, - he said quietly, - what is it you want?

She hid her face on his chest and pressed against him.

- Believe, believe me, I implore you... - she said. - I love an honest, pure life, and sin is loathsome to me, I do not know what I am doing myself. Simple people say: the evil one tempted me. And I can say about myself now that the evil one has tempted me.

- There, there... - he muttered.

He looked into her fixed, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke quietly and affectionately, and she gradually calmed down, and cheerfulness returned to her, both began to laugh.

Later, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment, the town with its cypresses looked quite dead, but the sea was still roaring and beating against the shore, a single boat rocked on the waves, and a small lantern flickered sleepily on it.

They found a carriage and drove to Oreanda.

- I just found out your surname downstairs in the hall: it is written von Diderits on the board, - said Gurov. - Is your husband German?

- No, I think his grandfather was German, but he himself is Orthodox.

In Oreanda they sat on a bench, not far from the church, looked down at the sea and remained silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist, white clouds stood motionless on the mountain peaks. The foliage did not stir on the trees, the cicadas chirped, and the monotonous, hollow roar of the sea, coming from below, spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. It roared like that below when neither Yalta nor Oreanda were here, it roars now and will roar just as indifferently and hollowly when we are gone. And in this constancy, in total indifference to the life and death of each of us, there lies hidden, perhaps, the pledge of our eternal salvation, the continuous movement of life on earth, continuous perfection. Sitting beside a young woman, who at dawn seemed so beautiful,soothed and enchanted by this fairy-tale setting — the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the wide sky, Gurov thought about how, in essence, if one thinks about it, everything is beautiful in this world, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget about the higher goals of existence, about our human dignity.

     A man approached — probably a watchman, — looked at them and left. And this detail seemed so mysterious and also beautiful. One could see a steamer arriving from Feodosia, lit by the morning dawn, already without lights.

     — Dew on the grass, — said Anna Sergeyevna after a silence.

     — Yes. Time to go home.

     They returned to the city.

     Then every noon they met on the embankment, had breakfast together, dined, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept poorly and that her heart beat anxiously, asking the same questions over and over, troubled now by jealousy, now by the fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or in the garden, when no one was near them, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with a backward glance and fear that someone might see, the heat, the smell of the sea and the constant flickering before his eyes of idle, dressed-up, well-fed people seemed to have transformed him; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how seductive, he was impatiently passionate, never leaving her side, while she often grew pensive and kept asking him to admit that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, but only saw in her a common woman. Almost every evening later on, they drove somewhere out of town, to Oreanda or to the waterfall; and the outing was a success, the impressions were invariably beautiful and majestic every time.

     They expected the husband to arrive. But a letter came from him, in which he informed her that his eyes had begun to ache, and he implored his wife to return home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeyevna hurried.

     — It is good that I am leaving, — she said to Gurov. — It is fate itself.

     She left by horse carriage, and he saw her off. They traveled the whole day. When she sat in the carriage of the express train and when the second bell struck, she said:

     — Let me look at you once more... Look at you once again. Just like that.

     She did not cry, but was sad, as if ill, and her face trembled.

     — I will think of you... remember you, — she said. — God be with you, stay here. Do not remember me unkindly. We are parting forever, it must be so, because we should not have met at all. Well, God be with you.

     The train left quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and a minute later the noise was no longer heard, as if everything had conspired on purpose to end this sweet oblivion, this madness, as soon as possible. And, left alone on the platform and looking into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the chirp of the crickets and the hum of the telegraph wires with a feeling as if he had just woken up. And he thought about how there had been one more adventure or occurrence in his life, and it too was already over, and now only a memory remained... He was moved, sad, and felt a slight remorse; after all, this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he was friendly and warm with her, but still, in his treatment of her, in his tone and caresses, there glimmered a shadow of slight mockery, the somewhat coarse arrogance of a happy man who was, moreover, almost twice her age. All the time she called him kind, extraordinary, noble; evidently, he seemed to her not what he really was, which meant he had involuntarily deceived her...

     Here at the station it already smelled of autumn, the evening was cool.

     "It is time for me to go north, too," thought Gurov, leaving the platform. "It is time!"

III

     At home in Moscow everything was already like winter, the stoves were lit, and in the mornings, when the children were getting ready for the gymnasium and drinking tea, it was dark, and the nanny lit the lamp for a short time. The frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sleigh riding, it is pleasant to see the white earth, the white roofs, one breathes softly, wonderfully, and at this time one remembers youthful years. The old lindens and birches, white with frost, have a good-natured expression, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms,and near them one no longer wants to think about the mountains and the sea.

     Gurov was a Moscovite, he returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and walked along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the ringing of bells, then the recent trip and the places he had been to lost all charm for him. Little by little he sank into Moscow life, already greedily reading through three newspapers a day and saying that he did not read Moscow newspapers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties, anniversaries, and it was already flattering to him that famous lawyers and artists visited him and that at the doctors' club he played cards with a professor. Already he could eat a whole portion of selyanka on a frying pan...

     A month or so would pass, and Anna Sergeyevna, it seemed to him, would be covered in the mist of memory and only occasionally would be dreamed of with a touching smile, as others had been dreamed of. But more than a month passed, deep winter set in, and in his memory everything was clear, as if he had parted with Anna Sergeyevna only yesterday. And the memories flared up ever stronger. Whether the voices of children preparing their lessons reached his study in the evening silence, whether he heard a romance or an organ in a restaurant, or the blizzard howled in the fireplace, suddenly everything was resurrected in his memory: what happened on the pier, and the early morning with the mist on the mountains, and the steamboat from Feodosia, and the kisses. He walked around the room for a long time and remembered, and smiled, and then the memories turned into dreams, and the past in his imagination mixed with what would be. Anna Sergeyevna did not appear to him in dreams, but followed him everywhere like a shadow and watched him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if she were alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she was; and he himself seemed better to himself than he was then, in Yalta. In the evenings she looked at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the gentle rustle of her clothes. On the street he followed women with his eyes, looking to see if there was anyone like her...

     And already a strong desire to share his memories with someone tormented him. But at home it was impossible to talk about his love, and outside the home there was no one. Certainly not with the lodgers or at the bank. And what was there to talk about? Had he really loved then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relations with Anna Sergeyevna? And he had to talk vaguely about love, about women, and no one guessed what was the matter, and only his wife moved her dark eyebrows and said:

     — The role of a fop does not suit you at all, Dimitri.

     One night, leaving the doctors' club with his partner, an official, he could not restrain himself and said:

     — If you only knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!

     The official got into the sleigh and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

     — Dmitry Dmitrich!

     — What?

     — You were right earlier: the sturgeon was a bit tainted!

These words, so ordinary, for some reason suddenly revolted Gurov, appearing to him as humiliating, unclean. What savage customs, what faces! What senseless nights, what uninteresting, unremarkable days! Frantic card games, gluttony, drunkenness, constant talk all about the same thing. Useless matters and talk all about the same thing take up the best part of one's time, one's best strength, and in the end there remains a kind of stunted, wingless life, some kind of nonsense, and it is impossible to leave or escape, exactly as if sitting in a madhouse or in a penal battalion!

Gurov did not sleep all night and was indignant, and then spent the whole day with a headache. And on the following nights he slept badly, constantly sitting up in bed and thinking or walking from corner to corner. He was fed up with the children, fed up with the bank, he did not want to go anywhere, or talk about anything.

In December during the holidays he prepared for a journey and told his wife that he was leaving for Petersburg to look after a young man's affairs, - and left for S. Why? He did not know well himself. He wanted to see Anna Sergeyevna and talk, to arrange a meeting, if possible.

He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the entire floor was covered with gray military cloth and there was an inkwell on the table, gray with dust, featuring a rider on a horse whose hand was raised with a hat, while the head was broken off. The porter gave him the necessary information: von Diderits lives on Staro-Goncharnaya Street, in his own house - it is not far from the hotel, he lives well, richly, has his own horses, everyone in town knows him. The porter pronounced it like this: Drydyrits.

Gurov went unhurriedly to Staro-Goncharnaya and found the house. Directly opposite the house stretched a fence, gray, long, with nails.

"One would run away from such a fence," thought Gurov, glancing now at the windows, now at the fence.

He considered: today is a non-working day, and the husband is probably at home. And anyway, it would be tactless to enter the house and cause embarrassment. If he sent a note, it might, perhaps, fall into the husband's hands, and then everything could be ruined. It is best to rely on chance. And he kept walking along the street and near the fence waiting for this chance. He saw a beggar enter the gate and get attacked by dogs, then, an hour later, he heard the playing of a piano, and the sounds reached him faint, unclear. It must be Anna Sergeyevna playing. The front door suddenly opened, and an old woman came out of it, and behind her ran a familiar white spitz. Gurov wanted to call the dog, but his heart suddenly started pounding, and from excitement he could not remember the name of the spitz.

He walked and hated the gray fence more and more, and already thought with irritation that Anna Sergeyevna had forgotten about him and, perhaps, was already amusing herself with another, and this was so natural in the position of a young woman who was forced to see this cursed fence from morning till night. He returned to his room and sat on the sofa for a long time, not knowing what to do, then had dinner, then slept for a long time.

"How stupid and restless all this is," he thought, waking up and looking at the dark windows; it was already evening. "So I have slept my fill for some reason. What am I going to do now at night?"

He sat on the bed, covered with a cheap gray, almost hospital-like blanket, and teased himself with vexation:

"So much for the lady with the dog... So much for an adventure... So sit here."

Earlier in the morning, at the station, a poster with very large letters had caught his eye: "The Geisha" was performing for the first time. He remembered this and went to the theater.

"It is very possible that she attends premieres," he thought.

The theater was full. And here, as generally in all provincial theaters, there was a haze above the chandelier, the gallery was noisily restless; in the first row before the start of the performance stood local dandies with their hands behind their backs; and here, in the governor's box, in the first seat sat the governor's daughter in a boa, while the governor himself hidden modestly behind the curtain, and only his hands were visible; the curtain swayed, the orchestra tuned for a long time. All the while the audience was entering and taking their seats, Gurov greedily searched with his eyes.

Anna Sergeyevna entered too. She sat in the third row, and when Gurov looked at her, his heart contracted, and he understood clearly,that for him there was now no person in the whole world closer, dearer, or more important; she, lost in a provincial crowd, this little woman, in no way remarkable, with a vulgar lorgnette in her hands, now filled his entire life, was his grief, his joy, the only happiness he now desired for himself; and to the sound of a bad orchestra, of wretched provincial violins, he thought of how beautiful she was. He thought and dreamed.

Together with Anna Sergeyevna, a young man with small sideburns, very tall and stooped, entered and sat down beside her; with every step he swayed his head and seemed to be constantly bowing. This was probably the husband whom she, in a fit of bitter feeling back in Yalta, had called a lackey. And indeed, in his long figure, in his sideburns, in his small bald spot, there was something lackeyishly modest; he smiled blandly, and in his buttonhole there sparkled some kind of academic badge, just like a lackey's number.

In the first intermission the husband went out to smoke, and she remained in her seat. Gurov, who was also sitting in the stalls, approached her and said in a trembling voice, forcing a smile:

— Hello.

She looked at him and turned pale, then looked again with horror, not believing her eyes, and tightly gripped both her fan and lorgnette in her hands, obviously struggling with herself to keep from fainting. Both were silent. She sat, he stood, frightened by her embarrassment, not daring to sit down beside her. The violins and a flute began to tune up, and suddenly it became terrifying; it seemed as if people were looking from all the boxes. But then she got up and walked quickly toward the exit; he followed her, and both walked aimlessly through corridors, up and down stairs, and before their eyes flashed men in judicial, teaching, and administrative uniforms, all with badges; ladies flashed by, fur coats on hangers, a draft blew, bringing the smell of tobacco butts. And Gurov, whose heart was beating violently, thought:

"Oh Lord! Why these people, this orchestra..."

And at that moment he suddenly remembered how that evening at the station, after seeing Anna Sergeyevna off, he had told himself that everything was over and they would never see each other again. But how far it still was from the end!

On a narrow, dark staircase marked "Entrance to the amphitheater," she stopped.

— How you frightened me! — she said, breathing heavily, still pale and stunned. — Oh, how you frightened me! I am barely alive. Why did you come? Why?

— But understand, Anna, understand... — he said in a low voice, hurrying. — I implore you, understand...

She looked at him with fear, with entreaty, with love, looking intently to fix his features more firmly in her memory.

— I suffer so much! — she continued, not listening to him. — I have thought only of you all the time, I lived on thoughts of you. And I wanted to forget, to forget, but why, why did you come?

Higher up, on the landing, two schoolboys were smoking and looking down, but Gurov did not care; he drew Anna Sergeyevna to him and began kissing her face, cheeks, and hands.

— What are you doing, what are you doing! — she said in horror, pushing him away. — We have both gone mad. Leave today, leave now... I implore you by everything holy, I beg you... Someone is coming!

Someone was coming up the stairs.

— You must leave... — Anna Sergeyevna continued in a whisper. — Do you hear, Dmitri Dmitritch? I will come to you in Moscow. I have never been happy, I am unhappy now and never, never shall I be happy, never! Do not make me suffer even more! I swear, I will come to Moscow. But now let us part! My dear, good, precious one, let us part!

She pressed his hand and began to go down quickly, constantly looking back at him, and it was clear from her eyes that she really was not happy. Gurov stood for a while, listened, then, when all was quiet, found his coat rack and left the theater.

IV

And Anna Sergeyevna began coming to him in Moscow. Once every 2-3 months she left S. and told her husband that she was going to consult a professor about a female complaint — and her husband believed and did not believe. Having arrived in Moscow,she stayed at the Slavyansky Bazar and immediately sent a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov went to see her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

     One day he was going to her in this way on a winter morning (the messenger had been to see him the evening before and had not found him). His daughter was with him, whom he wanted to see to school, as it was on the way. Heavy wet snow was falling.

     — It is now 3 degrees above zero, and yet it is snowing, — Gurov said to his daughter. — But this warmth is only on the surface of the earth, in the upper layers of the atmosphere the temperature is quite different.

     — Papa, why is there no thunder in winter?

     He explained this too. He spoke and thought about how he was going to a meeting and not a single living soul knew about it and, probably, never would know. He had two lives: one open, seen and known by all who needed to, full of conventional truth and conventional deception, exactly like the lives of his acquaintances and friends, and another — flowing in secret. And by some strange confluence of circumstances, perhaps accidental, everything that was important, interesting, and necessary for him, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, which constituted the core of his life, took place in secret from others, while everything that was his lie, his shell, in which he hid to conceal the truth, such as, for example, his work at the bank, arguments at the club, his "lower race", going to anniversaries with his wife, — all this was open. And he judged others by himself, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that every person, under the cover of secrecy, as under the cover of night, leads their real, most interesting life. Every personal existence is based on secrecy, and, perhaps, partly for this reason, a cultured person is so nervously concerned that personal secrecy should be respected.

     After seeing his daughter to school, Gurov went to the Slavyansky Bazar. He took off his fur coat downstairs, went upstairs and knocked quietly on the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, exhausted by the journey and the waiting, had been expecting him since yesterday evening; she was pale, looked at him and did not smile, and as soon as he entered, she already fell upon his breast. Just as if they had not seen each other for 2 years, their kiss was long, lingering.

     — Well, how are you living there? — he asked. — What is new?

     — Wait, I will tell you in a moment... I cannot.

     She could not speak, because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed a handkerchief to her eyes.

     "Well, let her cry, and I will sit for a while," — he thought and sat in an armchair.

     Then he rang and asked for tea to be brought to him; and then, while he drank tea, she was still standing, turned towards the window... She cried from agitation, from the sorrowful awareness that their life had turned out so sadly; they see each other only in secret, hiding from people like thieves! Is their life not broken?

     — Come now, stop! — he said.

     For him it was obvious that this love of theirs would not end soon, no one knew when. Anna Sergeyevna was becoming more and more attached to him, adored him, and it would be unthinkable to tell her that all this must have an end someday; indeed she would not have believed it.

     He went up to her and took her by the shoulders to caress her, to joke, and at that moment he saw himself in the mirror.

     His head was already beginning to turn gray. And it seemed strange to him that he had aged so much in recent years, had become so unattractive. The shoulders on which his hands rested were warm and shivering. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but, probably, already close to beginning to fade and wither, like his own life. Why does she love him so? He always seemed to women not what he was, and they loved in him not himself, but a person whom their imagination created and whom they greedily sought in their life; and then, when they noticed their mistake, they still loved him. And not one of them was happy with him. Time passed, he made acquaintances, came together, parted, but not once had he loved; there was everything else, but only not love.

     And only now, when his head had become gray, he fell in love, as one should, for real — for the first time in his life.

     Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other,like very close, dear people, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had destined them for one another, and it was incomprehensible why he was married and she was married; and it was as if they were two migratory birds, a male and a female, who had been caught and forced to live in separate cages. They forgave each other for what they were ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love of theirs had changed them both.

     Before, in sad moments, he would calm himself with all sorts of arguments that came into his head, but now he had no time for arguments, he felt deep compassion, he wanted to be sincere, tender…

     — Stop, my dear, — he would say. — You have had your cry — and that is enough… Now let us talk, let us think of something.

     Then they consulted for a long time, talked about how to rid themselves of the need to hide, to lie, to live in different cities, not to see each other for long periods. How to free themselves from these unbearable bonds?

     — How? How? — he asked, clutching his head. — How?

     And it seemed that a little more — and the solution would be found, and then a new, beautiful life would begin; and it was clear to both of them that the end was still far, far away, and that the most complicated and difficult part was only just beginning.

     

     

Black monk

     

I

     

     Andrey Vasilyich Kovrin, a master of arts, had become exhausted and unstrung his nerves. He did not undergo treatment, but somehow in passing, over a bottle of wine, he spoke with a doctor friend, and the latter advised him to spend the spring and summer in the country. Conveniently, a long letter arrived from Tanya Pesotskaya, who asked him to come to Borisovka and stay as a guest. And he decided that he really did need a change of scene.

     At first – it was in April – he went to his own place, to his ancestral Kovrinka, and lived there in solitude for three weeks; then, having waited for the roads to improve, he set out by horse to his former guardian and educator Pesotsky, a famous Russian horticulturist. From Kovrinka to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, it was considered no more than seventy versts, and traveling along the soft spring road in a comfortable sprung carriage was a true pleasure.

     Pesotsky's house was enormous, with columns, with lions from which the plaster had peeled, and with a footman in a tailcoat at the entrance. The old park, gloomy and austere, laid out in the English manner, stretched for nearly a whole verst from the house to the river and ended there with a steep, sheer clay bank, on which grew pines with exposed roots that looked like shaggy paws; below, the water gleamed unsociably, sandpipers flitted about with plaintive squeaks, and there was always such a mood there that one might as well sit down and write a ballad. On the other hand, near the house itself, in the courtyard and in the fruit orchard, which together with the nurseries occupied some thirty desyatinas, it was cheerful and joyous even in bad weather. Such amazing roses, lilies, camellias, such tulips of all possible colors, starting from bright white and ending with soot-black, in general such a wealth of flowers as at Pesotsky's, Kovrin had never happened to see anywhere else. Spring was only at its beginning, and the true luxury of the flowerbeds was still hidden in the greenhouses, but even what bloomed along the alleys and here and there in the beds was enough to make one feel, walking through the garden, as if in a kingdom of tender colors, especially in the early hours when dew sparkled on every petal.

     What constituted the decorative part of the garden and what Pesotsky himself contemptuously called trifles had once in childhood produced a fairy-tale impression on Kovrin. What whims, refined deformities, and mockeries of nature were there! There were espaliers of fruit trees, a pear tree that had the shape of a pyramidal poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an umbrella made of an apple tree, arches, monograms, candelabra, and even 1862 made of plum trees – a figure denoting the year when Pesotsky first took up gardening. One also came across beautiful, slender little trees with trunks straight and strong like those of palms, and only by looking closely could one recognize in these little trees gooseberries or currants. But what gladdened the garden most of all and gave it an animated look,so it was constant movement. From early morning until evening, around the trees, bushes, on the alleys and flowerbeds, like ants, people bustled with wheelbarrows, hoes, and watering cans...

     Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys' in the evening, around 10 o'clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semenych, in great anxiety. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer predicted a frost by morning, and meanwhile, the gardener Ivan Karlych had gone to town and there was no one to rely on. During supper, they spoke only of the morning frost, and it was decided that Tanya would not go to bed and at 1 o'clock would walk through the garden to see if everything was in order, while Yegor Semenych would get up at 3 o'clock or even earlier.

     Kovrin sat with Tanya all evening and after midnight went with her into the garden. It was cold. In the yard, there was already a strong smell of burning. In the large fruit orchard, which was called the commercial orchard and brought Yegor Semenych several thousand in net income annually, a thick, black, acrid smoke crawled along the ground and, enveloping the trees, saved those thousands from the frost. The trees here stood in a checkerboard pattern, their rows were straight and regular, exactly like ranks of soldiers, and this strict, pedantic regularity, and the fact that all the trees were the same height and had absolutely identical crowns and trunks, made the scene monotonous and even boring. Kovrin and Tanya walked along the rows where fires of manure, straw, and all sorts of refuse smoldered, and occasionally they encountered workers who wandered through the smoke like shadows. Only the cherries, plums, and certain varieties of apple trees were in bloom, but the entire orchard was drowned in smoke, and only near the nurseries could Kovrin breathe deeply.

     – Even in my childhood, I sneezed here from the smoke, – he said, shrugging his shoulders, – but to this day I do not understand how smoke can save from frost.

     – Smoke replaces the clouds when there are none... – Tanya replied.

     – And why are clouds needed?

     – In overcast and cloudy weather, there are no morning frosts.

     – I see!

     He laughed and took her by the hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with thin black eyebrows, the turned-up collar of her coat that prevented her from moving her head freely, and all of her, slender, graceful, with her dress tucked up from the dew, moved him.

     – Heavens, she is already grown up! – he said. – When I left here for the last time, 5 years ago, you were still a mere child. You were so thin, long-legged, with your hair down, wearing a short dress, and I teased you by calling you a heron... What time does!

     – Yes, 5 years! – Tanya sighed. – Much water has flowed since then. Tell me, Andryusha, in all conscience, – she spoke animatedly, looking into his face, – have you grown unaccustomed to us? But then, why am I asking? You are a man, you already live your own interesting life, you are a figure of importance... Estrangement is so natural! Но no matter what, Andryusha, I want you to consider us your own. We have a right to that.

     – I do, Tanya.

     – Word of honor?

     – Yes, word of honor.

     – You were surprised today that we have so many photographs of you. After all, you know my father adores you. Sometimes it seems to me that he loves you more than me. He is proud of you. You are a scholar, an extraordinary person, you have made a brilliant career for yourself, and he is certain that you turned out this way because he raised you. I do not stop him from thinking so. Let it be.

     Dawn was already beginning, and this was especially noticeable by the clarity with which the billows of smoke and the crowns of the trees began to stand out in the air. Nightingales sang, and from the fields came the cry of quails.

"However, it is time to sleep," said Tanya. "And it is cold, too." She took him by the arm. "Thank you, Andryusha, for coming. Our acquaintances are uninteresting, and there are few of them anyway. We have only the garden, garden, garden, and nothing else. Standard, half-standard," she laughed, "Aport, Rennet, Borovinka, budding, grafting... All, all of our life has gone into the garden, I don't even ever dream of anything except apple and pear trees. Of course, it is good, useful, but sometimes one wants something else for a change. I remember when you used to come to us for the holidays or just like that, the house became somehow fresher and brighter, as if the covers were taken off the chandeliers and furniture. I was a girl then, and yet I understood."

     She spoke for a long time and with great feeling. For some reason, it suddenly occurred to him that during the summer he might become attached to this small, weak, talkative creature, get carried away and fall in love, in the situation of both of them, it was so possible and natural! This thought moved and amused him; he leaned toward the dear, preoccupied face and sang softly:

     Onegin, I will not conceal,

     Madly I love Tatyana...

     When they came home, Yegor Semyonych had already gotten up. Kovrin did not feel like sleeping, he started talking with the old man and returned with him to the garden. Yegor Semyonych was tall, broad-shouldered, with a large belly and suffered from shortness of breath, but he always walked so fast that it was difficult to keep up with him. He had an extremely preoccupied look, always hurrying somewhere with such an expression as if, should he be late by even one minute, then everything would be lost!

     "Here is a story, brother..." he began, stopping to catch his breath. "On the surface of the earth, as you see, there is frost, but if you raise a thermometer on a stick about two sazhens higher than the ground, it is warm there... Why is that?"

     "Really, I don't know," said Kovrin and laughed.

     "Hm... One cannot know everything, of course... No matter how vast the mind is, you cannot fit everything in there. You are mostly about philosophy, aren't you?"

     "Yes. I lecture on psychology, but I am occupied with philosophy in general."

     "And it doesn't get boring?"

     "On the contrary, I live by it alone."

     "Well, God grant..." Yegor Semyonych muttered, stroking his gray sideburns in thought. "God grant... I am very glad for you... glad, brother..."

     But suddenly he listened intently and, making a terrible face, ran to the side and soon disappeared behind the trees in clouds of smoke.

     "Who tied a horse to the apple tree?" his desperate, soul-wrenching cry was heard. "What scoundrel and rascal dared to tie a horse to the apple tree? My God, my God! They have spoiled, defiled, desecrated, messed it all up! The garden is ruined! The garden is lost! My God!"

     When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted and insulted.

     "Well, what can you do with these anathema people?" he said in a tearful voice, throwing up his hands. "Stepka was hauling manure during the night and tied the horse to the apple tree! The rascal wound the huge reins tight as can be, so that the bark was rubbed in three places. How about that! I talk to him, and he stands there like a blockhead and just blinks his eyes! Hanging is too good for him!"

     Having calmed down, he embraced Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

     "Well, God grant... God grant..." he muttered. "I am very glad that you came. Unspeakably glad... Thank you."

     Then he, with the same fast gait and preoccupied face, went around the entire garden and showed his former pupil all the greenhouses, hothouses, ground sheds, and his two apiaries, which he called the wonder of our century.

     While they walked, the sun rose and brightly lit the garden. It became warm. Foreseeing a clear, cheerful, long day, Kovrin remembered that it was still only the beginning of May and that a whole summer lay ahead, just as clear, cheerful, and long, and suddenly a joyful youthful feeling stirred in his chest, such as he used to experience in childhood when running through this garden. And he himself embraced the old man and kissed him tenderly. Both, moved, went into the house and began to drink tea from old porcelain cups, with cream and rich, buttery pretzels, and these trifles again reminded Kovrin of his childhood and youth. The beautiful present and the awakening impressions of the past merged together; they made his soul feel crowded, but good.

     He waited,when Tanya woke up, he had coffee with her, took a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read carefully, made notes, and occasionally raised his eyes to look at the open windows or at the fresh flowers, still wet with dew, standing in vases on the table, and lowered his eyes to the book again, and it seemed to him that every fiber in him was trembling and playing with pleasure.

     

II

     

     In the country he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city. He read and wrote a lot, studied the Italian language and, when he walked, thought with pleasure about how he would soon sit down to work again. He slept so little that everyone was surprised; if he accidentally fell asleep during the day for half an hour, then he would not sleep all night afterward, and after a sleepless night, as if nothing had happened, he felt vigorous and cheerful.

     He talked a lot, drank wine, and smoked expensive cigars. Young neighbor ladies often came to the Pesotskys, almost every day, and played the piano and sang with Tanya; sometimes a young man, a neighbor, who played the violin well, would come. Kovrin listened to the music and singing with greed and was exhausted by them, and the latter was expressed physically by the fact that his eyes would close and his head would lean to one side.

     Once after evening tea he sat on the balcony and read. In the drawing room at that time Tanya – a soprano, one of the young ladies – a contralto, and the young man on the violin were practicing Braga's famous serenade. Kovrin listened to the words – they were Russian – and could not understand their meaning at all. Finally, leaving the book and listening carefully, he understood: a girl, sick with imagination, heard some mysterious sounds in the garden at night, so beautiful and strange that she had to recognize them as a sacred harmony, which is incomprehensible to us mortals and therefore flies back to the heavens. Kovrin's eyes began to close. He stood up and walked through the drawing room in exhaustion, then through the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya's arm and went out onto the balcony with her.

     – A certain legend has been occupying me since early this morning, – he said. – I don't remember if I read it somewhere or heard it, but the legend is somehow strange, inconsistent with anything. To begin with, it is not distinguished by clarity. A thousand years ago a certain monk, dressed in black, was walking across the desert, somewhere in Syria or Arabia… A few miles from the place where he was walking, fishermen saw another black monk, who was moving slowly across the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend seems not to recognize, and listen further. From the mirage another mirage was produced, then from the other a third, so that the image of the black monk began to be transmitted endlessly from one layer of the atmosphere to another. He was seen now in Africa, now in Spain, now in India, now in the Far North… Finally, he passed beyond the limits of the Earth's atmosphere and is now wandering through the entire universe, still never getting into those conditions under which he could fade away. Perhaps he is seen now somewhere on Mars or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the very point of the legend is that exactly a thousand years after the monk walked in the desert, the mirage will again enter the Earth's atmosphere and appear to people. And it is as if these thousand years are already at an end… According to the meaning of the legend, we should expect the black monk any day now.

     – A strange mirage, – said Tanya, who did not like the legend.

     – But most surprising of all, – Kovrin laughed, – is that I cannot remember how this legend got into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Hear it? Or, perhaps, did I dream of the black monk? I swear to God, I don't remember. But the legend occupies me. I have been thinking about it all day today.

     Letting Tanya go back to the guests, he left the house and walked around the flowerbeds in thought. The sun was already setting. The flowers, because they had just been watered, emitted a moist, irritating scent. In the house they began to sing again, and from a distance the violin gave the impression of a human voice. Kovrin, straining his mind to remember where he had heard or read the legend, headed slowly into the park and imperceptibly reached the river.Along the path that ran along the steep bank past exposed roots, he went down to the water, disturbing the sandpipers and startling two ducks. On the gloomy pines the last rays of the setting sun still reflected here and there, but on the surface of the river it was already real evening. Kovrin crossed to the other side by the footbridge. Before him now lay a wide field, covered with young, not yet blooming rye. Neither human habitation nor a living soul in the distance, and it seems that the path, if one follows it, will lead to that very unknown mysterious place where the sun has just set and where the evening glow flames so broadly and majestically.

«How spacious, free, and quiet it is here! – thought Kovrin, walking along the path. – And it seems the whole world is looking at me, hiding and waiting for me to understand it…»

But then waves ran through the rye, and a light evening breeze gently touched his uncovered head. A minute later another gust of wind, but stronger this time, – the rye began to rustle, and the dull murmur of the pines was heard from behind. Kovrin stopped in amazement. On the horizon, like a whirlwind or a waterspout, a tall black column rose from the earth to the sky. Its contours were unclear, but in the very first moment it was possible to understand that it did not stand still, but moved with terrible speed, moved exactly here, straight at Kovrin, and the closer it approached, the smaller and clearer it became. Kovrin rushed to the side, into the rye, to give it way, and barely managed to do so…

A monk in black clothing, with a grey head and black eyebrows, arms crossed on his chest, swept past… His bare feet did not touch the ground. Having already swept past about three sazhens, he looked back at Kovrin, nodded his head and smiled at him affectionately and at the same time slyly. But what a pale, terribly pale, thin face! Starting to grow again, he flew across the river, noiselessly struck the clay bank and the pines and, passing through them, vanished like smoke.

– Well, there you see… – Kovrin muttered. – So, there is truth in the legend.

Without trying to explain the strange phenomenon to himself, satisfied with the mere fact that he had managed to see not only the black clothing but even the face and eyes of the monk so closely and so clearly, pleasantly excited, he returned home.

In the park and in the garden people were walking peacefully, in the house music was playing, – so, only he alone had seen the monk. He strongly wanted to tell Tanya and Yegor Semenych about everything, but he realized that they would probably take his words for a delusion, and this would frighten them; better to remain silent. He laughed loudly, sang, danced the mazurka, he was in high spirits, and everyone, the guests and Tanya, found that today his face was somehow special, radiant, inspired, and that he was very interesting.

III

After supper, when the guests had left, he went to his room and lay down on the sofa: he wanted to think about the monk. But a minute later Tanya entered.

– Here, Andryusha, read father's articles, – she said, handing him a stack of pamphlets and reprints. – Excellent articles. He writes wonderfully.

– Oh, wonderfully indeed! – said Yegor Semenych, entering after her and laughing forcedly; he was embarrassed. – Do not listen, please, do not read! However, if you want to fall asleep, then by all means read: a wonderful sleeping aid.

– In my opinion, magnificent articles, – said Tanya with deep conviction. – You read them, Andryusha, and persuade papa to write more often. He could write a complete course on gardening.

Yegor Semenych laughed tensely, turned red and began to say the phrases that embarrassed authors usually say. Finally, he began to give in.

– In that case read the article by Gaucher first and these Russian short articles, – he muttered, sorting through the pamphlets with trembling hands, – otherwise it will be incomprehensible to you. Before reading my objections, you must know what I am objecting to. However, nonsense… a bore. And it is time to sleep, it seems.

Tanya went out. Yegor Semenych sat down by Kovrin on the sofa and sighed deeply.

– Yes, my dear fellow… – he began after some silence. – That is how it is, my dearest master. Here I am writing articles, and participating in exhibitions, and receiving medals… At Pesotsky's, they say, the apples are the size of a head, and Pesotsky, they say, has made a fortune for himself with the garden.In short, Pesotsky is rich and famous. But one asks: what is all this for? The garden is, indeed, beautiful, exemplary… It is not a garden, but a whole institution, having high state importance, because it is, so to speak, a step into a new era of Russian economy and Russian industry. But for what? What is the goal?

     – The work speaks for itself.

     – I do not mean it in 그 sense. I want to ask: what will happen to the garden when I die? In the form you see it now, it will not last even one month without me. The whole secret of success is not that the garden is large and there are many workers, but that I love the work – do you understand? – I love it, perhaps, more than myself. Look at me: I do everything myself. I work from morning till night. All the grafting I do myself, the pruning – myself, the planting – myself, everything – myself. When they help me, I am jealous and irritated to the point of rudeness. The whole secret is in love, that is, in the sharp master's eye, and in the master's hands, and in that feeling when you go somewhere to visit for an hour, you sit, but your heart is not in its place, you are not yourself: you fear lest something should happen in the garden. And when I die, who will watch? Who will work? A gardener? Workers? Yes? So here is what I will tell you, dear friend: the first enemy in our business is not a hare, not a cockchafer and not frost, but a stranger.

     – And Tanya? – asked Kovrin, laughing. – It is impossible that she should be more harmful than a hare. She loves and understands the work.

     – Yes, she loves and understands. If after my death she gets the garden and she is the mistress, then, of course, one could not wish for better. Well, and if, God forbid, she gets married? – whispered Yegor Semenych and looked at Kovrin in alarm. – That is just it! She will get married, children will come, there will be no time to think about the garden then.

     – What I fear most of all: she will marry some fine fellow, and he will get greedy and lease the garden to market women, and everything will go to the devil in the very first year! In our business, women are the scourge of God!

     Yegor Semenych sighed and was silent for a while.

     – Maybe it is egoism, but I tell you frankly: I do not want Tanya to get married. I am afraid! A certain dandy with a violin comes here and scrapes away; I know that Tanya will not marry him, I know well, but I cannot see him! In general, brother, I am quite a big eccentric. I admit it.

     Yegor Semenych stood up and walked around the room in agitation, and it was clear that he wanted to say something very important, but did not dare to.

     – I love you dearly and will speak with you frankly, – he finally decided, thrusting his hands into his pockets. – To certain delicate questions I relate simply and say directly what I think, and I cannot stand so-called hidden thoughts. I say directly: you are the only person to whom I would not be afraid to give my daughter. You are a person of intelligence, with a heart, and would not let my favorite work perish. And the main reason – I love you like a son… and I am proud of you. If a romance were to work out between you and Tanya somehow, then – well? I would be very glad and even happy. I say this directly, without affectation, as an honest person.

     Kovrin laughed. Yegor Semenovich opened the door to leave and stopped on the threshold.

     – If a son were born to you and Tanya, then I would make a gardener out of him, – he said, after thinking. – However, this is an empty dream… Good night.

     Left alone, Kovrin lay down more comfortably and took up the articles. One had such a title: "On Intermediate Culture", another: "A Few Words Regarding the Note of Mr. Z. on Trenching the Soil for a New Garden", a third: "More on Budding with a Dormant Eye" – and everything in that vein. But what a restless, uneven tone, what a nervous, almost sickly ardor! Here is an article, it seems, with the most peaceful title and indifferent content: it speaks of the Russian Antonovka apple tree. But Yegor Semenych begins it with "audiatur altera pars" 1 and ends – "sapienti sat" 2, and between these sayings there is a whole fountain of various poisonous words addressed to the "learned ignorance of our patented Messrs. gardeners, observing nature from the heights of their chairs", or Mr. Gaucher, "whose success is created by profanes and dilettantes", and here too an inappropriately strained and insincere regret,that men stealing fruit and breaking trees in the process can no longer be flogged.

     "A beautiful, lovely, healthy business, but even here there are passions and war," thought Kovrin. "It must be that everywhere and in all fields, people of ideas are nervous and distinguished by heightened sensitivity. Likely, this is how it must be."

     He remembered Tanya, who so likes Egor Semenych's articles. Small in stature, pale, thin, so that her collarbones show; eyes wide open, dark, intelligent, always peering somewhere and searching for something; her gait, like her father's, small and hurried. She talks a lot, loves to argue, and accompanies every even insignificant phrase with expressive facial expressions and gestures. She must be nervous to the highest degree.

     Kovrin began to read further, but understood nothing and gave it up. A pleasant excitement, the same with which he had recently danced the mazurka and listened to music, now tormented him and evoked many thoughts in him. He got up and began to walk around the room, thinking about the black monk. It occurred to him that if only he alone had seen this strange, supernatural monk, then it meant he was ill and had already reached the point of hallucinations. This consideration frightened him, but not for long.

     "But I feel good, and I am doing no harm to anyone; therefore, there is nothing bad in my hallucinations," he thought, and he felt good again.

     He sat on the sofa and hugged his head with his hands, restraining the inexplicable joy that filled his entire being, then walked again and sat down to work. But the thoughts he gathered from the book did not satisfy him. He wanted something gigantic, vast, astonishing. Toward morning he undressed and reluctantly went to bed: he had to sleep after all!

     When the steps of Egor Semenych were heard, leaving for the garden, Kovrin rang and ordered the footman to bring wine. He drank several glasses of Lafite with pleasure, then covered himself over his head; his consciousness became clouded, and he fell asleep.

     

IV

     

     Egor Semenych and Tanya often quarreled and said unpleasant things to each other.

     One morning they had a spat about something. Tanya burst into tears and went to her room. She did not come out for dinner or for tea. Egor Semenych at first walked about importantly, puffed up, as if wishing to make it understood that for him the interests of justice and order were above everything in the world, but soon he could not maintain the character and lost heart. He wandered sadly through the park and kept sighing: "oh, my God, my God!" and did not eat a single crumb at dinner. At last, guilty, tormented by conscience, he knocked at the locked door and called out timidly:

     - Tanya! Tanya?

     And in response to him from behind the door came a weak voice, exhausted by tears and at the same time resolute:

     - Leave me alone, I beg you.

     The languor of the hosts was reflected throughout the house, even on the people working in the garden. Kovrin was immersed in his interesting work, but in the end even he became bored and uncomfortable. To somehow dispel the general bad mood, he decided to intervene and toward evening knocked on Tanya's door. He was let in.

     - Ay-ay, what a shame! - he began jokingly, looking with surprise at Tanya's tear-stained, red-blotched, mournful face. - Is it really so serious? Ay-ay!

     - But if you only knew how he tortures me! - she said, and tears, burning, abundant tears splashed from her large eyes. - He has worn me out! - she continued, wringing her hands. - I told him nothing... nothing... I only said that there is no need to keep... extra workers, if... if it is possible to have day laborers whenever you want. After all... after all, the workers haven't done anything for a whole week... I... I only said that, and he started shouting and said... many hurtful, deeply insulting things to me. For what?

     - There, there, - Kovrin said, fixing her hair. - You've scolded, you've cried, and that's enough. You shouldn't stay angry for long, it's not good... especially since he loves you infinitely.

     - He has... he has ruined my whole life, - Tanya continued, sobbing. - I hear nothing but insults and... and offenses. He considers me superfluous in his house. Well? He is right. I will leave here tomorrow, I'll become a telegraph operator... Let him...

     - Now, now, now... No need to cry, Tanya. No need,sweetheart… You are both hot-tempered, since

Kovrin spoke tenderly and convincingly, while she continued to cry, her shoulders twitching and her hands clenching, as if a terrible misfortune had truly befallen her. He felt sorry for her all the more because her grief was not serious, yet she suffered deeply. What trifles were enough to make this creature miserable for a whole day, and perhaps for a whole lifetime! While comforting Tanya, Kovrin thought that, besides this girl and her father, in the whole world one could not find with a lamp at noon people who would love him as their own, as kin; if not for these two people, then perhaps he, having lost his father and mother in early childhood, would not have known until his very death what sincere tenderness and that naive, unreasoning love are, which people feel only for very close, blood relatives. And he felt that his semi-sick, frayed nerves responded, like iron to a magnet, to the nerves of this weeping, twitching girl. He could never have loved a healthy, robust, red-cheeked woman, but the pale, weak, unhappy Tanya appealed to him.

     And he willingly stroked her hair and shoulders, pressed her hands and wiped away her tears… Finally, she stopped crying. She complained for a long time about her father and about her difficult, unbearable life in this house, begging Kovrin to understand her position; then she began little by little to smile and sigh that God had sent her such a bad character, and in the end, laughing out loud, called herself a fool and ran out of the room.

     When a little later Kovrin went out into the garden, Yegor Semenych and Tanya were already walking side by side along the path as if nothing had happened, and both were eating rye bread with salt, as both were hungry.

     

V

     

     Pleased that his role as a peacemaker had been so successful, Kovrin went into the park. Sitting on a bench and reflecting, he heard the clatter of carriages and women's laughter – guests had arrived. When evening shadows began to fall in the garden, the sounds of a violin and singing voices were faintly heard, and this reminded him of the black monk. Where now, in what country or on what planet, is that optical incongruity rushing?

     Hardly had he remembered the legend and pictured in his imagination that dark phantom he had seen in the rye field, when from behind a pine tree, exactly opposite, there stepped out noiselessly, without the slightest rustle, a person of medium height with an uncovered gray head, all in dark and barefoot, looking like a beggar, and black eyebrows stood out sharply on their pale, as if dead, face. Nodding their head affably, this beggar or wanderer noiselessly approached the bench and sat down, and Kovrin recognized in them the black monk. For a minute both looked at each other – Kovrin with amazement, and the monk tenderly and, just as then, a little slyly, with a self-knowing expression.

     – But you are a mirage, – Kovrin said. – Why then are you here and sitting in one place? This does not fit the legend.

     – It does not matter, – the monk replied not at once, in a quiet voice, turning their face to him. – The legend, the mirage, and I – all this is a product of your excited imagination. I am a phantom.

     – Does that mean you do not exist? – Kovrin asked.

     – Think what you like, – said the monk and smiled faintly. – I exist in your imagination, and your imagination is a part of nature, so I exist in nature as well.

     – You have a very old, wise, and highly expressive face, as if you have indeed lived more than a thousand years, – Kovrin said. – I did not know that my imagination was capable of creating such phenomena. But why do you look at me with such rapture? Do you like me?

     – Yes. You are one of those few who are rightly called God's chosen ones. You serve eternal truth. Your thoughts, intentions, your amazing science, and your whole life bear a divine, heavenly seal, since they are dedicated to the rational and the beautiful, that is, to what is eternal.

     – You said: eternal truth… But is eternal truth accessible and necessary to people if there is no eternal life?

     – There is eternal life, – said the monk.

     – Do you believe in the immortality of humans?

     – Yes, of course. A great, brilliant future awaits you humans. And the more people like you there are on earth, the sooner this future will be realized. Without you,without servants of the higher principle, living consciously and freely, humanity would be insignificant; developing in the natural order, it would have waited a long time for the end of its earthly history. You, however, will lead it into the kingdom of eternal truth several thousand years earlier – and in this lies your high merit. You embody the blessing of God, which has rested upon men.

     – And what is the purpose of eternal life? – Kovrin asked.

     – The same as any life – enjoyment. True enjoyment is in knowledge, and eternal life will provide countless and inexhaustible sources for knowledge, and in this sense it is said: in my Father's house are many mansions.

     – If you only knew how pleasant it is to listen to you! – said Kovrin, rubbing his hands with pleasure.

     – Very glad.

     – But I know: when you leave, I will be troubled by the question of your essence. You are a phantom, a hallucination. Does that mean I am mentally ill, abnormal?

     – Even if so. Why be disturbed? You are ill because you worked beyond your strength and became exhausted, and this means you sacrificed your health to an idea and the time is near when you will give to it your very life. What could be better? This is what all noble natures gifted from above strive for in general.

     – If I know that I am mentally ill, can I trust myself?

     – And why do you know that the men of genius, whom the whole world trusts, did not see phantoms too? Scientists say now that genius is akin to insanity. My friend, only mediocre, herd-like people are healthy and normal. Considerations about a nervous age, overwork, degeneration, etc., can seriously worry only those who see the purpose of life in the present, that is, herd-like people.

     – The Romans said: mens sana in corpore sano 3.

     – Not everything that the Romans or Greeks said is true. An elevated mood, excitement, ecstasy – everything that distinguishes prophets, poets, martyrs for an idea from ordinary people, is contrary to the animal side of man, that is, his physical health. I repeat: if you want to be healthy and normal, go to the herd.

     – Strange, you repeat what often comes into my own head, – said Kovrin. – You seem to have spied on and overheard my secret thoughts. But let us not talk about me. What do you mean by eternal truth?

     The monk did not answer. Kovrin looked at him and could not distinguish his face: its features were blurring and dissolving. Then the monk's head and hands began to disappear; his torso merged with the bench and with the evening twilight, and he vanished completely.

     – The hallucination is over! – said Kovrin and laughed. – A pity, though.

     He went back to the house cheerful and happy. The little that the black monk had said to him flattered not his vanity, but his whole soul, his whole being. To be the chosen one, to serve eternal truth, to stand in the rank of those who will make humanity worthy of the kingdom of God several thousand years earlier, that is, deliver people from several extra thousand years of struggle, sin, and suffering, to give everything to the idea – youth, strength, health, to be ready to die for the common good – what a high, what a happy lot! His past rushed through his memory, pure, chaste, full of work, he remembered what he had studied and what he himself had taught others, and decided that there was no exaggeration in the monk's words.

     Tanya was walking through the park toward him. She was already wearing a different dress.

     – You are here? – she said. – And we have been looking and looking for you... But what is the matter with you? – she wondered, looking at his ecstatic, radiant face and at his eyes, full of tears. – How strange you are, Andryusha.

     – I am satisfied, Tanya, – said Kovrin, putting his hands on her shoulders. – I am more than satisfied, I am happy! Tanya, dear Tanya, you are an extremely likable being. Dear Tanya, I am so glad, so glad!

     He warmly kissed both her hands and continued:

     – I have just experienced bright, wonderful, unearthly moments. But I cannot tell you everything, because you will call me mad or will not believe me. Let us talk about you. Dear, glorious Tanya! I love you and have already grown accustomed to loving. Your proximity, our meetings ten times a day have become a need of my soul. I do not know,how I will manage without you when I leave for my place.

     – Well! – Tanya laughed. – You will forget about us in two days. We are small people, and you are a great man.

     – No, let's speak seriously! – he said. – I will take you with me, Tanya. Yes? Will you go with me? Do you want to be mine?

     – Well! – Tanya said and wanted to laugh again, but the laughter did not come, and red spots appeared on her face.

     She began to breathe fast and walked quickly, quickly, but not toward the house, but further into the park.

     – I hadn't thought about this… hadn't thought! – she spoke, as if in despair wringing her hands.

     And Kovrin followed her and spoke all with the same radiant, ecstatic face:

     – I want a love that would take possession of all of me, and only you, Tanya, can give me this love. I am happy! Happy!

     She was stunned, bent over, shriveled up and as if she had aged ten years at once, but he found her beautiful and loudly expressed his delight:

     – How beautiful she is!

     

VI

     

     Upon learning from Kovrin that not only was the romance settled, but that there would even be a wedding, Yegor Semyonych walked from corner to corner for a long time, trying to hide his excitement. His hands began to shake, his neck swelled and turned crimson, he ordered the racing droshky to be harnessed and drove off somewhere. Tanya, who saw how he lashed the horse and how deeply, almost to his ears, he pulled down his cap, understood his mood, locked herself in her room and cried all day.

     In the greenhouses, peaches and plums had already ripened; the packing and shipping of this delicate and fickle cargo to Moscow required much attention, labor and trouble. Due to the fact that the summer was very hot and dry, it became necessary to water every tree, which took much time and manpower, and caterpillars appeared in great numbers, which the workers and even Yegor Semyonych and Tanya, to the great disgust of Kovrin, crushed right with their fingers. With all that, it was already necessary to take orders for the fall for fruits and trees and to conduct extensive correspondence. And at the busiest time, when, it seemed, no one had a free minute, field work began, which took away more than half of the workers from the garden; Yegor Semyonych, heavily tanned, exhausted, angry, galloped now to the garden, now to the field and shouted that he was being torn to pieces and that he would put a bullet in his forehead.

     And then there was also the fuss with the trousseau, to which the Pesotskys attached no small importance; from the clinking of scissors, the clicking of sewing machines, the fumes of irons and from the caprices of the dressmaker, a nervous, touchy lady, everyone's head was spinning in the house. And as if on purpose, guests arrived every day, who had to be entertained, fed and even stayed overnight. But all this hard labor passed unnoticed, as if in a fog. Tanya felt as if love and happiness had caught her by surprise, although since she was fourteen she had been sure for some reason that Kovrin would marry specifically her. She was amazed, bewildered, did not believe herself… Then suddenly such joy would flood in that she wanted to fly up below the clouds and pray to God there, and then suddenly it would be remembered that in August she would have to part with her childhood home and leave her father, or, God knows where from, the thought would come that she was insignificant, small and unworthy of such a great man as Kovrin, – and she goes to her room, locks herself in and cries bitterly for several hours. When there are guests, it suddenly seems to her that Kovrin is unusually handsome and that all women are in love with him and envy her, and her soul is filled with delight and pride, as if she has conquered the whole world, but as soon as he smiles kindly at some young lady, she is already trembling with jealousy, goes to her room – and tears again. These new sensations took possession of her completely, she helped her father mechanically and did not notice the peaches, or the caterpillars, or the workers, or how fast time was running.

     Almost the same thing was happening with Yegor Semyonych. He worked from morning till night, was always rushing somewhere, losing his temper, getting irritated, but all this was in a kind of magical half-sleep. It was as if two people were already sitting in him: one was the real Yegor Semyonych, who, listening to the gardener Ivan Karlych reporting to him about disorders, was outraged and in despair grabbed his head, and the other,not the real one, exactly like someone half-drunk, who would suddenly break off a business conversation mid-sentence, touch the gardener's shoulder, and begin to mutter:

     – Say what you will, but blood means a lot. His mother was an amazing, most noble, most intelligent woman. It was a delight to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel's. She drew beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang... Poor thing, may she rest in peace, she died of consumption.

     The not-real Egor Semenych would sigh and, after a silence, continue:

     – When he was a boy and growing up at my place, he had the same angelic face, clear and kind. His look, his movements, and his conversation are tender and elegant, just like his mother's. And his mind? He always amazed us with his mind. And it must be said, he is a Master for a reason! For a reason! Just wait, Ivan Karlych, what he will be like in ten years! He will be out of reach!

     But then the real Egor Semenych, catching himself, would make a terrible face, grab his head, and shout:

     – Devils! They've fouled it, ruined it, made it loathsome! The garden is lost! The garden is dead!

     And Kovrin worked with his former diligence and did not notice the commotion. Love only added fuel to the fire. After every meeting with Tanya he, happy, ecstatic, went to his room and with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and declared his love to her, took up his book or his manuscript. What the black monk said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, the brilliant future of humanity, etc., gave his work a special, extraordinary significance and filled his soul with pride, a consciousness of his own height. Once or twice a week, in the park or in the house, he met with the black monk and talked with him for a long time, but this did not frighten him, but, on the contrary, delighted him, as he was already firmly convinced that such visions visit only chosen, outstanding people who have dedicated themselves to serving an idea.

     One day the monk appeared during dinner and sat in the dining room by the window. Kovrin was delighted and very cleverly started a conversation with Egor Semenych and with Tanya about what might be interesting for the monk; the black guest listened and nodded his head affably, and Egor Semenych and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, not suspecting that Kovrin was talking not to them, but to his hallucination.

     Unnoticeably the Dormition Fast approached, and after it soon the wedding day, which, at the persistent wish of Egor Semenych, was celebrated "with a bang", that is, with senseless revelry that lasted two days. They ate and drank about three thousand worth, but because of poor hired music, loud toasts, and the scurrying of servants, from the noise and crowding, they did not appreciate the taste of either the expensive wines or the amazing appetizers ordered from Moscow.

     

VII

     

     Once on one of the long winter nights Kovrin was lying in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, who had headaches in the evenings from the lack of habit of living in the city, had been asleep for a long time and occasionally uttered some incoherent phrases in a delirium.

     It struck three o'clock. Kovrin put out the candle and lay down; he lay for a long time with his eyes closed, but could not fall asleep because, as it seemed to him, it was very hot in the bedroom and Tanya was delirious. At half past four he lit the candle again and at that time saw the black monk, who was sitting in an armchair near the bed.

     – Hello, – said the monk and, after a short silence, asked: – What are you thinking about now?

     – About fame, – replied Kovrin. – In the French novel that I just read, there is a depiction of a man, a young scientist, who does foolish things and wastes away from a longing for fame. This longing is incomprehensible to me.

     – Because you are intelligent. You treat fame with indifference, like a toy that does not interest you.

     – Yes, that's true.

     – Renown does not smile upon you. What is flattering, or amusing, or instructive in the fact that your name will be carved on a tombstone and then time will erase this inscription along with the gilding? And, fortunately, there are too many of you for weak human memory to be able to retain your names.

     – Naturally, – Kovrin agreed. – And why remember them? But let us talk about something else. For example, about happiness. What is happiness?When the clock struck 5, he sat on the bed, his feet hanging down onto the carpet, and spoke, addressing the monk:

     – In ancient times, one happy man finally grew afraid of his happiness – it was so great! – and, to appease the gods, he sacrificed his favorite ring to them. You know? And I, like Polycrates, am beginning to be a little disturbed by my happiness. It seems strange to me that from morning till night I experience only joy, it fills all of me and muffles all other feelings. I do not know what sadness, grief, or boredom are. Here I am not sleeping, I have insomnia, but I am not bored. Seriously, I am beginning to be perplexed.

     – But why? – the monk was amazed. – Is joy a supernatural feeling? Should it not be the normal state of man? The higher a person is in mental and moral development, the freer they are, the more pleasure life gives them. Socrates, Diogenes, and Marcus Aurelius experienced joy, not sadness. And the apostle says: rejoice always. So rejoice and be happy.

     – And what if the gods get angry? – Kovrin joked and laughed. – If they take away my comfort and make me freeze and starve, then it will hardly be to my taste.

     Tanya meanwhile woke up and looked at her husband with amazement and horror. He was speaking, addressing the armchair, gesturing and laughing: his eyes sparkled and there was something strange in the laughter.

     – Andryusha, who are you talking to? – she asked, grabbing the hand he had stretched out toward the monk. – Andryusha! To whom?

     – Ah? To whom? – Kovrin was embarrassed. – To him… There he sits, – he said, pointing to the black monk.

     – There is no one here… no one! Andryusha, you are ill!

     Tanya embraced her husband and pressed against him as if protecting him from visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

     – You are ill! – she sobbed, trembling with her whole body. – Forgive me, dear, darling, but I have noticed for a long time that your soul is upset by something… You are mentally ill, Andryusha…

     Her trembling was communicated to him as well. He looked once more at the armchair, which was already empty, suddenly felt weakness in his arms and legs, got scared and began to dress.

     – It is nothing, Tanya, nothing… – he muttered, trembling. – In fact, I am a little unwell… it is time to admit it.

     – I have been noticing for a long time… and papa noticed, – she said, trying to hold back her sobs. – You talk to yourself, smile strangely somehow… you do not sleep. Oh, my god, my god, save us! – she said in horror. – But do not be afraid, Andryusha, do not be afraid, for god's sake, do not be afraid…

     She also began to dress. Only now, looking at her, did Kovrin understand all the danger of his position, understand what the black monk and the conversations with him meant. It was clear to him now that he was insane.

     Both, not knowing why themselves, dressed and went into the hall: she in front, he behind her. There already, awakened by the sobs, in a dressing gown and with a candle in his hands, stood Yegor Semyonych, who was staying with them.

     – Do not be afraid, Andryusha, – Tanya said, trembling as if in a fever, – do not be afraid… Papa, this will all pass… all pass…

     Kovrin could not speak from agitation. He wanted to say to his father-in-law in a joking tone:

     – Congratulate me, I think I have gone mad, – but he only moved his lips and smiled bitterly.

     At 9 o'clock in the morning, they put a coat and a fur coat on him, wrapped him in a shawl, and took him in a carriage to the doctor. He began to be treated.

     

VIII

     

     Summer came again, and the doctor ordered him to go to the country. Kovrin had already recovered, stopped seeing the black monk, and he only had to bolster his physical strength. Living at his father-in-law's in the country, he drank a lot of milk, worked only 2 hours a day, did not drink wine, and did not smoke.

On the eve of St. Elijah's Day, a vigil was being served in the house. When the deacon handed the censer to the priest, the old, vast hall began to smell exactly like a cemetery, and Kovrin felt bored. He went out into the garden. Not noticing the magnificent flowers, he walked through the garden, sat on a bench, then strolled through the park; reaching the river, he went down and stood there in thought, looking at the water. The gloomy pines with shaggy roots, which had seen him here last year so young, joyful, and vigorous, did not whisper now, but stood motionless and mute, as if they did not recognize him. And indeed, his head was cropped, his long beautiful hair was gone, his gait was sluggish, and his face, compared to last summer, had filled out and grown pale.

He crossed to the other side via the footbridge. Where there had been rye last year, mown oats now lay in rows. The sun had already set, and a broad red glow blazed on the horizon, foretelling windy weather for tomorrow. It was quiet. Looking in the direction where the black monk had first appeared last year, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes, until the evening glow began to fade...

When he, sluggish and dissatisfied, returned home, the vigil was already over. Yegor Semenych and Tanya were sitting on the terrace steps drinking tea. They were talking about something, but seeing Kovrin, they suddenly fell silent, and he concluded from their faces that their conversation had been about him.

– It seems it is time for you to drink your milk, – Tanya said to her husband.

– No, it is not time... – he replied, sitting down on the lowest step. – Drink it yourself. I do not want any.

Tanya exchanged an anxious look with her father and said in a guilty voice:

– You notice yourself that milk is good for you.

– Yes, very good! – Kovrin smiled sarcastically. – I congratulate you: since Friday I have gained another 1 pound in weight. – He squeezed his head tightly with his hands and spoke with anguish: – Why, why did you treat me? Bromide preparations, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear over every sip, over every step – all this will ultimately lead me to idiocy. I was going mad, I had megalomania, but on the other hand I was cheerful, vigorous, and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I have become more sensible and solid, but in return I am like everyone else: I am a mediocrity, I am bored with living... Oh, how cruelly you have treated me! I saw hallucinations, but whom did that harm? I ask: whom did that harm?

– God knows what you are saying! – Yegor Semenych sighed. – It is even boring to listen.

– Then do not listen.

The presence of people, especially Yegor Semenych, now irritated Kovrin, he answered him dryly, coldly, and even rudely, and looked at him only mockingly and with hatred, while Yegor Semenych was embarrassed and coughed guiltily, although he felt no guilt on his part. Not understanding why their sweet, good-natured relations had changed so sharply, Tanya huddled close to her father and looked anxiously into his eyes; she wanted to understand and could not, and for her it was only clear that their relations were becoming worse and worse every day, that her father had aged greatly lately, and her husband had become irritable, capricious, fault-finding, and uninteresting. She could no longer laugh and sing, she ate nothing at dinner, she did not sleep for whole nights, expecting something terrible, and was so exhausted that once she lay in a faint from dinner until evening. During the vigil it seemed to her that her father was crying, and now, when the three of them were sitting on the terrace, she made an effort not to think about it.

– How lucky Buddha and Mahomet or Shakespeare were that their kind relatives and doctors did not treat them for ecstasy and inspiration! – Kovrin said. – If Mahomet had taken potassium bromide for his nerves, worked only two hours a day and drunk milk, then as little would have remained of that remarkable man as of his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will ultimately ensure that humanity grows dull, mediocrity will be considered genius, and civilization will perish. If you only knew, – Kovrin said with vexation, – how grateful I am to you!

He felt strong irritation and, to avoid saying too much, he rose quickly and went into the house. It was quiet,and through the open windows the scent of tobacco and jalap drifted in from the garden. In the vast dark hall, moonlight lay in green patches on the floor and on the piano. Kovrin recalled the delights of last summer, when it smelled of jalap in the same way and the moon shone in the windows. To recapture last year's mood, he quickly went to his study, lit a strong cigar, and ordered the valet to bring some wine. But the cigar left a bitter and disgusting taste in his mouth, and the wine did not taste the same as last year. And what it means to lose a habit! From the cigar and two sips of wine, his head spun and palpitations began, so that he had to take potassium bromide.

     Before going to bed, Tanya said to him:

     – Father adores you. You are angry at him for something, and it is killing him. Look: he is aging not by days, but by hours. I implore you, Andryusha, for God's sake, for the sake of your late father, for the sake of my peace, be kind to him!

     – I cannot and I will not.

     – But why? – Tanya asked, beginning to tremble all over. – Explain to me, why?

     – Because he is not sympathetic to me, that is all, – Kovrin said carelessly and shrugged his shoulders, – but let us not talk about him: he is your father.

     – I cannot, I cannot understand! – Tanya uttered, clutching her temples and staring at one point. – Something inconceivable, terrible is happening in our house. You have changed, you have become unlike yourself… You, an intelligent, extraordinary person, get irritated over trifles, interfere in squabbles… Such small things agitate you that sometimes one is simply amazed and cannot believe: is it really you? Well, well, do not be angry, do not be angry, – she continued, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. – You are intelligent, kind, noble. You will be fair to father. He is so kind!

     – He is not kind, but good-natured. Vaudeville uncles like your father, with well-fed good-natured faces, unusually hospitable and eccentric, used to touch and amuse me in stories, in vaudevilles, and in life, but now they are disgusting to me. They are egoists to the marrow of their bones. Most disgusting to me is their satiety and this gastric, purely bovine or swinish optimism.

     Tanya sat on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

     – This is torture, – she uttered, and it was clear from her voice that she was already extremely exhausted and that it was hard for her to speak. – Since the very winter not a single peaceful minute… This is terrible, my God! I am suffering…

     – Yes, of course, I am Herod, and you and your papa are the Innocents of Egypt. Of course!

     His face seemed unattractive and unpleasant to Tanya. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. And even earlier she had noticed that something was already missing from his face, as if since he had his hair cut, his face had also changed. She wanted to say something hurtful to him, but immediately she caught herself in a hostile feeling, got scared, and left the bedroom.

     

IX

     

     Kovrin received an independent chair. The introductory lecture was scheduled for the second of December, and a notice about this was posted in the university corridor. But on the appointed day, he informed the inspector of students by telegram that he would not deliver the lecture due to illness.

     He was bleeding from the throat. He spat blood, but it happened about twice a month that it flowed profusely, and then he became extremely weak and fell into a somnolent state. This illness did not particularly frighten him, as he knew that his late mother had lived with exactly the same illness for 10 years, even more; and the doctors assured him that it was not dangerous, and only advised him not to get excited, to lead a regular life, and to talk less.

     In January, the lecture again did not take place for the same reason, and in February it was already too late to start the course. It had to be postponed until next year.

     He no longer lived with Tanya, but with another woman, who was 2 years older than him and looked after him like a child. His mood was peaceful, submissive: he obeyed willingly, and when Varvara Nikolaevna – that was his companion's name – prepared to take him to the Crimea, he agreed, although he had a premonition that nothing good would come of this trip.

     They arrived in Sevastopol in the evening and stayed at a hotel,to rest and go to Yalta tomorrow. Both were exhausted by the journey. Varvara Nikolaevna had some tea, lay down, and soon fell asleep. But Kovrin did not go to bed. Even at home, an hour before leaving for the station, he had received a letter from Tanya and had not dared to open it, and now it lay in his side pocket, and the thought of it disturbed him unpleasantly. Sincerely, in the depths of his soul, he now considered his marriage to Tanya a mistake, was glad that he had finally parted with her, and the memory of this woman, who had ultimately turned into a walking living relic, and in whom, it seemed, everything had already died except for large, intently gazing, intelligent eyes, the memory of her evoked in him only pity and annoyance at himself. The handwriting on the envelope reminded him of how unfair and cruel he had been two years ago, how he had vented his inner emptiness, boredom, loneliness, and dissatisfaction with life on innocent people. By the way, he remembered how he once tore his dissertation and all the articles written during his illness into small pieces, and how he threw them out the window, and the pieces, flying in the wind, caught on trees and flowers; in every line he saw strange, groundless claims, lightheaded defiance, audacity, megalomania, and this made such an impression on him as if he were reading a description of his own vices; but when the last notebook was torn and flew out the window, for some reason he suddenly felt annoyed and bitter, he went to his wife and said many unpleasant things to her. My God, how he tormented her! Once, wishing to cause her pain, he told her that her father played an unattractive role in their romance, since he had asked him to marry her; Yegor Semenych accidentally overheard this, ran into the room and out of despair could not utter a single word, and only trampled in one spot and somehow strangely lowed, as if his tongue had failed him, and Tanya, looking at her father, screamed in a piercing voice and fainted. It was ugly.

All this came to mind at the sight of the familiar handwriting. Kovrin went out onto the balcony; the weather was quiet and warm, and it smelled of the sea. The wonderful bay reflected the moon and lights and had a color that is hard to find a name for. It was a delicate and soft combination of blue and green; in places the water resembled the color of blue vitriol, and in places, it seemed, moonlight had thickened and filled the bay instead of water, and on the whole what harmony of colors, what a peaceful, calm, and sublime mood!

On the lower floor, under the balcony, the windows were probably open, because women's voices and laughter were clearly heard. Apparently, there was a party there.

Kovrin made an effort, opened the letter and, entering his room, read:

"My father just died. I owe this to you, as you killed him. Our garden is perishing, strangers are already managing it, that is, exactly what poor father was so afraid of is happening. I owe this to you too. I hate you with all my soul and wish for you to perish sooner. Oh, how I suffer! Unbearable pain burns my soul… Be cursed. I took you for an extraordinary person, for a genius, I loved you, but you turned out to be a madman…"

Kovrin could not read further, tore the letter to pieces and threw it away. An uneasiness similar to fear took possession of him. Behind the screens, Varvara Nikolaevna was sleeping, and one could hear how she breathed; from the lower floor came women's voices and laughter, but he had such a feeling as if there were not a single living soul in the whole hotel except for him. Because unhappy, grief-stricken Tanya in her letter cursed him and wished for his downfall, he felt eerie, and he glanced briefly at the door, as if afraid that the unknown force, which in just two years had caused so much destruction in his life and the lives of his loved ones, would not enter the room and take charge of him again.

He already knew from experience that when nerves act up, the best remedy for them is work. One must sit at the table and force oneself, at all costs, to concentrate on some single thought. He took out from his red portfolio a notebook in which a draft of a small compiled work was sketched,invented by him in case he felt bored in Crimea without work. He sat down at the table and occupied himself with this summary, and it seemed to him that his peaceful, submissive, indifferent mood was returning. The notebook with the summary even led him to reflect on the vanity of the world. He thought about how much life takes for those insignificant or very ordinary benefits that it can give to a person. For example, to obtain a chair at the age of forty, to be an ordinary professor, to expound in a sluggish, boring, heavy language ordinary and moreover someone else's thoughts, – in a word, in order to reach the position of a mediocre scientist, he, Kovrin, had to study for fifteen years, work days and nights, endure a severe mental illness, go through an unsuccessful marriage and perform many various stupidities and injustices, which it would be pleasant not to remember. Kovrin now clearly realized that he was a mediocrity, and willingly reconciled himself to this, since, in his opinion, every person should be satisfied with what he is.

     The summary had almost completely calmed him down, but the torn letter showed white on the floor and prevented him from concentrating. He got up from the table, picked up the scraps of the letter and threw them out the window, but a light wind blew from the sea, and the scraps scattered across the windowsill. Again he was seized by an anxiety similar to fear, and it began to seem that in the entire hotel there was not a single soul besides him… He went out onto the balcony. The bay, like a living thing, looked at him with a multitude of blue, dark blue, turquoise and fiery eyes and beckoned to him. Indeed, it was hot and stifling and it would not hurt to take a bath.

     Suddenly on the lower floor under the balcony a violin began to play, and two tender female voices began to sing. It was something familiar. In the romance that was sung below, it spoke of some girl, diseased in imagination, who heard mysterious sounds in the garden at night and decided that it was a sacred harmony, incomprehensible to us, mortals… Kovrin's breath caught, and his heart tightened with sadness, and a wonderful, sweet joy, which he had long since forgotten, trembled in his chest.

     A tall black column, resembling a whirlwind or a waterspout, appeared on that shore of the bay. It moved with terrible speed across the bay toward the hotel, becoming smaller and darker, and Kovrin barely had time to step aside to make way… A monk with an uncovered gray head and black eyebrows, barefoot, with hands crossed on his chest, rushed past and stopped in the middle of the room.

     – Why did you not believe me? – he asked reproachfully, looking affectionately at Kovrin. – If you had believed me then, that you are a genius, then you would have spent these two years not so sadly and scantily.

     Kovrin already believed that he was God's chosen one and a genius, he vividly recalled all his former conversations with the black monk and wanted to speak, but blood flowed from his throat straight onto his chest, and he, not knowing what to do, moved his hands over his chest, and his cuffs became wet with blood. He wanted to call Varvara Nikolaevna, who was sleeping behind the screens, made an effort and uttered:

     – Tanya!

     He fell to the floor and, rising on his hands, called again:

     – Tanya!

     He called Tanya, called the large garden with luxurious flowers, sprayed with dew, called the park, the pines with shaggy roots, the rye field, his wonderful science, his youth, courage, joy, called life, which was so beautiful. He saw on the floor near his face a large pool of blood and could no longer utter a single word due to weakness, but an inexpressible, boundless happiness filled his entire being. Below under the balcony a serenade was playing, and the black monk whispered to him that he was a genius and that he was dying only because his weak human body had already lost its balance and could no longer serve as a shell for a genius.

     When Varvara Nikolaevna woke up and came out from behind the screens, Kovrin was already dead, and a blissful smile was frozen on his face.

     VOLODYA 

     On one of the summer Sundays, around five o'clock in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old youth, unattractive, sickly and timid,Volodya sat in the gazebo at the Shumikhins' summer house and was bored. His gloomy thoughts flowed in three directions. Firstly, tomorrow, on Monday, he was to take an exam in mathematics; he knew that if he failed to solve the written problem tomorrow, he would be expelled, as he had been in the sixth grade for two years and had a yearly mark in algebra of 2 3/4. Secondly, his stay with the Shumikhins, people who were wealthy and claimed to be aristocratic, caused constant pain to his pride. It seemed to him that m-me Shumikhina and her nieces looked at him and his maman as poor relatives and dependents, that they did not respect maman and laughed at her. Once he accidentally overheard m-me Shumikhina telling her cousin Anna Fedorovna on the terrace that his maman continued to try to look young and beautify herself, that she never paid her gambling losses and had a penchant for other people's shoes and other people's tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to go to the Shumikhins, described to her what an insulting role she played with these people, tried to persuade her, said insolent things, but she, frivolous, spoiled, having spent two fortunes in her lifetime - her own and her husband's, always gravitating toward high society, did not understand him, and twice a week Volodya had to accompany her to the hated summer house.

Thirdly, the young man could not for a moment get rid of a strange, unpleasant feeling that was completely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with the Shumikhins' cousin and guest, Anna Fedorovna. She was a lively, loud-voiced and laugh-prone lady, about thirty years old, healthy, strong, rosy, with round shoulders, a round fat chin and a constant smile on her thin lips. She was not beautiful and not young - Volodya knew this perfectly well, but for some reason he was unable not to think of her, not to look at her when she, playing croquet, shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back or when, after long laughter and running around on the stairs, she would fall into an armchair and, closing her eyes, breathing heavily, pretend that her chest felt tight and stifled. She was married. Her husband, a solid architect, came to the summer house once a week, slept excellently and returned back to the city. Volodya's strange feeling began with him hating this architect for no reason and rejoicing every time he left for the city.

Now, sitting in the gazebo and thinking about tomorrow's exam and about maman, who was being laughed at, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (as the Shumikhins called Anna Fedorovna), to hear her laughter, the rustle of her dress... This desire did not resemble that pure, poetic love that was familiar to him from novels and which he dreamed of every evening when going to bed; it was strange, incomprehensible, he was ashamed of it and feared it like something very bad and impure, which was hard to admit to oneself...

- This is not love, - he said to himself. - One does not fall in love with thirty-year-olds and married women... This is just a little intrigue... Yes, an intrigue...

Thinking about the intrigue, he remembered his invincible shyness, the absence of a mustache, his freckles, narrow eyes, placed himself in his imagination next to Nyuta - and this pair seemed impossible to him; then he hurried to imagine himself handsome, brave, witty, dressed in the very latest fashion...

In the very heat of his dreams, while he, hunched over and looking at the ground, sat in a dark corner of the gazebo, light footsteps were heard. Someone was walking unhurriedly along the alley. Soon the footsteps went quiet and something white flickered at the entrance.

- Is anyone here? - a female voice asked.

Volodya recognized the voice and raised his head in alarm.

- Who is there? - Nyuta asked, entering the gazebo. - Oh, is it you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? And how can one keep thinking, thinking, thinking... one could go mad that way!

Volodya stood up and looked at Nyuta in confusion. She had just returned from the bathing house. A sheet and a fuzzy towel hung on her shoulders, and from under the white silk scarf on her head, wet hair peeked out, sticking to her forehead. A moist, cool smell of the bathing house and almond soap came from her. From walking fast, she was out of breath.The top button of her blouse was undone, so that the youth

Volodya listened and, in heavy, strained reflection, scratched his temple.

- Only very proud people remain silent and love solitude, - Nyuta continued, pulling his hand away from his temple. - You are a proud man, Volodya. Why are you looking from under your brows? Please look me straight in the face! Come on now, you seal!

Volodya decided to speak. Wanting to smile, he twitched his lower lip, blinked his eyes and again pulled his hand toward his temple.

- I... I love you! - he uttered.

Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed.

- What do I hear?! - she began to sing, as opera singers sing when they hear something terrible. - How? What did you say? Repeat it, repeat it...

- I... I love you! - Volodya repeated.

And already without any participation of his will, understanding and realizing nothing, he took half a step toward Nyuta and took her by the arm above the wrist. His eyes grew blurred and tears came to them, the whole world turned into one large, shaggy towel that smelled of the bathhouse.

- Bravo, bravo! - he heard cheerful laughter. - Why are you silent? I want you to speak! Well?

Seeing that he was not being prevented from holding her arm, Volodya looked at Nyuta's laughing face and clumsily, awkwardly took her by the waist with both hands, so that the palms of both his hands met on her back. He held her by the waist with both hands, and she, throwing her hands behind her head and showing the dimples on her elbows, adjusted her hair under her scarf and spoke in a calm voice:

- You must, Volodya, be agile, courteous, sweet, and one can become like that only under the influence of female society. However, what an unpleasant... wicked face you have. You must talk, laugh... Yes, Volodya, don't be a grouch, you are young and will still have time to play the philosopher. Now, let me go, I'm going. Let go!

She freed her waist without effort and, humming something, left the gazebo. Volodya remained alone. He smoothed down his hair, smiled and walked from corner to corner about three times, then sat on the bench and smiled once more. He felt unbearably ashamed, so much so that he even wondered that human shame could reach such sharpness and intensity. From shame he smiled, whispered some incoherent words and gestured.

He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and above all that he had dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist, although neither by age, nor by his outward qualities, nor by social status did he, as it seemed to him, have any right to do so.

He jumped up, left the gazebo and, without looking back, went into the depths of the garden away from the house.

"Oh, if only I could leave here sooner! - he thought, clutching his head. God, if only it were sooner!"

The train on which Volodya was to go with maman left at eight hours forty minutes. About three hours remained until the train, but he would have gone to the station right now with pleasure, without waiting for maman.

In the eighth hour he approached the house. His whole figure depicted determination: come what may! He decided to enter boldly, look straight, speak loudly, regardless of everything.

He passed the terrace, the large hall, the drawing room and stopped in the latter to catch his breath. From here it could be heard how they were drinking tea in the neighboring dining room. M-me Shumikhina, maman and Nyuta were talking about something and laughing.

Volodya listened.

- I assure you! - Nyuta was saying. - I didn't believe my own eyes! When he began to declare his love to me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I didn't recognize him. And you know, he has a manner! When he said that he was in love with me, there was something beastly in his face, like a Circassian.

- Really! - gasped maman, breaking into a long laugh. - Really! How he reminds me of his father!

Volodya ran back and skipped out into the fresh air. "And how can they speak aloud about this! - he suffered, throwing up his hands and looking at the sky in horror. - They speak aloud, cold-bloodedly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?"

But it was necessary to go into the house, no matter what. He walked along the alley three times, calmed down a little and entered the house.- Why do you not come in time to drink tea? - m-me Shumikhina asked strictly.

- I am to blame, I... I must go, - he muttered, without raising his eyes. Maman, it is already eight o'clock!

- Go by yourself, my dear, - maman said languidly, - I am staying the night at Lili's. Goodbye, my friend... Let me cross you...

She crossed her son and said in French, addressing Nyuta:

- He looks a little like Lermontov... Doesn't he?

Having somehow said goodbye and without looking at anyone's face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later he was already walking along the road to the station and was glad of it. Now he was no longer afraid or ashamed, he breathed easily and freely.

Half a verst from the station he sat on a stone by the road and began to look at the sun, which was more than half hidden behind the embankment. At the station lights had already been lit here and there, a single murky green light flickered, but the train was not yet visible. Volodya found it pleasant to sit, not moving, and to listen to how evening was coming on little by little. The gloom of the gazebo, the footsteps, the smell of the bathing house, the laughter and the waist - all this stood out in his imagination with striking clarity, and all this was no longer as frightening and significant as before...

"Nonsense... She did not pull her hand away and laughed when I held her by the waist, - he thought, - so she liked it. If it had been disgusting to her, she would have been angry..."

And now Volodya felt annoyed that there, in the gazebo, he had not had enough courage. He felt sorry that he was leaving so stupidly, and he was already sure that if that occasion were to repeat itself, he would be bolder and look at things more simply.

And it was not difficult for the occasion to repeat itself. At the Shumikhins' they walk for a long time after supper. If Volodya goes for a walk with Nyuta through the dark garden, then - there is the occasion!

"I will return, - he thought, - and I will leave tomorrow by the morning train... I will say that I missed the train".

And he returned... M-me Shumikhina, maman, Nyuta and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace playing vint. When Volodya lied to them that he had missed the train, they became worried lest he should be late for his exam tomorrow, and advised him to get up earlier. All the time they were playing cards, he sat to one side, greedily watching Nyuta and waiting... A plan was already ready in his head: he would approach Nyuta in the darkness, take her by the hand, then embrace her; there was no need to say anything, as everything would be clear to both without talk.

But after supper the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and continued playing cards. They played until one o'clock in the morning and then dispersed to sleep.

"How stupid all this is! - Volodya felt annoyed, lying down in bed. - But no matter, I will wait for tomorrow... Tomorrow in the gazebo again. It is nothing..."

He did not try to sleep, but sat in bed, his arms around his knees, and thought. The thought of the exam was disgusting to him. He had already decided that he would be expelled and that there was nothing terrible in this expulsion. On the contrary, everything was very good, even very much so. Tomorrow he would be free as a bird, he would put on civilian clothes, he would smoke openly, come here and court Nyuta whenever he liked; and he would no longer be a gymnasium student, but a "young man". And the rest, which is called a career and a future, was so clear: Volodya would join as a volunteer, become a telegrapher, or finally go into a pharmacy, where he would work his way up to a pharmacist... are there not plenty of positions? An hour or two passed, and he was still sitting and thinking...

In the third hour, when it was already growing light, the door creaked cautiously and maman entered the room.

- You are not sleeping? - she asked, yawning. - Sleep, sleep, I am only here for a minute... I will just take the drops...

- What do you need them for?

- Poor Lili has spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow...

She took a bottle of something from the cabinet, went to the window, read the label and went out.

- Marya Leontyevna, those are not the drops! - Volodya heard a woman's voice a minute later. - That is lily of the valley, and Lili is asking for morphine. Is your son sleeping? Ask him to find it...

It was Nyuta's voice. Volodya turned cold. He quickly put on his trousers, threw his greatcoat over his shoulders and went to the door.

- Do you understand? Morphine! - Nyuta explained in a whisper. - It must be written there in Latin.Wake Volodya up, he will find...

Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was wearing the same blouse in which she had gone swimming. Her hair was uncombed, scattered over her shoulders, her face sleepy, dark from the twilight...

- Here Volodya is awake... - she said. - Volodya, look, my dear, for the morphine in the cabinet! What a punishment with this Lili... She always has something.

Maman muttered something, yawned and left.

- Look then, - said Nyuta. - Why are you standing there?

Volodya went to the small cabinet, knelt down and began to sort through the vials and boxes of medicines. His hands were trembling, and in his chest and stomach there was a sensation as if cold waves were running through all his insides. From the smell of ether, carbolic acid and various herbs, which he grabbed without any need with trembling hands and which scattered because of this, he felt stifled and his head spun.

"It seems maman has left, - he thought. - That is good... good..."

- Will it be soon? - Nyuta asked with a drawl.

- Just a moment... This, I think, is morphine... - said Volodya, having read the word "morph..." on one of the labels. - Here you are!

Nyuta stood in the doorway so that one of her feet was in the corridor and the other in his room. She was adjusting her hair, which was difficult to adjust because it was so thick and long! - and looked vacantly at Volodya. In the spacious blouse, sleepy, with loose hair, in that meager light that came into the room from the white but not yet sunlit sky, she seemed to Volodya enchanting, luxurious... Charmed, trembling with his whole body and with pleasure remembering how he had embraced this wonderful body in the gazebo, he handed her the drops and said:

- How you are...

- What?

She entered the room.

- What? - she asked, smiling.

He was silent and looked at her, then, as he had in the gazebo, took her by the hand... And she looked at him, smiled and waited: what would happen next?

- I love you... - he whispered.

She stopped smiling, thought and said:

- Wait, I think someone is coming. Oh, these schoolboys! - she said in a low voice, going to the door and peering into the corridor. - No, no one is visible...

She returned...

Then it seemed to Volodya that the room, Nyuta, the dawn and he himself - everything merged into one sensation of sharp, extraordinary, unprecedented happiness, for which one could give one's whole life and go to eternal torment, but half a minute passed, and all this suddenly disappeared. Volodya saw only a full, unattractive face, distorted by an expression of loathing, and he himself suddenly felt disgust for what had happened.

- However, I must go, - said Nyuta, looking at Volodya with distaste. - How ugly, pathetic... fie, an ugly duckling!

How hideous her long hair, the spacious blouse, her steps, her voice now seemed to Volodya!..

"An ugly duckling... - he thought after she had left. - Indeed I am hideous... Everything is hideous".

Outside the sun was already rising, birds sang loudly; one could hear how the gardener stepped in the garden and how his wheelbarrow creaked... And a little later the lowing of cows and the sounds of a shepherd's pipe were heard. The sunlight and the sounds said that somewhere in this world there is a life pure, refined, poetic. But where is it? Neither maman nor all those people who surrounded him ever spoke to Volodya about it.

When the footman woke him for the morning train, he pretended to be sleeping...

"To hell with it, to hell with everything!" - he thought.

He got out of bed during the eleventh hour. Combing his hair before the mirror and looking at his unattractive face, pale from the sleepless night, he thought:

"Quite true... An ugly duckling".

When maman saw him and was horrified that he was not at the exam, Volodya said:

- I overslept, maman... But do not worry, I will present a medical certificate.

M-me Shumikhina and Nyuta woke up during the first hour. Volodya heard how the awakened m-me Shumikhina opened the window in her room with a clang, how Nyuta replied to her coarse voice with a peal of laughter. He saw,how the door opened and a procession of nieces and hangers-on (among whom was maman) stretched from the living room toward breakfast, how the washed, laughing face of Nyuta flashed by, and next to her face the black eyebrows and beard of the architect who had just arrived. 

     Nyuta was in a Little Russian costume, which did not suit her at all and made her clumsy; the architect cracked jokes that were vulgar and flat; in the cutlets served for breakfast, there was too much onion - or so it seemed to Volodya. It also seemed to him that Nyuta laughed loudly on purpose and kept glancing in his direction to let him know that the memory of the night did not bother her in the least and that she did not notice the presence of the ugly duckling at the table. 

     At the fourth hour Volodya was traveling with maman to the station. Filthy memories, a sleepless night, the impending expulsion from the gymnasium, pangs of conscience - all this now stirred a heavy, gloomy malice in him. He looked at the thin profile of maman, at her small nose, at the waterproof cloak given to her by Nyuta, and muttered: 

     - Why do you powder your face? It is not becoming at your age! You put on beauty, you do not pay your gambling debts, you smoke other people's tobacco... it is disgusting! I do not love you... I do not love you! 

     He was insulting her, and she moved her eyes in fright, clasped her hands and whispered in horror: 

     - What are you saying, my friend? My God, the coachman will hear! Be quiet, or the coachman will hear! He hears everything! 

     - I do not love you... I do not love you! - he continued, gasping for breath. - You are immoral, soulless... Do not dare to wear that waterproof! Do you hear? Or I will tear it to shreds... 

     - Think of what you are saying, my child! - maman began to cry. - The coachman will hear! 

     - And where is my father's fortune? Where is your money? You squandered everything! I am not ashamed of my poverty, but I am ashamed that I have such a mother... When my comrades ask about you, I always blush. 

     On the train they had to travel two stations to the city. All the while Volodya stood on the platform and trembled with his whole body. He did not want to go into the carriage because his mother, whom he hated, was sitting there. He hated himself, the conductors, the smoke from the locomotive, the cold, to which he attributed his shivering... And the heavier his soul became, the more strongly he felt that somewhere in this world, among some people, there is a life pure, noble, warm, elegant, full of love, affection, gaiety, freedom... He felt this and yearned so strongly that even one passenger, looking intently into his face, asked: 

     - You probably have a toothache? 

     In the city maman and Volodya lived with Marya Petrovna, a noblewoman who rented a large apartment and sublet it to lodgers. Maman rented two rooms: in one, with windows, where her bed stood and two pictures in gold frames hung on the walls, she lived herself, and in the other, adjacent, small and dark, lived Volodya. There was a sofa here on which he slept, and besides this sofa there was no other furniture; the whole room was occupied by wicker baskets of clothes, hat boxes and all sorts of junk that maman kept for some reason. Volodya prepared his lessons in his mother's room or in the "common" room - as the large room was called where all the lodgers gathered during dinner and in the evenings. 

     Returning home, he lay down on the sofa and covered himself with a blanket to stop the shivering. The hat boxes, the baskets and the junk reminded him that he had no room of his own, no refuge where he could hide from maman, from her guests and from the voices that now drifted from the "common" room; the satchel and books scattered in the corners reminded him of the exam he had missed... For some reason, quite out of place, Menton came to his memory, where he lived with his late father when he was seven years old; he remembered Biarritz and two English girls with whom he ran on the sand... He wanted to revive in his memory the color of the sky and the ocean, the height of the waves and his mood then, but he did not succeed; the English girls flashed through his imagination as if alive, but everything else mixed together and blurred out of order... 

     "No, it is cold here", - Volodya thought, got up, put on his overcoat and went to the "common" room. 

     In the "common" room they were drinking tea.Three people were sitting at the samovar: maman, the music teacher, an old lady in tortoiseshell pince-nez, and Avgustin Mihalych, an elderly, very fat Frenchman who worked at a perfume factory. 

     - I haven't had dinner today, - maman was saying. - We should send the maid for bread. 

     - Dunyash! - the Frenchman shouted. 

     It turned out that the mistress of the house had sent the maid away somewhere. 

     - Oh, that means nothing, - said the Frenchman, smiling broadly. - I will go for the bread myself right now. Oh, it is nothing! 

     He placed his strong, smelly cigar in a prominent place, put on his hat, and went out. After his departure, maman began telling the music teacher about how she had stayed with the Shumihins and how well she had been received there. 

     - After all, Lili Shumihina is my relative... - she was saying. - Her late husband, General Shumihin, is my husband's cousin. And she herself was born Baroness Kolb... 

     - Maman, that is not true! - Volodya said irritably. - Why lie? 

     He knew perfectly well that maman was telling the truth; in her story about General Shumihin and the born Baroness Kolb there was not a single word of a lie, but nevertheless he still felt that she was lying. The lie was felt in her manner of speaking, in the expression of her face, in her gaze, in everything. 

     - You are lying! - Volodya repeated and struck the table with his fist with such force that all the dishes trembled and maman's tea spilled. - Why do you tell stories about generals and baronesses? All of this is a lie! 

     The music teacher was at a loss and coughed into her handkerchief, pretending she had choked, and maman began to cry. 

     "Where to go?" - Volodya thought. 

     He had already been out on the street; he was ashamed to go to his comrades. Again, the two English girls inappropriately came to his mind... He walked from corner to corner through the "common" room and entered Avgustin Mihalych's room. Here it smelled strongly of essential oils and glycerin soap. On the table, on the windows, and even on the chairs stood a multitude of flasks, small glasses, and wine glasses with multicolored liquids. Volodya took a newspaper from the table, unfolded it, and read the title: "Figaro"... The newspaper emitted a certain strong and pleasant scent. Then he took a revolver from the table... 

     - Enough, pay no attention! - the music teacher was consoling maman in the next room. - He is still so young! At his age, young people always permit themselves too much. One must resign oneself to this. 

     - No, Evgenya Andreevna, he is too spoiled! - maman was saying in a sing-song voice. - There is no elder over him, and I am weak and can do nothing. No, I am miserable! 

     Volodya put the muzzle of the revolver in his mouth, felt for something resembling a trigger or a catch and pressed with his finger... Then he felt for another protrusion and pressed again. Taking the muzzle out of his mouth, he wiped it against the hem of his greatcoat and examined the lock; he had never in his life held a weapon in his hands before... 

     - It seems this needs to be cocked... - he reflected. - Yes, it seems... 

     Avgustin Mihalych entered the "common" room and, laughing, began to tell a story about something. Volodya again put the muzzle in his mouth, gripped it with his teeth, and pressed something with his finger. A shot rang out... Something struck Volodya in the back of the head with terrible force, and he fell onto the table, face first into the wine glasses and flasks. Then he saw how his late father in a top hat with a wide black band, who had worn mourning in Mentone for some lady, suddenly seized him with both hands and they both flew into a very dark, deep abyss. 

     Then everything became confused and disappeared...

When in the provincial town of S. visitors complained about the boredom and monotony of life, the local residents, as if justifying themselves, said that, on the contrary, it is very good in S., that there is a library, a theater, a club in S., there are balls, and that, finally, there are intelligent, interesting, pleasant families with whom one can make acquaintances. And they pointed to the Turkin family as the most educated and talented.

     This family lived on the main street, near the governor, in their own house. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a stout, handsome brunette with sideburns, organized amateur performances for charitable purposes, played old generals himself and at the same time coughed very funnily. He knew many anecdotes, charades, sayings, loved to joke and be witty, and he always had such an expression that it was impossible to understand whether he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a thin, pretty lady in a pince#nez, wrote novellas and novels and willingly read them aloud to her guests. The daughter, Ekaterina Ivanovna, a young girl, played the piano. In a word, every member of the family had some talent of their own. The Turkins received guests cordially and showed them their talents cheerfully, with hearty simplicity. In their large stone house, it was spacious and cool in summer, half of the windows looked out into an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests sat in the house, knives clattered in the kitchen, the smell of fried onions wafted in the yard – and every time this foreshadowed a bountiful and delicious dinner.

     And to Dr. Startsev, Dmitry Ionych, when he had just been appointed as a zemstvo doctor and settled in Dyalizh, nine versts from S., it was also said that he, as an intelligent person, must get acquainted with the Turkins. One winter on the street he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich; they talked about the weather, the theater, cholera, and an invitation followed. In the spring, on a holiday – it was Ascension, – after seeing patients, Startsev went to town to amuse himself a little and, by the way, buy some things for himself. He walked on foot, without rushing (he did not have his own horses yet), and all the while kept singing: When I had not yet drunk tears from the cup of existence…

     

     In town he had dinner, walked in the garden, then somehow the invitation from Ivan Petrovich came to his mind of its own accord, and he decided to go to the Turkins, to see what kind of people they were.

     – Hello please, – said Ivan Petrovich, meeting him on the porch. – Very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Come, I will introduce you to my better half. I am telling him, Verochka, – he continued, introducing the doctor to his wife, – I am telling him that he has no Roman right to sit in his hospital, he must give his leisure to society. Isn't that right, darling?

     – Sit here, – said Vera Iosifovna, seating the guest next to her. – You can court me. My husband is jealous, he is an Othello, but we will try to behave in such a way that he notices nothing.

     – Oh you, chick, naughty one… – Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. – You have arrived very opportunely, – he addressed the guest again, – my better half has written a grand novel and will read it aloud today.

     – Jean, – Vera Iosifovna said to her husband, – dites que l'on nous donne du the1.

     Ekaterina Ivanovna was introduced to Startsev, an eighteen-year-old girl, very much like her mother, just as thin and pretty. Her expression was still childlike and her waist slender, delicate; and her virginal, already developed chest, beautiful and healthy, spoke of spring, real spring. Then they drank tea with jam, with honey, with candy and with very tasty cookies that melted in the mouth. With the onset of evening, guests gathered little by little, and to each of them Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes and said:

     – Hello please.

     Then everyone sat in the living room, with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began like this: "The frost was strengthening…" The windows were wide open, one could hear knives clattering in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onions reached them… In the soft, deep armchairs it was peaceful, the lights blinked so kindly in the twilight of the living room; and now, on a summer evening,when voices, laughter floated in from the street and the scent of lilac wafted from the yard, it was hard to understand how the frost was growing harder and how the setting sun illuminated with its cold rays the snowy plain and the traveler walking lonely along the road; Vera Iosifovna read about how a young, beautiful countess established schools, hospitals, libraries in her village and how she fell in love with a wandering artist, – she read about things that never happen in life, and yet it was pleasant and comfortable to listen, and all sorts of good, peaceful thoughts came to mind, – one did not want to get up.

     – Not bad… – Ivan Petrovich said quietly.

     And one of the guests, listening and drifting away in thought somewhere very, very far, said barely audibly:

     – Yes… indeed…

     An hour passed, then another. In the city garden nearby, an orchestra played and a choir of folksingers sang. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, they remained silent for about 5 minutes, listening to "Luchinushka" sung by the choir, and this song conveyed what was not in the novel and what does happen in life.

     – Do you publish your works in magazines? – Startsev asked Vera Iosifovna.

     – No, – she replied, – I do not publish anywhere. I write and hide it in my cupboard. Why publish? – she explained. – After all, we have the means.

     And everyone for some reason sighed.

     – And now you, Kotik, play something, – Ivan Petrovich said to his daughter.

     They raised the lid of the piano, opened the sheet music that was already lying ready. Ekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands; and then immediately struck again with all her might, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest quivered, she stubbornly kept striking the same spot, and it seemed she would not stop until she had hammered the keys inside the piano. The living room filled with thunder; everything thundered: the floor, the ceiling, the furniture… Ekaterina Ivanovna played a difficult passage, interesting precisely for its difficulty, long and monotonous, and Startsev, listening, imagined stones tumbling down from a high mountain, tumbling and tumbling on and on, and he wanted them to stop tumbling sooner, and at the same time Ekaterina Ivanovna, pink from the effort, strong, energetic, with a lock of hair fallen on her forehead, pleased him very much. After a winter spent in Dyalizh, among the sick and the peasants, to sit in a living room, to look at this young, elegant and, probably, pure creature and to listen to these noisy, annoying, but still cultured sounds, – was so pleasant, so new…

     – Well, Kotik, today you played like never before, – Ivan Petrovich said with tears in his eyes when his daughter finished and stood up. – Die, Denis, you couldn't write it better.

     Everyone surrounded her, congratulated her, marveled, assured her that they had not heard such music for a long time, and she listened in silence, slightly smiling, and triumph was written all over her figure.

     – Splendid! Excellent!

     – Splendid! – Startsev also said, yielding to the general enthusiasm. – Where did you study music? – he asked Ekaterina Ivanovna. – At the conservatory?

     – No, I am only just planning to go to the conservatory, but for now I studied here, with Madame Zavlovskaya.

     – Did you finish the course at the local gymnasium?

     – Oh no! – Vera Iosifovna answered for her. – We invited teachers to the house, for in a gymnasium or in an institute, you must agree, there could be bad influences; while a girl is growing up, she should be under the influence of her mother alone.

     – And yet I will go to the conservatory, – said Ekaterina Ivanovna.

     – No, Kotik loves her mama. Kotik will not upset papa and mama.

     – No, I will go! I will go! – Ekaterina Ivanovna said, joking and being capricious, and stamped her foot.

     And at supper, Ivan Petrovich was already showing his talents. Laughing with his eyes alone, he told anecdotes, made witticisms, proposed funny riddles and solved them himself and all the time spoke in his extraordinary language, developed by long practice in wit and, evidently, having long since become a habit for him: most-excellent, not bad, most-humbly thank you…

     But that was not all. When the guests, full and satisfied, crowded in the hall, picking out their coats and canes, the footman Pavlusha busied himself near them, or,as he was called here, Pava, a boy of about fourteen, with a close-cropped head and full cheeks.

     – Now then, Pava, perform! – Ivan Petrovich said to him.

     Pava struck a pose, raised a hand, and spoke in a tragic tone:

     – Die, unhappy woman!

     And everyone burst out laughing.

     "Amusing," – thought Startsev, going out into the street.

     He stopped by a restaurant and drank some beer, then set off on foot to his home in Dyalizh. He walked and sang all the way: Your voice for me, both tender and languid…

     Having walked nine versts and then lying down to sleep, he did not feel the slightest fatigue, but on the contrary, it seemed to him that he would have gladly walked another twenty versts.

     "Not bad…" – he remembered, falling asleep, and laughed.

II

     Startsev kept meaning to visit the Turkins, but there was a great deal of work at the hospital, and he could not find a free hour. More than a year passed in this way in labor and solitude; but then a letter in a blue envelope was brought from the town…

     Vera Iosifovna had long suffered from migraines, but lately, when Kotik frightened her every day with threats of leaving for the conservatory, the attacks began to repeat more often. All the town doctors had been to the Turkins; finally, it was the zemstvo doctor's turn. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter in which she asked him to come and ease her suffering. Startsev came and after that began to visit the Turkins often, very often… He did indeed help Vera Iosifovna a little, and she already told all the guests that he was an extraordinary, wonderful doctor. But he no longer went to the Turkins for the sake of her migraine…

     A holiday. Ekaterina Ivanovna finished her long, tedious exercises on the piano. Then they sat for a long time in the dining room drinking tea, and Ivan Petrovich told something funny. But then a bell rang; it was necessary to go to the front hall to meet some guest; Startsev took advantage of the moment of confusion and said to Ekaterina Ivanovna in a whisper, greatly agitated:

     – For god's sake, I implore you, do not torture me, let us go into the garden!

     She shrugged her shoulders as if puzzled and not understanding what he wanted from her, but she stood up and went.

     – You play the piano for three, four hours, – he said, following her, – then you sit with your mother, and there is no possibility of talking to you. Give me at least a quarter of an hour, I implore you.

     Autumn was approaching, and in the old garden it was quiet, sad and dark leaves lay on the paths. It grew dark early.

     – I have not seen you for a whole week, – continued Startsev, – and if you only knew what suffering it is! Let us sit down. Listen to me.

     Both had a favorite spot in the garden: a bench under an old wide maple. And now they sat on this bench.

     – What do you wish? – Ekaterina Ivanovna asked dryly, in a businesslike tone.

     – I have not seen you for a whole week, I have not heard you for so long. I passionately want, I thirst for your voice. Speak.

     She delighted him with her freshness, the naive expression of her eyes and cheeks. Even in the way her dress sat on her, he saw something extraordinarily sweet, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. And at the same time, despite this naivety, she seemed to him very intelligent and developed beyond her years. With her he could talk about literature, about art, about anything, could complain to her about life, about people, although during a serious conversation, it happened, she would suddenly start laughing inappropriately or run away into the house. She, like almost all girls in S., read a lot (in general in S. people read very little, and in the local library they said that if it were not for the girls and young Jews, the library might as well be closed); this pleased Startsev infinitely, he asked her with excitement every time what she had been reading in recent days, and, enchanted, listened when she told him.

     – What did you read this week while we did not see each other? – he asked now. – Speak, I beg you.

     – I read Pisemsky.

     – What exactly?

     – "A Thousand Souls", – replied Kotik. – And what a funny name Pisemsky had: Alexei Feofilaktych!

     – Where are you going? – Startsev was horrified when she suddenly stood up and went toward the house. – I must speak with you,I must explain myself... Stay with me for at least five minutes! I implore you!

     She stopped, as if wanting to say something, then awkwardly thrust a note into his hand and ran into the house, and there sat down at the piano again.

     "Tonight, at eleven o'clock," Startsev read, "be at the cemetery by the Demetti monument."

     "Well, that is not clever at all," he thought, coming to his senses. "What has the cemetery to do with it? For what purpose?"

     It was clear: Kotik was playing a prank. Who, indeed, would seriously think of scheduling a tryst at night, far out of town, in a cemetery, when it could easily be arranged on the street, in the town garden? And was it becoming of him, a district doctor, a smart, solid person, to sigh, receive little notes, drag himself to cemeteries, and do foolish things that even high schoolers laugh at now? Where would this romance lead? What would his colleagues say when they found out? So thought Startsev, wandering near the tables at the club, but at half past ten he suddenly went and drove to the cemetery.

     He already had his own pair of horses and a coachman, Panteleimon, in a velvet waistcoat. The moon was shining. It was quiet, warm, but autumnally warm. In the outskirts, near the slaughterhouses, dogs were howling. Startsev left the horses at the edge of town, in one of the alleys, and went to the cemetery on foot. "Everyone has their whims," he thought. "Kotik is also strange and - who knows? - perhaps she is not joking, she will come," - and he surrendered to this weak, empty hope, and it intoxicated him.

     He walked for about half a verst through a field. The cemetery appeared in the distance as a dark strip, like a forest or a large garden. A white stone fence appeared, gates... In the moonlight one could read on the gates: "The hour is coming in which..." Startsev entered through the wicket, and the first thing he saw were white crosses and monuments on both sides of a wide avenue and black shadows from them and from the poplars; and all around, white and black were visible far away, and sleepy trees bowed their branches over the white. It seemed that it was brighter here than in the field; the maple leaves, looking like paws, stood out sharply on the yellow sand of the avenues and on the slabs, and the inscriptions on the monuments were clear. At first Startsev was struck by what he saw now for the first time in his life and what, probably, he would never happen to see again: a world unlike anything else, - a world where the moonlight is so good and soft, as if its cradle were here, where there is no life, none at all, but in every dark poplar, in every grave, one feels the presence of a mystery promising a quiet, beautiful, eternal life. From the slabs and withered flowers, together with the autumn scent of leaves, wafts forgiveness, sadness, and peace.

     Silence was all around; in deep humility, stars looked down from the sky, and Startsev's footsteps sounded so sharp and out of place. And only when the clock in the church began to strike and he imagined himself dead, buried here forever, it seemed to him that someone was looking at him, and for a moment he thought that this was not peace or silence, but the dull anguish of non-existence, a suppressed despair...

     The Demetti monument was in the form of a chapel, with an angel on top; once an Italian opera troupe was passing through S., one of the singers died, she was buried and this monument was erected. No one in town remembered her anymore, but the icon lamp over the entrance reflected the moonlight and seemed to be burning.

     There was no one. And who would go here at midnight? But Startsev waited, and, as if the moonlight were heating the passion in him, he waited passionately and pictured kisses and embraces in his imagination. He sat near the monument for about half an hour, then walked along the side avenues, hat in hand, waiting and thinking about how many women and girls were buried here in these graves, who were beautiful, charming, who loved, burned with passion at night, surrendering to caresses. How poorly Mother Nature essentially jokes with a human being, how insulting it is to realize this! Startsev thought so, and at the same time he wanted to shout that he wants, that he waits for love at any cost; before him, no longer pieces of marble, but beautiful bodies turned white, he saw forms that shyly hid in the shadows of the trees, felt warmth, and this longing became painful...

     And as if a curtain had fallen, the moon went behind the clouds,And suddenly everything around grew dark. Startsev barely found the gates, - it was already as dark as an autumn night, - then wandered for about an hour and a half, searching for the side street where he had left his horses.

     - I am tired, I can hardly stay on my feet, - he said to Panteleimon.

     And, sitting down with pleasure in the carriage, he thought:

     "Oh, I really should not be getting fat!"

III

     The next day in the evening he went to the Turkins to propose. But this proved inconvenient, as a hairdresser was doing Ekaterina Ivanovna's hair in her room. She was preparing for a dance evening at the club.

     He had to sit in the dining room for a long time again and drink tea. Ivan Petrovich, seeing that the guest was pensive and bored, took out some notes from his waistcoat pocket, read a funny letter from a German manager about how in the estate all the "lock-ups" had spoiled and the "shyness" had collapsed.

     "And they will probably give quite a dowry," thought Startsev, listening absent-mindedly.

     After a sleepless night he was in a state of daze, as if he had been drugged with something sweet and soporific; it was foggy in his soul, but joyful, warm, and at the same time some cold, heavy little piece in his head reasoned:

     "Stop before it is too late! Is she a match for you? She is spoiled, capricious, sleeps until two o'clock, and you are a sexton's son, a district doctor..."

     "Well, so what?" he thought. "Let her be."

     "Besides, if you marry her," the little piece continued, "then her relatives will force you to quit the district service and live in the city."

     "Well, so what?" he thought. "In the city, then in the city. They will give a dowry, we will set up a home..."

     Finally Ekaterina Ivanovna entered in a ball gown, low-cut, pretty, clean, and Startsev was so admired and came into such delight that he could not utter a single word, but only looked at her and laughed.

     She began to say goodbye, and he - there was no reason for him to stay here anymore - got up, saying that it was time for him to go home: patients were waiting.

     - It cannot be helped, - said Ivan Petrovich, - go then, and by the way, give Kotik a lift to the club.

     In the yard it was drizzling, it was very dark, and only by Panteleimon's hoarse cough could one guess where the horses were. They raised the top of the carriage.

     - I walk on the carpet, you walk while you lie, - said Ivan Petrovich, seating his daughter in the carriage, - he walks while he lies... Move on! Goodbye please!

     They drove off.

     - And I was at the cemetery yesterday, - Startsev began. - How ungenerous and unmerciful of you...

     - You were at the cemetery?

     - Yes, I was there and waited for you almost until two o'clock. I suffered...

     - And suffer on, if you do not understand jokes.

     Ekaterina Ivanovna, pleased that she had played such a clever trick on the man in love and that she was loved so much, burst out laughing and suddenly screamed in fright, as at that very moment the horses were turning sharply into the club gates and the carriage tilted. Startsev put his arm around Ekaterina Ivanovna's waist; she, frightened, pressed against him, and he could not restrain himself and passionately kissed her on the lips, on the chin and hugged her tighter.

     - Enough, - she said dryly.

     And in a moment she was no longer in the carriage, and the policeman near the illuminated entrance of the club shouted in a disgusting voice at Panteleimon:

     - Why have you stopped, you crow? Move along!

     Startsev went home, but soon returned. Dressed in a borrowed tailcoat and a stiff white tie, which somehow kept bristling and wanting to slide off the collar, he sat at midnight in the club living room and spoke to Ekaterina Ivanovna with passion:

     - Oh, how little they know who have never loved! It seems to me no one has yet described love correctly, and one can hardly describe this tender, joyful, agonizing feeling, and whoever has experienced it at least once will not try to convey it in words. Why prefaces, descriptions? Why unnecessary eloquence? My love is boundless... I beg, I implore you, - Startsev finally uttered, - be my wife!

     - Dmitry Ionych, - said Ekaterina Ivanovna with a very serious expression, after thinking. - Dmitry Ionych, I am very grateful to you for the honor, I respect you, but...

she stood up and continued while standing, – but, excuse me, I cannot be your wife. Let us speak seriously. Dmitry Ionych, you know that more than anything in life I love art, I madly love, I adore music, I have dedicated my whole life to it. I want to be an artist, I want fame, success, freedom, and you want me to continue living in this town, to continue this empty, useless life that has become unbearable to me. To become a wife – oh no, forgive me! A person must strive for a higher, brilliant goal, and family life would bind me forever. Dmitry Ionych (she smiled slightly, as, having pronounced "Dmitry Ionych", she remembered "Alexei Feofilaktych"), Dmitry Ionych, you are a kind, noble, intelligent person, you are better than anyone... – tears welled up in her eyes, – I feel for you with all my soul, but... but you will understand...

     And, so as not to start crying, she turned away and left the drawing room.

     Startsev's heart stopped beating restlessly. Leaving the club for the street, he first of all tore off his stiff tie and took a full breath. He felt a little ashamed and his pride was wounded, – he had not expected a refusal, – and he could not believe that all his dreams, yearnings, and hopes had led him to such a foolish end, just like in a little play at an amateur performance. And he felt sorry for his feelings, for this love of his, so sorry that, it seemed, he could have broken into sobs or hit Panteleimon on his broad back with his umbrella with all his might.

     For about three days work fell from his hands, he did not eat, did not sleep, but when a rumor reached him that Ekaterina Ivanovna had left for Moscow to enter the conservatory, he calmed down and began living as before.

     Later, sometimes remembering how he had wandered through the cemetery or how he had traveled all over the town searching for a frock coat, he would stretch lazily and say:

     – What a lot of trouble, though!

IV

     Four years passed. Startsev already had a large practice in town. Every morning he hurriedly received patients at his place in Dyalizh, then left for the town patients, leaving no longer with a pair, but with a troika with bells, and returned home late at night. He had grown stout, heavy, and walked on foot reluctantly, as he suffered from shortness of breath. And Panteleimon had also grown stout, and the more he grew in width, the more sadly he sighed and complained about his bitter lot: the driving had overcome him!

     Startsev visited various houses and met many people, but did not become close with anyone. The townspeople with their conversations, views on life, and even their appearance irritated him. Experience taught him little by little that as long as you play cards with a townsman or have a snack with him, he is a peaceful, good-natured, and even not stupid person, but one only has to start talking with him about something inedible, for example, about politics or science, as he is at a loss or starts such philosophy, dull and mean, that one can only wave a hand and walk away. When Startsev tried to talk even with a liberal townsman, for example, about how humanity, thank god, is moving forward and that in time it will get along without passports and without the death penalty, the townsman looked at him sideways and distrustfully and asked: "So, then anyone can cut anyone they like on the street?" And when Startsev in company, at dinner or tea, talked about the fact that one must work, that one cannot live without work, everyone took this as a reproach and began to get angry and argue intrusively. Despite all that, the townspeople did nothing, absolutely nothing, and were interested in nothing, and it was impossible to think of what to talk about with them. And Startsev avoided conversations, but only had snacks and played vint, and when he found a family celebration in some house and was invited to dine, he sat and ate in silence, looking into his plate; and everything that was said at that time was uninteresting, unfair, stupid, he felt irritation, became agitated, but remained silent, and because he was always sternly silent and looked into his plate, he was nicknamed in town "the stuck-up Pole", although he was never a Pole.

     From such entertainments as theater and concerts, he refrained, but on the other hand he played vint every evening, for about three hours, with enjoyment. He had one more entertainment,which he had drawn into imperceptibly, little by little, was this – in the evenings to take out of his pockets the banknotes gained from practice, and, it happened, the banknotes – yellow and green, which smelled of perfume, and vinegar, and incense, and fish oil, – were stuffed into all his pockets to the amount of about seventy rubles; and when several hundred had accumulated, he took them to the Mutual Credit Society and placed them there in a current account.

     In all four years since the departure of Ekaterina Ivanovna, he had been to the Turkins' only twice, at the invitation of Vera Iosifovna, who was still being treated for migraines. Every summer Ekaterina Ivanovna came to stay with her parents, but he did not see her even once; somehow it never happened.

     But now four years had passed. On one quiet, warm morning, a letter was brought to the hospital. Vera Iosifovna wrote to Dmitry Ionych that she missed him very much, and asked him to certainly pay her a visit and ease her suffering, and by the way, today was her birthday. At the bottom was a postscript: "I join in mama's request. K."

     Startsev thought it over and in the evening went to the Turkins'.

     – Ah, hello, if you please! – Ivan Petrovich met him, smiling only with his eyes. – Bonjour-te.

     Vera Iosifovna, already significantly aged, with white hair, shook Startsev's hand, sighed affectedly, and said:

     – You, doctor, do not want to look after me, you never visit us, I am already old for you. But now a young one has arrived, perhaps she will be luckier.

     And Kotik? She had grown thinner, paler, became more beautiful and slender; but this was already Ekaterina Ivanovna, and not Kotik; there was no longer the former freshness and expression of childish naivety. Both in her look and in her manners there was something new – timid and guilty, as if here, in the Turkins' house, she no longer felt at home.

     – How many years, how many winters! – she said, giving Startsev her hand, and it was clear that her heart was beating anxiously; and looking intently, with curiosity into his face, she continued: – How stout you have grown! You are tanned, matured, but on the whole you have changed little.

     And now she pleased him, pleased him very much, but something was already lacking in her, or something was extra, – he himself could not have said exactly what, but something already prevented him from feeling as before. He did not like her paleness, the new expression, the weak smile, the voice, and a little later he already did not like her dress, the armchair in which she sat, he did not like something in the past, when he had almost married her. He remembered his love, the dreams and hopes that had excited him four years ago, – and he felt awkward.

     They drank tea with sweet pie. Then Vera Iosifovna read a novel aloud, read about things that never happen in life, and Startsev listened, looked at her gray, beautiful head and waited for her to finish.

     "Talentless," – he thought, – "is not the one who does not know how to write stories, but the one who writes them and does not know how to hide it."

     – Not bad, – said Ivan Petrovich. Then Ekaterina Ivanovna played the piano loudly and for long, and, when she finished, she was thanked for a long time and admired.

     "And it is good that I did not marry her," – thought Startsev.

     She looked at him and, apparently, waited for him to suggest going into the garden, but he remained silent.

     – Let us talk then, – she said, approaching him. – How do you live? What is new with you? How? I have been thinking about you all these days, – she continued nervously, – I wanted to send you a letter, wanted to go to you in Dyalizh myself, and I had already decided to go, but then changed my mind, – god knows how you feel toward me now. I awaited you today with such excitement. For god's sake, let us go into the garden. They went into the garden and sat there on a bench under the old maple, as four years ago. It was dark.

     – So how are you getting along? – Ekaterina Ivanovna asked.

     – All right, we live little by little, – Startsev replied.

     And he could think of nothing more. They were silent for a while.

     – I am agitated, – said Ekaterina Ivanovna and covered her face with her hands, – but you do not pay attention. I feel so good at home, I am so glad to see everyone and cannot get used to it. So many memories! It seemed to me that we would talk with you without ceasing, until morning.Now he saw her face close up, her shining eyes, and here, in the darkness, she seemed younger than in the room, and it was as if her former childlike expression had even returned to her. And indeed, she looked at him with naive curiosity, as if she wanted to take a closer look and understand the person who had once loved her so passionately, with such tenderness and so unhappily; her eyes thanked him for that love. And he remembered everything that had happened, all the slightest details, how he wandered through the cemetery, how later toward morning, exhausted, he returned home, and he suddenly felt sad and sorry for the past. A small flame flickered in his soul.

– And do you remember how I walked you to the evening at the club? – he said. – It was raining then, it was dark…

The flame kept growing in his soul, and he already wanted to talk, to complain about life…

– Eh! – he said with a sigh. – You are asking how I am doing. How do we live here? Not at all. We grow old, get fat, let ourselves go. Day and night – twenty-four hours are gone, life passes dully, without impressions, without thoughts… Profit during the day, and in the evening the club, a society of card players, alcoholics, wheezers, whom I cannot stand. What is good about it?

– But you have work, a noble goal in life. You used to love talking about your hospital so much. I was some kind of strange person then, I imagined myself a great pianist. Nowadays all young ladies play the piano, and I also played like everyone else, and there was nothing special about me; I am the same kind of pianist as mama is a writer. And of course, I did not understand you then, but later, in Moscow, I often thought of you. I thought only of you. What a happiness it is to be a zemstvo doctor, to help sufferers, to serve the people. What happiness! – Ekaterina Ivanovna repeated with enthusiasm. – When I thought of you in Moscow, you appeared to me so ideal, so elevated…

Startsev remembered the banknotes which he took out of his pockets in the evenings with such pleasure, and the flame in his soul went out.

He stood up to go to the house. She took him by the arm.

– You are the best of the people I have known in my life, – she continued. – We will see each other, talk, won't we? Promise me. I am not a pianist, I no longer delude myself on my own account and will neither play nor talk about music in your presence.

When they entered the house and Startsev saw her face in the evening light and her sad, grateful, searching eyes turned toward him, he felt uneasy and thought again:

"It is a good thing I did not marry then."

He began to say goodbye.

– You have no Roman right to leave without supper, – Ivan Petrovich said, seeing him off. – This is quite perpendicular on your part. Come on, perform! – he said, turning to Pava in the hallway.

Pava, no longer a boy but a young man with a mustache, struck a pose, raised his hand and said in a tragic voice:

– Die, unhappy woman!

All this irritated Startsev. Getting into the carriage and looking at the dark house and garden, which were once so dear and precious to him, he remembered everything at once – Vera Iosifovna's novels, and Kotik's noisy playing, and Ivan Petrovich's wit, and Pava's tragic pose, and thought that if the most talented people in the whole city are so untalented, then what must the city itself be like.

Three days later Pava brought a letter from Ekaterina Ivanovna.

"You are not coming to see us. Why? – she wrote. – I am afraid that you have changed toward us; I am afraid, and the very thought of it terrifies me. Reassure me, come and say that everything is fine.

I must talk to you. Yours E. T."

He read this letter, thought for a while, and said to Pava:

– Tell them, my dear fellow, that I cannot come today, I am very busy. I will come, say, in about three days.

But three days passed, a week passed, and he still did not go. Once, driving past the Turkins' house, he remembered that he should stop by at least for a minute, but he thought it over and… did not stop by.

And he never visited the Turkins again.

V

Several more years passed. Startsev has grown even heavier, fatter, breathes heavily and already walks with his head thrown back. When he, puffy and red, drives in a troika with bells and Panteleimon,also plump and red, with a fleshy back of the head, sits on the box, stretching forward straight, wooden-like arms, and shouts to those he meets "Keep to the rrright!", then the picture is impressive, and it seems that not a human but a pagan god is driving. He has a massive practice in the city, no time to breathe, and already owns an estate and two houses in the city, and he is picking out a third, more profitable one, and when they tell him at the Mutual Credit Society about some house scheduled for auction, he goes into that house without ceremony and, passing through all the rooms, paying no attention to the undressed women and children who look at him with amazement and fear, pokes at all the doors with his cane and says:

     – Is this the study? Is this the bedroom? And what is here?

     And at the same time he breathes heavily and wipes the sweat from his forehead.

     He has many troubles, but still he does not give up his zemstvo post; greed has overcome him, he wants to make it both here and there. In Dyalizh and in the city he is already called simply Ionych. – "Where is Ionych going?" or: "Shouldn't we invite Ionych for a consultation?"

     Probably because his throat is overgrown with fat, his voice has changed, becoming thin and sharp. His character has also changed: he has become heavy, irritable. When receiving patients, he usually gets angry, impatiently taps his cane on the floor and shouts in his unpleasant voice:

     – Please answer only the questions! No talking!

     He is lonely. He lives boringly, nothing interests him.

     In all the time he has lived in Dyalizh, his love for Kotik was his only joy and, probably, his last. In the evenings he plays vint at the club and then sits alone at a large table and has supper. He is served by the waiter Ivan, the oldest and most respected, they serve him Lafite № 17, and already everyone – the club directors, the cook, and the waiter – knows what he likes and what he does not like, they try with all their might to please him, otherwise, god forbid, he might suddenly get angry and start tapping his cane on the floor.

     While having supper, he occasionally turns around and interferes in some conversation:

     – What are you talking about? Eh? Who?

     And when, it happens, at a neighboring table a conversation starts about the Turkins, he asks:

     – Which Turkins are you talking about? The ones where the daughter plays the piano?

     That is all that can be said about him.

     And the Turkins? Ivan Petrovich has not aged, has not changed at all and as before is always joking and telling anecdotes; Vera Iosifovna reads her novels to guests as before willingly, with hearty simplicity. And Kotik plays the piano every day, for about four hours. She has noticeably aged, is often unwell and every autumn leaves with her mother for the Crimea. Seeing them off at the station, Ivan Petrovich, when the train moves, wipes away tears and shouts:

     – Goodbye please!

 

The Upheaval

     Mashenka Pavletskaya, a young woman, having only just finished her course at the institute, returning from a walk to the Pushkins' house, where she lived as a governess, found an extraordinary upheaval. The doorman Mikhailo, who opened for her, was agitated and red as a lobster.

     Noise could be heard from above.

     "Probably the mistress is having a fit… – Mashenka thought, – or she had a quarrel with her husband…"

     In the front hall and in the corridor she met the maids. One maid was crying. Then Mashenka saw the master himself, Nikolai Sergeich, run out of the door of her room, a small, not yet old man with a bloated face and a large bald spot. He was red. He was twitching… Not noticing the governess, he passed by her and, raising his hands up, exclaimed:

     – Oh, how terrible this is! How tactless! How stupid, wild! Vile!

     Mashenka entered her room, and here for the first time in her life she had to experience in all its sharpness the feeling that is so familiar to dependent, defenseless people living on the bread of the rich and high-born. They were searching her room. The mistress Fedosya Vasilyevna, a stout broad-shouldered lady with thick black eyebrows, bare-headed and angular, with barely noticeable mustaches and red hands, in face and manners resembling a common peasant cook, stood at her table and was putting back into a workbag balls of wool, scraps of cloth, papers… Obviously, the appearance of the governess was unexpected for her, as,looking back and seeing her pale, surprised face, she grew slightly embarrassed and muttered:

     – Pardon,1 I… I accidentally spilled… caught it with my sleeve…

     And, having said something else, Madame Kushkina rustled her train and left. Mashenka scanned her room with surprised eyes and, understanding nothing, not knowing what to think, shrugged her shoulders, turning cold with fear… What was Fedosya Vasilyevna looking for in her bag? If indeed, as she says, she accidentally caught it with her sleeve and spilled it, then why did Nikolai Sergeich dash out of the room so red and agitated? Why was one drawer at the table slightly pulled out? The money box, in which the governess hid ten-kopeck pieces and old stamps, was unlocked. It had been opened, but they had not managed to lock it, although they had scratched the entire lock. The bookshelf, the surface of the table, the bed – everything bore fresh traces of a search. And in the laundry basket too. The linen was folded neatly, but not in the order in which Mashenka had left it when leaving the house. A search, then, was real, a most real one, but for what purpose, why? What happened? Mashenka remembered the agitation of the doorman, the commotion that was still going on, the housemaid in tears; did all this not have a connection with the search that had just taken place at her room? Was she not involved in some kind of terrible affair? Mashenka turned pale and, cold all over, sank onto the laundry basket.

     The housemaid entered the room.

     – Liza, do you know why they… searched me? – the governess asked her.

     – The mistress lost a brooch worth two thousand… – said Liza.

     – Yes, but why search me?

     – Everyone, miss, was searched. They searched all of me too… They stripped us all naked and searched us… And I, miss, as if before God… Not only did I not take their brooch, but I didn't even go near the dressing table. I’ll say the same at the police station.

     – But… why search me? – the governess continued to wonder.

     – The brooch, I say, was stolen… The mistress herself rummaged through everything with her own hands. They even searched Mikhailo the doorman themselves. A pure disgrace! Nikolai Sergeich just looks on and cackles like a hen. And you, miss, are trembling for nothing. They found nothing on you. If you didn't take the brooch, then you have nothing to fear.

     – But this, Liza, is base… insulting! – Mashenka said, gasping with indignation. – This is a meanness, a low act! What right did she have to suspect me and rummage through my things?

     – You are living among strangers, miss, – Liza sighed. – Even though you are a lady, you are still… as if a servant… It’s not like living with your papa and mama…
