DEAD SOULS

VOLUME ONE

Chapter One

Through the gates of the inn of the provincial town of NN rolled a rather handsome small spring carriage of the kind favored by bachelors: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners with about a hundred serfs — in short, all those known as gentlemen of middling rank. In the carriage sat a gentleman who was not handsome but not bad-looking either, neither too fat nor too thin; one could not say he was old, yet he was not exactly young either. His arrival caused absolutely no stir in the town and was accompanied by nothing out of the ordinary; only two Russian peasants standing at the door of a tavern across from the inn made a few remarks, which concerned the carriage more than the man sitting in it. "Look at that," said one to the other, "what a wheel! What do you think — would that wheel make it to Moscow if it came to that, or wouldn't it?" "It would," answered the other. "But to Kazan, I reckon, it wouldn't make it?" "It wouldn't make it to Kazan," answered the other. And that was the end of the conversation. Then, as the carriage pulled up to the inn, a young man came by in white canvas trousers, very tight and short, wearing a tailcoat with pretensions to fashion, from beneath which a shirtfront was visible, fastened with a Tula pin bearing a bronze pistol. The young man turned around, looked at the carriage, held his cap with his hand to keep it from flying off in the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage drove into the courtyard, the gentleman was met by the inn's servant, or waiter as they are called in Russian taverns, lively and fidgety to such a degree that it was impossible to make out what his face looked like. He ran out briskly, napkin in hand — tall all over and in a long dimity coat with the back riding nearly up to his neck, tossed his hair, and swiftly led the gentleman up along the wooden gallery to show him the room God had seen fit to send him. The room was of a familiar sort, for the inn was also of a familiar sort — that is, precisely the kind found in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day travelers receive a comfortable room with cockroaches peering out like prunes from every corner, and a door into the adjoining room always blocked by a chest of drawers, behind which lives a neighbor, a quiet and peaceful man but extraordinarily curious, interested in knowing every detail about the traveler. The exterior of the inn matched its interior: it was very long, two stories tall; the lower story was not plastered and remained in dark red brick, made even darker by the harsh changes of weather and grimy enough on its own; the upper story was painted in the eternal yellow paint; below were little shops with horse collars, rope, and ring-shaped rolls. In the corner shop, or rather in its window, sat a vendor of hot mead with a copper samovar as red as his face, so that from a distance one might have thought two samovars were standing in the window, if one of them had not had a beard as black as pitch.

While the arriving gentleman was inspecting his room, his belongings were brought in: first a trunk of white leather, somewhat worn, showing it had been on the road more than once. The trunk was carried in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a sheepskin coat, and the valet Petrushka, a fellow of about thirty in a loose secondhand coat that had clearly come from his master's shoulder, a fellow with a somewhat surly look and very thick lips and nose. After the trunk came a small mahogany box inlaid with Karelian birch veneer, a pair of shoe lasts, and a roasted chicken wrapped in blue paper. When all of this had been brought in, the coachman Selifan went off to the stables to fuss over the horses, while the valet Petrushka began settling into a small anteroom, a very dark little cubbyhole, into which he had already managed to drag his overcoat and along with it some particular smell of his own,which had been reported and brought in along with a bag containing various servants' toiletries. In this little cubbyhole he fitted a narrow three-legged bed against the wall, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, beaten flat as a pancake, and perhaps just as greasy as a pancake, which he had managed to obtain from the innkeeper.

While the servants were busy settling in and fussing about, the gentleman went to the common dining room. What these common dining rooms are like, every traveler knows very well: the same walls painted with oil paint, darkened at the top from pipe smoke and polished smooth at the bottom by the backs of various travelers, and even more so by local merchants, for on market days the merchants would come here in groups of six or seven to drink their customary pair of teas; the same smoke-blackened ceiling; the same smoke-darkened chandelier with a multitude of hanging glass pendants that jumped and jingled every time the waiter ran across the worn oilcloth, briskly swinging a tray on which sat as great a multitude of teacups as birds on a seashore; the same paintings covering the entire walls, painted in oils — in short, everything the same as everywhere else; the only difference being that one painting depicted a nymph with such enormous breasts as the reader has surely never seen. Such a trick of nature, however, does occur in various historical paintings of unknown time, origin, and by whom they were brought to Russia, sometimes even by our noblemen, lovers of the arts, who had bought them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who accompanied them. The gentleman removed his cap and unwound from his neck a woolen scarf of rainbow colors, the kind that wives prepare with their own hands for married men, supplying proper instructions on how to wrap up, while for bachelors — I cannot say for certain who makes them, God only knows, I have never worn such scarves. Having unwound the scarf, the gentleman ordered dinner to be served. While the various dishes customary in taverns were being brought to him — such as cabbage soup with a flaky pastry deliberately kept for travelers over the course of several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, roast capon, salted cucumber, and the eternal sweet flaky pastry always ready at one's service — while all of this was being served to him, both reheated and simply cold, he made the servant, or waiter, tell him all manner of nonsense — about who had previously kept the tavern and who kept it now, and how much income it brought in, and whether their master was a great scoundrel; to which the waiter, as usual, replied: "Oh, a great rogue, sir." Just as in enlightened Europe, so too in enlightened Russia there are now a great many respectable people who cannot eat in a tavern without having a chat with the servant, and sometimes even amusing themselves by joking with him. However, the newcomer did not ask entirely idle questions; he inquired with extraordinary precision about who the governor of the town was, who the chairman of the court was, who the prosecutor was — in short, he did not overlook a single important official; but with even greater precision, if not with genuine interest, he inquired about all the notable landowners: how many serfs each one owned, how far each lived from town, what kind of character each had and how often each came to town; he inquired attentively about the state of the region: whether there had been any diseases in their province — epidemic fevers, deadly fevers of any kind, smallpox and the like — and all of this so thoroughly and with such precision as to suggest something more than mere curiosity. In his manner the gentleman had something solid about him, and he blew his nose extraordinarily loudly. It is unknown how he managed it, but his nose rang out like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, entirely innocent distinction nonetheless earned him a great deal of respect from the tavern servant, so that every time the latter heard that sound, he would shake his hair, straighten up more respectfully, and, bowing his head down from above, would ask: is anything needed? After dinner the gentleman drank a cup of coffee and sat down on the sofa, placing behind his back a pillow which in Russian taverns, instead of elastic wool, is stuffed with something extraordinarily resembling brick and cobblestone. Then he began to yawn and ordered himself to be shown to his room, where, lying down, he slept for two hours. Having rested,He wrote on a scrap of paper, at the request of the inn servant, his rank, name, and surname to be reported to the proper authorities, to the police. On the paper, the waiter, descending the stairs, spelled out the following: "Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, on personal business." While the waiter was still spelling out the note, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself set off to see the town, with which he appeared to be satisfied, having found that the town was in no way inferior to other provincial towns: the yellow paint on the stone houses struck the eye sharply, and the gray on the wooden ones showed modestly dark. The houses were one, two, and one and a half stories tall, with the eternal mezzanine, considered very handsome by provincial architects. In places these houses seemed lost amid streets as wide as fields and endless wooden fences; in places they clustered together, and here more movement of people and liveliness was noticeable. There were signs nearly washed away by rain, bearing pretzels and boots, here and there with blue trousers painted on them and the name of some tailor from Arshava; here a shop with caps, peaked caps, and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fyodorov"; there a billiard table was painted with two players in tailcoats of the kind worn on our stages by guests who enter in the last act. The players were depicted taking aim with their cues, their arms bent somewhat backward and their legs askew, having just executed an entrechat in the air. Beneath all this was written: "And here is the establishment." Here and there on the street stood tables with nuts, soap, and gingerbread resembling soap; here a tavern with a fat fish painted on it and a fork stuck into it. Most often of all one noticed the darkened double-headed imperial eagles, which have now been replaced by the laconic inscription: "Drinking House." The pavement everywhere was in poor condition. He also looked into the town garden, which consisted of thin little trees that had taken poorly to the soil, with supports at the base in the form of triangles, very neatly painted with green oil paint. However, though these little trees were no taller than reeds, it had been said of them in the newspapers, in a description of an illumination, that "our town has been adorned, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, wide-branching trees that provide coolness on a sweltering day," and that on the occasion "it was very touching to see how the hearts of the citizens trembled with an abundance of gratitude and shed streams of tears as a token of appreciation to the lord mayor." Having inquired in detail of a sentry where one could most conveniently get to the cathedral, the government offices, and the governor's residence if the need arose, he went to take a look at the river that flowed through the middle of the town, tore off along the way a playbill nailed to a post, intending to read it properly when he got home, gazed intently at a lady of not unpleasant appearance who was passing along the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in military livery with a bundle in his hand, and, casting one more glance over everything, as if to fix the layout of the place firmly in his memory, went straight home to his room, assisted slightly on the stairs by the inn servant. Having had his fill of tea, he sat down at the table, ordered a candle to be brought, took the playbill from his pocket, held it up to the candle, and began to read, squinting his right eye a little. There was not much of note in the playbill, however: a drama by Mr. Kotzebue was being performed, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplevyin and Cora by Miss Zyablova, the remaining persons were even less remarkable; yet he read through them all, got as far as the price of the stalls, and learned that the playbill had been printed at the provincial government printing house, then turned it over to the other side to see whether there was anything there as well, but finding nothing, rubbed his eyes, folded it neatly, and placed it in his little box, where he was in the habit of putting everything that came his way. The day was apparently concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour cabbage soup, and a sound sleep with the full force of a pump handle, as they say in certain parts of the vast Russian state.

The entire following day was devoted to calls; the newcomer set out to pay visits to all the town dignitaries. He paid his respects to the governor, who, as it turned out, was like Chichikov neither fat,he was slender, wore the Order of Anna around his neck, and there were even rumors that he had been put forward for a star;[ The Star — the Order of Stanislav.

] however, he was a great good-natured fellow and even embroidered on tulle himself sometimes. Then he called on the vice-governor, then visited the prosecutor, the chairman of the chamber, the chief of police, the tax farmer, the superintendent of the state factories… it is a pity that it is rather difficult to remember all the mighty of this world; but suffice it to say that the newcomer showed extraordinary energy when it came to paying visits: he even turned up to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical board and the city architect. And afterward he sat for a long time in his carriage, trying to think of whom else he might call on, but there were no more officials to be found in the town. In his conversations with these men of power he was very skillfully able to flatter each one. He hinted to the governor, as if in passing, that entering his province was like entering paradise, that the roads were velvet everywhere, and that those governments which appointed wise dignitaries deserved great praise. To the chief of police he said something very complimentary about the town's watchmen; and in conversation with the vice-governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were still only state councillors, he even said by mistake twice: "Your Excellency," which pleased them greatly. The result of all this was that the governor invited him to come to his private evening party that very day, and the other officials likewise, each in his own way — some to dinner, some to a game of boston, some to a cup of tea.

About himself the newcomer seemed to avoid saying much; when he did speak, it was in generalities, with noticeable modesty, and his conversation in such cases took on a somewhat bookish turn: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and not worthy of much concern, that he had experienced much in his lifetime, had suffered in the service for the sake of truth, had made many enemies who had even made attempts on his life, and that now, wishing to find peace, he was seeking at last to choose a place of residence, and that, having arrived in this town, he considered it his absolute duty to pay his respects to its foremost dignitaries. That was all the town learned about this new personage, who very soon made a point of showing himself at the governor's evening party. Preparations for this party took well over two hours, and here the newcomer displayed such attentiveness to his toilet as is not often seen anywhere. After a brief after-dinner nap he ordered water brought for washing and spent an extraordinarily long time rubbing soap into both cheeks, propping them from the inside with his tongue; then, taking a towel from the shoulder of the inn servant, he wiped his full face on all sides with it, beginning from behind the ears and snorting twice directly into the face of the inn servant beforehand. Then he put on his shirt front before the mirror, plucked two hairs that had crept out of his nose, and immediately afterward found himself in a tailcoat of cranberry color with a shimmer. Thus dressed, he rolled off in his own carriage through the endlessly wide streets, lit by the meager illumination of lamps flickering here and there. However, the governor's house was so brightly lit as if for a ball; a carriage with lanterns stood before the entrance, two gendarmes flanked the door, the shouts of outriders rang out in the distance — in short, everything as it should be. Entering the ballroom, Chichikov had to shut his eyes for a moment, because the glare from the candles, lamps, and ladies' dresses was tremendous. Everything was flooded with light. Black tailcoats flickered and darted about separately and in clusters here and there, just as flies dart about on white gleaming sugar loaves in the heat of a July summer, when the old housekeeper chops and breaks the sugar into sparkling lumps before an open window; all the children have gathered around and watch, following curiously the movements of her rough hands lifting the hammer, while airy squadrons of flies, lifted by the light air, fly in boldly, as if they were the full masters of the place, and taking advantage of the old woman's dim sight and the sun troubling her eyes, they cover the choice morsels here in scattered fashion, there in dense clusters. Satiated by the rich summer, which at every step sets out tempting dishes for them anyway, they flew in not at all to eat, but merely to show themselves, to stroll back and forth across the sugar heap, to rub their hind or front legs together,or scratch themselves under their little wings with them, or stretch out both front paws and rub them over their heads, then turn around and fly off again, only to return with fresh bothersome squadrons. Chichikov had barely had time to look around before he was seized by the arm by the governor, who introduced him on the spot to the governor's wife. The visiting guest did not let himself down here either: he said some compliment, quite fitting for a man of middle age, holding a rank neither too high nor too low. When the established pairs of dancers had pressed everyone against the wall, he stood with his hands clasped behind his back and watched them for a minute or two with great attention. Many of the ladies were well dressed and fashionable, while others had dressed in whatever God had sent to the provincial town. The men here, as everywhere, were of two kinds: the slender ones, who hovered constantly around the ladies; some of them were of such a sort that it was hard to tell them apart from Petersburg men — they had equally well-considered and tastefully combed sideburns, or simply presentable, very smoothly shaved oval faces, they sidled up to the ladies just as carelessly, spoke French just as readily, and made the ladies laugh just as they did in Petersburg. The other kind of men consisted of the stout ones, or those like Chichikov — that is, not exactly too stout, yet not thin either. These, on the contrary, squinted and backed away from the ladies and only glanced around to see whether the governor's servant had set up a green table for whist somewhere. Their faces were full and round, some even had warts, a few were pockmarked, they wore their hair neither in topknots nor in curls nor in the style the French call "devil take me" — their hair was either cropped short or slicked down, and their features were more rounded and solid. These were the distinguished officials of the town. Alas! the stout ones know better how to manage their affairs in this world than the slender ones. The slender ones serve mostly on special assignments or merely appear on the rolls and dart this way and that; their existence is somehow too light, too airy, and altogether unreliable. The stout ones, however, never occupy indirect positions, but always direct ones, and once they settle somewhere, they settle firmly and solidly, so that the place is more likely to crack and bend beneath them than they are to be dislodged. They have no love for outward show; their frock coat is not cut as smartly as the slender man's, yet in their strongboxes there is the grace of God. The slender man in three years has not a single soul left that isn't pawned at the lending house; the stout man, calm and unhurried, suddenly turns up with a house at one end of town, bought in his wife's name, then another house at the other end, then a little village near the town, then an estate with all its appurtenances. At last the stout man, having served God and tsar and earned universal respect, leaves the service, moves away, and becomes a landowner, a fine Russian gentleman, a generous host — and lives well, and lives very well. And after him, once again, his slender heirs squander all their father's wealth at post-horse speed, as is the Russian custom. It cannot be concealed that reflections of almost this very kind occupied Chichikov at the time he was surveying the company, and the result was that he finally joined the stout ones, among whom he encountered almost all familiar faces: the prosecutor with very black, thick eyebrows and a left eye that winked slightly, as though saying, "Come along, brother, into the other room — I've something to tell you there" — a man who was, for all that, serious and taciturn; the postmaster, a short man but a wit and a philosopher; the president of the chamber, a most sensible and amiable man — all of whom greeted him like an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov responded with a bow tilted slightly to one side, though not without a certain pleasantness. Here he also made the acquaintance of the very affable and courteous landowner Manilov, and of Sobakevich, who appeared somewhat ungainly and who, at their very first meeting, stepped on his foot, saying, "I beg your pardon." Here too a card was thrust into his hand for whist, which he accepted with an equally polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not rise again until supper. All conversation ceased entirely, as always happens when people at last give themselves over to a serious occupation. Although the postmaster was very talkative, even he, once he had taken the cards in hand,that very moment expressed on his face a thoughtful physiognomy,

The next day Chichikov went to dine and spend the evening at the police chief's, where from three o'clock after dinner they sat down to whist and played until two in the morning. There, among others, he made the acquaintance of a landowner named Nozdrev, a man of about thirty, a lively fellow who after three or four words began addressing him as "thou". With the police chief and the prosecutor Nozdrev was also on familiar terms and treated them in a friendly manner; but when they sat down to play for high stakes, the police chief and the prosecutor examined his tricks with extraordinary attention and watched nearly every card he played. The next day Chichikov spent the evening at the home of the president of the chamber, who received his guests in a dressing gown, somewhat greasy, and among them two ladies of some sort. Then he attended an evening at the vice-governor's, a large dinner at a tax farmer's, a small dinner at the prosecutor's which, however, was worth a large one; a snack after mass given by the mayor of the town, which was also worth a full dinner. In short, not a single hour did he have to remain at home, and he returned to the inn only to sleep. The newcomer somehow managed to find his footing in everything and showed himself to be an experienced man of the world. Whatever the conversation might be about, he always knew how to keep it going: if the talk was of a stud farm, he spoke of stud farms; if they spoke of good dogs, here too he offered very sound remarks; if the matter concerned an inquiry conducted by the treasury chamber, he showed that judicial tricks were not unknown to him either; if there was discussion of a game of billiards, he did not miss a beat there either; if they spoke of virtue, he discoursed on virtue very well, even with tears in his eyes; of the distilling of spirits, and of spirits he knew the business; of customs inspectors and officials, and of them he judged as though he had himself been both an official and an inspector. But what was remarkable was that he knew how to wrap all of this in a certain dignity, knew how to carry himself well. He spoke neither loudly nor softly, but exactly as one ought. In short, whichever way you turned, he was a very decent man. All the officials were pleased by the arrival of the new face. The governor expressed himself to the effect that he was a well-intentioned man; the prosecutor, that he was a capable man; the gendarmerie colonel said he was a learned man; the president of the chamber, that he was a knowledgeable and respectable man; the police chief, that he was a respectable and amiable man; the police chief's wife, that he was the most amiable and courteous of men. Even Sobakevich himself, who rarely spoke well of anyone, having arrived rather late from town and already completely undressed and lying down on the bed beside his lean wife, said to her: "I, my dear, was at the governor's evening party, and dined at the police chief's, and made the acquaintance of Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov: a most agreeable man!" To which his wife replied: "Hmm!" and gave him a kick with her foot.

Such an opinion, most flattering to the guest, was formed of him in the town, and it held until a certain strange peculiarity of the guest and an enterprise, or, as they say in the provinces, a passage, which the reader will soon learn of, threw nearly the entire town into utter bewilderment.

Chapter Two

The visiting gentleman had already been living in the town for more than a week, driving about to evening parties and dinners and thus spending, as they say, a very pleasant time. At last he resolved to extend his visits beyond the town and to call on the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps he was prompted to this by another, more substantial reason, a matter more serious, closer to his heart… But the reader will learn of all this gradually and in due time, if only he has the patience to read the tale here offered, a very long one, which will afterward spread wider and more spaciously as it draws nearer to the end that crowns the affair. The coachman Selifan was given orders to harness the horses early in the morning to the well-known chaise; Petrushka was ordered to remain at home and keep watch over the room and the trunk. It will not be amiss for the reader to become acquainted with these two serfs belonging to our hero. Though, of course, they are not such conspicuous figures, and that,what are called secondary or even tertiary characters, though the main threads and springs of the poem do not rest upon them and only here and there touch and lightly catch at them — yet the author is exceedingly fond of being thorough in all things and, from this angle, despite being a Russian himself, wishes to be as precise as a German. This will not, however, take much time or space, since little need be added to what the reader already knows — namely, that Petrushka went about in a rather wide brown coat that had come off his master's shoulder and had, as is customary among people of his station, a large nose and thick lips. He was more of a silent than a talkative character; he even had a noble inclination toward enlightenment, that is, toward reading books, the content of which gave him no trouble whatsoever: it was all perfectly the same to him whether it was the adventures of a lovesick hero, a simple primer, or a prayer book — he read everything with equal attention; had chemistry been put before him, he would not have refused that either. What he enjoyed was not what he read but rather the reading itself, or, better said, the very process of reading — the fact that from letters there forever emerges some word, which sometimes the devil only knows what it means. This reading was performed mostly in a recumbent position in the anteroom, on the bed and on the mattress, which had on that account become flattened and thin as a pancake. Besides his passion for reading, he had two other habits that constituted two further characteristic traits: sleeping without undressing, just as he was, in the same coat, and always carrying about with him a certain peculiar atmosphere of his own, a smell of his own, which had something of an inhabited room about it, so that it was enough for him merely to set up his bed somewhere — even in a room that had previously been uninhabited — and drag his overcoat and belongings there, and it would already seem as though people had lived in that room for ten years. Chichikov, being a man of very delicate sensibilities and even in some cases fastidious, would draw in the air with his fresh morning nose and merely wince and shake his head, muttering: "You, brother, the devil knows what's wrong with you, do you sweat or something. You ought to at least go to the bathhouse." To which Petrushka made no reply and tried at once to busy himself with something — either he would go at the master's hanging dress coat with a brush, or simply tidy something up. What he thought during those moments of silence — perhaps he was saying to himself: "And you're a fine one too, aren't you tired of repeating the same thing forty times over" — God only knows, it is hard to know what a household serf thinks at the moment his master is giving him instruction. So, that is what can be said at first about Petrushka. The coachman Selifan was an entirely different sort of man… But the author feels very much ashamed to have kept his readers so long occupied with people of the lower class, knowing from experience how reluctantly they make the acquaintance of the lower orders. Such is the Russian character: a powerful passion for rubbing shoulders with someone who might be even one rank above him, and a nodding acquaintance with a count or a prince is worth more to him than any close and intimate friendship. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a collegiate councillor. Court councillors may perhaps make his acquaintance, but those who have already climbed to the ranks of generals — those, God knows, may perhaps cast one of those contemptuous glances that a proud man casts upon everything that crawls at his feet, or, what is even worse, may perhaps pass by with an inattention that is devastating to the author. But however distressing either prospect may be, it is nonetheless necessary to return to the hero. So, having given the necessary orders the evening before, waking very early in the morning, washing himself, rubbing himself from head to toe with a wet sponge — which was done only on Sundays, and that day happened to be a Sunday — having shaved in such a way that his cheeks became genuine satin in terms of smoothness and gloss, having put on a coat of cranberry color with a sparkle and then his overcoat lined with great bear fur, he descended the staircase, supported by the arm now on one side, now on the other by the inn servant, and took his seat in the chaise. With a rumble the chaise rolled out from under the gates of the inn and into the street. A passing priest removed his hat, several boys in dirty shirts stretched out their hands, saying:"Master, spare a coin for an orphan!" The coachman, noticing that one of them was particularly fond of jumping onto the footboard, gave him a crack of the whip, and the carriage began bouncing over the cobblestones. Not without some relief was the striped toll barrier spotted in the distance, signaling that the paved road, like any other torment, would soon come to an end; and after knocking his head against the carriage body a few more times quite hard, Chichikov at last rolled along on soft ground. No sooner had the town fallen behind than, as is our custom, nonsense and wilderness began to spread on both sides of the road: tussocks, fir trees, low scraggly bushes of young pines, charred trunks of old ones, wild heather, and similar rubbish. There were villages stretched out in a line, their construction resembling old stacked firewood, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations beneath them in the shape of hanging embroidered towels. Several peasants, as usual, sat yawning on benches before their gates in their sheepskin coats. Women with broad faces and bound chests looked out from the upper windows; from the lower ones a calf peered out, or a pig thrust its blind snout. In a word, familiar sights. Having traveled the fifteenth verst, he recalled that here, according to Manilov, his village ought to be; but the sixteenth verst flew by as well, and still no village was in sight, and had it not been for two peasants they happened to meet, they would hardly have managed to find their way.

To the question of how far the village of Zamanilovka was, the peasants took off their hats, and one of them, the shrewder of the two, who wore a wedge-shaped beard, replied:

— Manilovka, perhaps, and not Zamanilovka?

— Well yes, Manilovka.

— Manilovka! once you go another verst, there it is, that is, straight to the right.

— To the right? — the coachman called back.

— To the right, — said the peasant. — That'll be the road to Manilovka; there's no such place as Zamanilovka. It's called that, that is, its name is Manilovka, and there's no Zamanilovka here at all. Up there on the hill you'll see a house, stone, two stories, the master's house, where, that is, the master himself lives. That's your Manilovka, and there's no Zamanilovka here and never has been.

They set off to find Manilovka. After traveling two versts they came upon a turn onto a country road, but two, then three, then four versts seemed to pass, and still no two-story stone house was to be seen. At that point Chichikov recalled that if a friend invites you to his village fifteen versts away, it means the real distance is a good thirty. The village of Manilovka could have tempted few with its setting. The master's house stood alone on a rise, that is, on a height exposed to every wind that cared to blow; the slope of the hill on which it stood was covered with clipped turf. Scattered over it in the English manner were two or three flowerbeds with lilac bushes and yellow acacias; five or six birches in small clumps here and there raised their slender fine-leafed tops. Beneath two of them a gazebo was visible with a flat green dome, blue wooden columns, and an inscription: "Temple of Solitary Reflection"; lower down a pond covered with green growth, which is, after all, nothing unusual in the English-style gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this rise, and partly along the slope itself, gray log huts darkened in every direction, which our hero, for reasons unknown, immediately set about counting and tallied more than two hundred; not a single growing tree or patch of greenery anywhere among them; everywhere only bare logs met the eye. Though the time during which they will be passing through the entryway, the anteroom, and the dining room is rather brief, let us try to see whether we might make some use of it and say a thing or two about the master of the house. But here the author must confess that such an undertaking is very difficult. It is far easier to portray characters of a grand scale: there you simply fling paint with a full hand onto the canvas — dark burning eyes, heavy brows, a forehead creased by a wrinkle, a black or flame-red cloak thrown over the shoulder — and the portrait is done; but these gentlemen, of whom there are so many in the world, who at first glance look very much alike, and yet upon closer inspection reveal a great many elusive peculiarities — these gentlemen are terribly difficult to portray. Here one must strain one's attention greatly before all the subtle, nearly invisible features come forward, and in general one must delve far deeper with a gaze already sharpened by the science of inquiry.

God alone, perhaps, could have said what Manilov's character was. There is a type of person known by the name: so-so people, neither one thing nor another, neither Bogdan in the city nor Selifan in the village, as the proverb goes. Perhaps Manilov ought to be counted among them. In appearance he was a presentable man; his features were not without pleasantness, but into that pleasantness it seemed that far too much sugar had been poured; in his manners and turns of phrase there was something ingratiating, something that sought favor and acquaintance. He smiled enticingly, was fair-haired, with blue eyes. In the first minute of conversation with him you cannot help saying: "What a pleasant and kind man!" In the next minute you say nothing at all, and in the third you say: "The devil knows what this is!" — and step away; and if you do not step away, you will feel a deadly boredom. You will never get from him a single lively or even a bold word, such as you might hear from almost anyone if you touch upon a subject that stirs them. Everyone has their own passion: one man's passion has turned to greyhounds; another fancies himself a great lover of music and feels all the deep passages in it wonderfully; a third is a master at dining splendidly; a fourth at playing a role just one notch above the one assigned to him; a fifth, with more modest desires, sleeps and dreams of strolling at a public promenade with a flügel-adjutant, to show off before his friends, acquaintances, and even strangers; a sixth is already endowed with a hand that feels a supernatural urge to bend the corner of some diamond ace or deuce, while the hand of a seventh fairly itches to establish order somewhere, to get a little closer to the person of a station superintendent or coachmen — in a word, everyone has something of their own, but Manilov had nothing. At home he spoke very little and for the most part reflected and pondered, but what he pondered about was likewise known only to God. One could not say that he occupied himself with the estate; he never even rode out to the fields, the estate ran somehow on its own. When the steward said: "It would be good, master, to do such and such," — "Yes, not a bad idea," he would usually reply, smoking his pipe, which he had made a habit of smoking back when he served in the army, where he was considered the most modest, most refined, and most cultivated of officers. "Yes, precisely, not a bad idea," he would repeat. When a peasant came to him and, scratching the back of his head, said: "Master, allow me to go off to work, to earn some money for the poll tax," — "Go ahead," he would say, smoking his pipe,and it never even occurred to him that the peasant had gone off to drink. Sometimes, standing on the porch and looking out at the yard and the pond, he would talk about how wonderful it would be if suddenly an underground passage were built from the house, or if a stone bridge were constructed across the pond, with shops on both sides where merchants would sit and sell various small goods needed by the peasants. At such moments his eyes would become extraordinarily soft and his face would take on the most contented expression; yet all these grand schemes invariably ended in nothing but words. On the desk in his study there always lay some little book, bookmarked at page fourteen, which he had been continuously reading for two years already. In his house something was always missing: the drawing room had beautiful furniture upholstered in elegant silk fabric that had surely cost a pretty penny, but there had not been enough for two armchairs, and those two stood covered in plain matting; the host, however, had for several years warned every guest with the words: "Do not sit in those chairs, they are not ready yet." In another room there was no furniture at all, though in the first days after the wedding it had been said: "Darling, tomorrow we really must see about putting at least some furniture in that room for the time being." In the evenings a very elegant candelabra of dark bronze with three antique graces and a fashionable mother-of-pearl shield was placed on the table, and beside it stood some plain brass invalid of a candlestick, lame, leaning to one side, and covered in tallow, though neither the host, nor the hostess, nor the servants took any notice of this. His wife… but then, they were perfectly content with each other. Despite the fact that more than eight years of marriage had passed, each still brought the other a little piece of apple, or a sweet, or a nut, and said in a touchingly tender voice that expressed complete love: "Open wide, darling, I'll pop this little piece in for you." Needless to say, the little mouth opened on such occasions most graciously.

For birthdays, surprises were prepared: some beaded case for a toothpick. And very often, sitting together on the sofa, suddenly and for no apparent reason whatsoever, one would set aside a pipe and the other some needlework, if she happened to be holding any at the time, and they would press upon each other such a long and languid kiss that one could easily have smoked a small straw cigarette during its duration. In a word, they were what is called happy. Of course, one might observe that there are many other matters to attend to in a household besides prolonged kisses and surprises, and many questions might be raised. Why, for instance, is the cooking done so stupidly and carelessly in the kitchen? Why is the pantry rather bare? Why is the housekeeper a thief? Why are the servants slovenly drunkards? Why does the entire household sleep shamelessly and loaf about the rest of the time? But all these are vulgar matters, and Manilova was well brought up. And a good upbringing, as is well known, is acquired in boarding schools. And in boarding schools, as is well known, three principal subjects form the foundation of human virtues: French, essential for the happiness of family life, the pianoforte, for providing pleasant moments for one's husband, and finally the domestic arts proper: knitting purses and other surprises. There are, however, various improvements and changes in methods, especially in the present day; all of this depends largely on the good sense and abilities of the boarding school proprietresses themselves. In some schools it goes like this: first the pianoforte, then French, and after that the domestic arts. And sometimes it goes like this: first the domestic arts, that is, knitting surprises, then French, and after that the pianoforte. Various methods exist. Thereupon Manilov explained the matter to the coachman with the same amiability and even addressed him once as "you" in the polite form.

The coachman, upon hearing that he needed to pass two turns and take the third, said: "We'll manage, your honor," — and Chichikov drove off, accompanied for a long time by the bows and handkerchief-wavings of the hosts, who rose up on their tiptoes.

Manilov stood on the porch for a long time, watching the departing carriage with his eyes, and when it had completely disappeared from view, he still stood there, smoking his pipe. At last he went inside, sat down in a chair, and gave himself over to reflection, rejoicing in his heart that he had provided his guest with a small pleasure. Then his thoughts drifted imperceptibly to other subjects and finally wandered off to goodness knows where. He thought of the happiness of a life of friendship, of how fine it would be to live with a friend on the bank of some river, then a bridge began to be built across that river in his mind, then an enormous house with such a tall belvedere that one could see even Moscow from it and sit there in the evenings drinking tea in the open air and discussing various pleasant subjects. Then that he and Chichikov had arrived together at some social gathering in fine carriages, where they charmed everyone with the pleasantness of their manner, and that the sovereign, having learned of their friendship, had promoted them to generals, and further on, at last, goodness knows what else, which he himself could make no sense of at all. The strange request of Chichikov suddenly interrupted all his reveries. The thought of it somehow refused to settle in his head: no matter how he turned it over, he could not explain it to himself, and all the while he sat and smoked his pipe, which went on until supper itself.

Chapter Three

Meanwhile Chichikov sat in a pleasant frame of mind in his carriage, which had long been rolling along the post road. From the preceding chapter it is already clear what constituted the chief object of his tastes and inclinations, and therefore it is no wonder that he soon immersed himself in it entirely, body and soul. The suppositions, calculations, and reflections that wandered across his face were evidently very agreeable, for they left behind them at every moment traces of a satisfied smile. Occupied with them,he paid no attention at all to how his coachman, pleased with the reception from Manilov's household serfs, was making quite sensible remarks to the dappled trace horse harnessed on the right side. This dappled horse was terribly crafty and made only a show of pulling, while the bay shaft horse and the trace horse of a light chestnut color, known as the Assessor because he had been acquired from some assessor, worked with all their hearts, so that even in their eyes one could notice the pleasure they derived from it. "Be clever, be clever! I'll outsmart you!" said Selifan, rising up and cracking his whip at the lazy one. "You just do your job, you German pantaloon-wearer! The bay is a respectable horse, he does his duty, I'll gladly give him an extra measure, because he's a respectable horse, and the Assessor is also a good horse… Come on, come on! Why are you twitching your ears? Listen, you fool, when someone's talking to you! I'm not going to teach you bad habits, you lout. Look where he's creeping!" Here he cracked the whip at him again, adding: "Ugh, you barbarian! You damned Bonaparte!" Then he shouted at all of them: "Hey there, dear ones!" and lashed all three, not as a punishment but to show he was pleased with them. Having given them such a treat, he turned his speech back to the dappled one: "You think you can hide your behavior. No, you live by the truth if you want people to show you respect. Now the landowner we just visited, those are good people. I'm happy to talk when someone's a good person; with a good person we're always friends, close companions; whether it's tea or a bite to eat — gladly, if the person's good. Every man shows respect to a good person. Now our master, everyone respects him, because he, you hear, served in the government service, he's a collegiate councillor…"

Reasoning in this way, Selifan gradually drifted into the most remote abstractions. Had Chichikov been listening, he would have learned many details that concerned him personally; but his thoughts were so absorbed in their own subject that only a powerful clap of thunder made him come to his senses and look around; the entire sky was completely overcast with clouds, and the dusty post road was being sprinkled with drops of rain. Finally a thunderclap rang out a second time, louder and closer, and the rain suddenly poured down as if from a bucket. At first, taking a slanting direction, it lashed one side of the carriage body, then the other, then, changing its manner of attack and becoming perfectly straight, it drummed directly on the top of the carriage body; the splashes at last began to reach his face. This made him draw the leather curtains with their two small round windows meant for viewing the roadside scenery, and order Selifan to drive faster. Selifan, also interrupted right in the middle of his speech, realized that indeed there was no time to dawdle, pulled out from under the box some piece of grey cloth, put it on by the sleeves, grabbed the reins, and shouted at his troika, which had been barely lifting its feet, having felt a pleasant languor from the instructive speeches. But Selifan could not for the life of him recall whether he had passed two or three turnings. Thinking it over and trying to remember the road a little, he figured out that there had been many turnings, all of which he had missed. Since a Russian man in decisive moments will find what to do without going into lengthy deliberations, he turned right at the first crossroad, shouted: "Hey there, dear friends!" and set off at a gallop, giving little thought to where the road he had taken would lead.

The rain, however, seemed to have set in for a long time. The dust lying on the road quickly turned to mud, and every minute it became harder for the horses to drag the carriage. Chichikov was already growing greatly anxious at not seeing Sobakevich's village for so long. By his reckoning, they should have arrived long ago. He peered to either side, but the darkness was such that you could not see your hand in front of your face.

"Selifan!" he said at last, leaning out of the carriage.

"What is it, master?" answered Selifan.

"Have a look — can you see any village?"

"No, master, there's nothing to be seen anywhere!" After which Selifan, waving his whip, struck up something that was not quite a song but something so long it had no end. Everything went into it: all the encouraging and urging cries,with which horses are treated all across Russia from one end to the other; adjectives of every gender thrown in without any further consideration, whatever first came to the tongue. It got to the point where he finally began calling them secretaries.

Meanwhile Chichikov began to notice that the chaise was rocking from side to side and giving him rather violent jolts; this made him feel that they had turned off the road and were probably dragging along a ploughed field. Selifan seemed to have figured this out himself, but said not a word.

"What are you doing, you rascal, what road are you taking?" said Chichikov.

"Well, what's to be done, master, the weather's like this; you can't see the whip, it's so dark!" Having said this, he tilted the chaise so sharply that Chichikov was forced to hold on with both hands. Only then did he notice that Selifan had been drinking.

"Hold on, hold on, you'll tip us over!" he shouted at him.

"No, master, how could I tip it over," said Selifan. "It's a bad thing to tip over, I know that myself; I won't tip it over at all." He then began to turn the chaise slightly, turning and turning until he finally overturned it completely on its side. Chichikov went sprawling into the mud with both hands and feet. Selifan did stop the horses, though they would have stopped on their own, being thoroughly exhausted. This unforeseen incident left him utterly astonished. Climbing down from the box, he stood before the chaise with both hands on his hips, while his master floundered in the mud trying to crawl out, and after some reflection said: "Well, look at that, it's gone and tipped over!"

"You're drunk as a cobbler!" said Chichikov.

"No, master, how could I be drunk! I know that's a bad thing, being drunk. I had a chat with a friend, because you can talk with a good person, there's no harm in that; and we had a bite together. Having a bite is no offense; you can have a bite with a good person."

"And what did I tell you last time when you got drunk? eh? forgotten?" said Chichikov.

"No, your honor, how could I forget. I know my business. I know it's not good to be drunk. I had a chat with a good person, because..."

"I'll give you such a flogging that you'll know how to chat with a good person!"

"As your grace sees fit," replied Selifan, agreeable to everything, "if you want to flog, then flog; I have nothing against it at all. Why not flog, if it's deserved, that's the master's will. It needs to be done, because a peasant gets out of hand, order must be kept. If it's deserved, then flog; why not flog?"

To such reasoning the master found absolutely nothing to reply. But at that moment, it seemed as though fate itself had decided to take pity on him. From a distance came the sound of dogs barking. Overjoyed, Chichikov gave the order to urge the horses on. A Russian coachman has a good instinct in place of eyes; and so it happens that he sometimes, with eyes shut, drives at full speed and always arrives somewhere. Selifan, unable to see a thing, directed the horses so straight toward the village that he stopped only when the chaise struck the fence with its shafts and there was simply nowhere further to go. Chichikov could only make out through the thick curtain of pouring rain something resembling a roof. He sent Selifan to find the gate, which would no doubt have taken a long time had there not been in Russia, in place of doormen, bold dogs who announced him so loudly that he pressed his fingers to his ears. A light flickered in one window and reached in a misty stream as far as the fence, pointing out the gate to our travelers. Selifan began to knock, and soon, as the wicket gate opened, a figure appeared wrapped in a peasant coat, and the master and servant heard a hoarse woman's voice:

"Who's knocking? What do you want at this hour?"

"Travelers, my dear, let us in for the night," said Chichikov.

"Look at him, what a sharp one," said the old woman, "arriving at such an hour! This isn't an inn for you: a landowner lives here."

"What's to be done, my dear: you see, we've lost our way. Can't spend the night out on the steppe at such an hour."

"Yes, it's dark out, a bad time," added Selifan.

"Quiet, fool," said Chichikov.

"And who are you?" said the old woman.

"A nobleman, my dear."

The word "nobleman" seemed to make the old woman think for a moment.

"Wait, I'll go tell the mistress,"she said, and about two minutes later returned with a lantern in hand.

The gate was unlocked. A small light flickered in another window. The carriage drove into the yard and stopped before a small house, which was difficult to make out in the darkness. Only one half of it was lit by the light coming from the windows; visible too was a puddle in front of the house, which that same light struck directly. The rain drummed loudly on the wooden roof and trickled down in gurgling streams into a barrel set out to catch it. Meanwhile the dogs were howling in every conceivable voice: one, throwing its head back, drew out its notes so long and with such effort, as though it were being paid God knows what salary for it; another chopped away briskly, like a sexton; among them rang out, like a post bell, the tireless treble of what was probably a young pup, and all of this was finally crowned by a bass, perhaps an old dog endowed with a sturdy canine constitution, for it wheezed the way a choral double bass wheezes when a concert is in full swing: the tenors rise on tiptoe from their desperate desire to hit a high note, and everything strains upward, heads thrown back, while he alone, tucking his unshaven chin into his cravat, squatting and sinking almost to the ground, lets his note rumble up from there, making the windowpanes shake and rattle. From the barking alone, composed of such musicians, one might have supposed that the village was a decent-sized one; but our hero, soaked through and chilled to the bone, was thinking of nothing but his bed. The carriage had barely come to a full stop before he had already jumped out onto the porch, staggered, and nearly fell. Another woman came out onto the porch, younger than the first but very much like her. She led him into a room. Chichikov cast two quick glances around: the room was hung with old striped wallpaper; pictures of various birds; between the windows, small old-fashioned mirrors with dark frames shaped like curled leaves; behind each mirror were tucked either a letter, or an old deck of cards, or a stocking; a wall clock with painted flowers on its face… it was impossible to notice anything more. He felt his eyes sticking together, as though someone had smeared them with honey. A moment later the mistress of the house entered, a woman of advanced years, in some sort of nightcap hastily put on, with flannel around her neck, one of those dear landladies, small landowners, who lament over poor harvests and losses and hold their heads slightly to one side, yet all the while quietly accumulate little sums of money in calico pouches tucked away in the drawers of a chest. Into one pouch they sort all the ruble coins, into another the fifty-kopek pieces, into a third the quarter-rubles, though to look at it one would think the chest contained nothing but linen, nightgowns, skeins of thread, and a ripped-apart cloak intended to be made into a dress someday, if the old one happens to get scorched while baking holiday pastries and fritters of all kinds, or wears itself out on its own. But the dress will not get scorched and will not wear itself out: the old woman is thrifty, and the cloak is destined to lie for a long time in its ripped-apart state, and then to pass by last will and testament to the niece of a second cousin, along with all manner of other odds and ends.

Chichikov apologized for having disturbed her with his unexpected arrival.

"Never mind, never mind," said the mistress. "What a time God has brought you here! Such a commotion and blizzard… After the road you ought to have a bite of something, but it's the middle of the night, there's no way to prepare anything."

The hostess's words were interrupted by a strange hissing sound, which gave the guest quite a fright; the noise resembled what it would sound like if the entire room had filled with snakes; but glancing upward, he was reassured, having figured out that the wall clock had taken it into its head to strike. The hissing was immediately followed by a wheezing, and finally, straining with all its might, it struck two o'clock with a sound as though someone were beating a stick against a cracked pot, after which the pendulum resumed its calm ticking from side to side.

Chichikov thanked the hostess, saying that he needed nothing, that she should not trouble herself about anything, that apart from a bed he required nothing at all, and he was merely curious to know what parts he had ended up in and how far it was from here to the estate of the landowner Sobakevich, to which the old woman replied,and had never heard such a name and that no such landowner existed at all.

— At least you know Manilov? — said Chichikov.

— And who is Manilov?

— A landowner, my dear.

— No, never heard of him, there's no such landowner.

— Well, who is there?

— Bobrov, Svinin, Kanapatyev, Kharpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov.

— Are they wealthy or not?

— No, father, there are no very wealthy ones. Some have twenty souls, some thirty, but those with a hundred — there are none like that.

Chichikov noted that he had wandered into quite a backwater.

— How far is it at least to the city?

— About sixty versts. How sorry I am that there is nothing for you to eat! Would you not like, dear sir, to have some tea?

— Thank you, my dear. I need nothing but a bed.

— True, after such a journey one really does need to rest. Here, make yourself comfortable, dear sir, on this sofa. Hey, Fetinya, bring the featherbed, pillows, and a sheet. What weather God has sent: such thunder — I had a candle burning before the icon all night. Oh, my dear, you've got your whole back and side covered in mud, like a pig! Where did you manage to get so dirty?

— Thank God I only got dirty, I should be grateful I didn't break my ribs entirely.

— Good heavens, what a dreadful thing! Do you need something to rub your back with?

— Thank you, thank you. Don't trouble yourself, just tell your girl to dry and clean my clothes.

— Do you hear, Fetinya! — said the mistress, turning to the woman who had come out onto the porch with a candle, and who had already managed to drag in the featherbed and, having fluffed it up on both sides with her hands, sent a whole flood of feathers flying all around the room. — Take his coat along with his underclothes and first dry them by the fire, the way we used to do for the late master, and then rub them down and beat them out properly.

— Yes, ma'am! — said Fetinya, spreading the sheet over the featherbed and laying down the pillows.

— Well, there's your bed ready, — said the mistress. — Good night, dear sir, I wish you a peaceful rest. Is there anything else you need? Perhaps you are used, my dear, to having someone scratch your heels at night? My late husband could never fall asleep without it.

But the guest declined the heel-scratching as well. The mistress left, and he immediately hurried to undress, handing Fetinya all the gear he had taken off, both outer and under, and Fetinya, having also wished him a good night on her part, carried off these wet garments. Left alone, he looked with some pleasure at his bed, which rose almost to the ceiling. Fetinya was evidently a master at fluffing featherbeds. When, having placed a chair, he climbed up onto the bed, it sank under him almost to the very floor, and the feathers, forced out from their bounds, flew into every corner of the room. He blew out the candle, pulled the cotton quilt over himself, and, curling up under it like a pretzel, fell asleep that very instant. He woke the next day quite late in the morning. The sun was shining directly into his eyes through the window, and the flies, which had been sleeping peacefully on the walls and ceiling the night before, had all turned their attention to him: one settled on his lip, another on his ear, a third was trying to find a way to settle on his very eye, and the one that had the imprudence to sit too close to his nostril he drew in his sleep right into his nose, which made him sneeze violently, —

the circumstance that had been the cause of his awakening. Casting a glance around the room, he now noticed that not all the pictures were of birds: among them hung a portrait of Kutuzov and some old man painted in oils with red cuffs on his uniform, such as were worn in the time of Pavel Petrovich. The clock again emitted its hissing and struck ten; a woman's face peeped through the door and instantly hid itself, for Chichikov, wishing to sleep more soundly, had thrown off everything entirely. The face that had peeped in seemed to him somewhat familiar. He began trying to recall who it might be, and at last remembered that it was the landlady. He put on his shirt; his clothes, already dried and brushed, lay beside him. Having dressed, he went up to the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that a turkey cock which had come up to the window at that moment — and the window was very close to the ground — suddenly gabbled something to him very rapidly in its strange language, probably "I wish you good health," to which Chichikov called it a fool. Going up to the window, he began to examine the views before him: the window looked out almost directly onto the chicken yard; at any rate, the narrow little courtyard in front of him was full of birds and all manner of domestic creatures. Turkeys and hens were beyond counting; among them a rooster paced with measured steps, shaking his comb and turning his head to one side as if listening for something; a sow with her family turned up there too; right there, raking through a heap of rubbish, she swallowed a chick in passing and, without noticing it, went on devouring watermelon rinds in her usual manner. This small courtyard, or chicken yard, was fenced off by a plank fence, beyond which stretched extensive kitchen gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, beets, and other household vegetables. Here and there throughout the garden were scattered apple trees and other fruit trees, covered with nets to protect them from magpies and sparrows, of which the latter moved in whole slanting clouds from one place to another. For this same purpose several scarecrows had been set up on long poles with outstretched arms; on one of them the landlady's own cap had been placed. Beyond the kitchen gardens came the peasants' cottages, which, though built in a scattered fashion and not laid out in regular streets, showed, as Chichikov observed, the prosperity of their inhabitants, for they were kept up as they should be: the worn planking on the roofs had everywhere been replaced with new; the gates were nowhere warped, and in the covered peasant sheds facing him he noticed here a spare nearly new cart standing, and in some places two. "Her little village is not so small," he said to himself, and resolved on the spot to have a talk and get better acquainted with the landlady. He peeped through the crack of the door from which she had poked her head, and seeing her seated at the tea table, went in to her with a cheerful and amiable air.

— Good morning, my dear sir. How did you sleep? — said the landlady, rising from her seat. She was dressed better than the day before — in a dark gown and no longer in her nightcap, but something was still tied around her neck all the same.

— Well, well, — said Chichikov, settling into an armchair. — And how are you, my dear?

— Poorly, my dear.

— How so?

— Couldn't sleep. My lower back aches all over, and my leg, just above the ankle, keeps throbbing.

— It'll pass, it'll pass, my dear. No need to worry about that.

— God grant it does. I've been rubbing it with lard and soaking it in turpentine too. And what will you have with your tea? There's some fruit liqueur in the flask.

— Not bad at all, my dear, we'll have some of the fruit liqueur.

The reader has, I think, already noticed that Chichikov, despite his amiable manner, spoke with considerably more freedom than he had with Manilov, and stood on no ceremony whatsoever. It must be said that in Russia, if we have not yet caught up with foreigners in some other respects, we have far outstripped them in the art of social intercourse. It is impossible to enumerate all the shades and subtleties of our manner of dealing with people. A Frenchman or a German would never in his life figure out or understand all its peculiarities and distinctions; he would speak in almost the same voice and the same language to a millionaire and to a petty tobacco dealer, though of course he would inwardly grovel to a fitting degree before the former. With us it is different: with us there are such sages who will speak to a landowner with two hundred soulswill speak quite differently than they would with someone who has three hundred, and those with three hundred will speak differently again than with someone who has five hundred, and with someone who has five hundred, differently again than with someone who has eight hundred — in short, even if you go up to a million, you will always find shades of distinction. Let us suppose, for example, that there exists a government office, not here but in some far-off kingdom, and in that office there exists, let us suppose, a head clerk. I ask you to look at him as he sits among his subordinates — why, you would not be able to utter a word for sheer awe! Pride and nobility, and what does his face not express? Simply take a brush and paint him: Prometheus, a veritable Prometheus! He surveys the room like an eagle, moves smoothly, with measured steps. That same eagle, the moment he steps out of the room and approaches his superior's office, scurries along like a partridge with papers tucked under his arm, and there is no keeping up with him. In society and at a party, where everyone is of modest rank, Prometheus remains Prometheus, but let someone of slightly higher rank appear, and Prometheus undergoes a transformation that even Ovid could not have invented: a fly, smaller even than a fly, he has shrunk himself to a grain of sand! "Why, that is not Ivan Petrovich," you say, looking at him. "Ivan Petrovich is taller, but this one is short and thin; that one speaks loudly, in a bass voice, and never laughs, but this one — the devil knows what — squeaks like a bird and laughs at everything." You draw closer and look — it is indeed Ivan Petrovich! "Well, well," you think to yourself… But let us return, however, to our characters. Chichikov, as we have already seen, had resolved to stand on no ceremony whatsoever, and so, taking his cup of tea and pouring some fruit liqueur into it, he began the following conversation:

"You have a fine little village here, my dear. How many souls does it have?"

"The souls in it, my dear man, are close to eighty," said the mistress, "but the trouble is, times are hard, and last year's harvest was so poor that God preserve us."

"And yet the peasants look sturdy enough, and the cottages are solid. And may I ask your surname? I was so distracted… arriving in the middle of the night…"

"Korobochka, collegiate secretary's widow."

"Most grateful to you. And your first name and patronymic?"

"Nastasya Petrovna."

"Nastasya Petrovna? A fine name, Nastasya Petrovna. My own aunt, my mother's sister, is named Nastasya Petrovna."

"And what is your name?" asked the landowner. "You are, I suppose, an assessor?"

"No, my dear," replied Chichikov with a smile, "I am no assessor — I am simply traveling on my own little affairs."

"Ah, so you are a buyer! What a pity, truly, that I sold my honey to the merchants so cheaply — why, you, my dear man, would surely have bought it from me."

"Well, honey is not what I would have bought."

"What else then? Hemp, perhaps? But I have very little hemp at the moment — half a pood, that is all."

"No, my dear, a different sort of goods: tell me, have any of your peasants died?"

"Oh, my dear sir, eighteen souls," said the old woman with a sigh. "And such fine people they were, all of them workers. After that, true enough, new ones were born, but what good are they — all such small fry; and then the assessor came around — pay the poll tax, he says, for each soul. The people are dead, yet you pay as if they were alive. Last week my blacksmith burned up — such a skilled blacksmith he was, and he knew locksmithing too."

"Did you have a fire, my dear?"

"God spared us from such a calamity — a fire would have been far worse; he burned up on his own, my dear man. Something caught fire inside him — he had drunk far too much — and a blue flame came off him, and he smoldered away, smoldered and turned black as coal, yet he was such a wonderfully skilled blacksmith! And now I have no way to go out: there is no one to shoe the horses."

"It is all God's will, my dear!" said Chichikov with a sigh. "Nothing can be said against the wisdom of God… Why not let me have them, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"Who, my dear man?"

"Why, all those who have died."

"But how am I to let you have them?"

"Just like that. Or sell them, if you like. I will give you money for them."

"But how? Do you really mean to dig them up out of the ground?"

Chichikov saw that the old woman had gone too far afield and that it was absolutely necessary to explain the matter to her. In a few words he explained that the transfer or purchase would exist only on paper and that the souls would be registered as though they were still living.

"But what do you want them for?" said the old woman, staring at him wide-eyed.

"That is my own affair."

"But they are dead, after all."— Who says they're alive? That's exactly why they're a loss to you — because they're dead: you're paying for them, and now I'll relieve you of the trouble and the payments. Do you understand? Not only will I relieve you of that, but on top of it I'll give you fifteen rubles. Well, is it clear now?

— I really don't know, — the mistress said slowly. — I've never sold dead ones before.

— Of course not! It would be more of a wonder if you had sold them to someone. Or do you think there's actually some use in them?

— No, I don't think that. What use could there be in them, no use at all. The only thing that troubles me is that they're already dead.

"Well, the woman seems thick-headed!" — Chichikov thought to himself.

— Listen, my dear. Just think it over carefully: — you're ruining yourself, paying the poll tax on him as if he were alive…

— Oh, my dear, don't even speak of it! — the landowner broke in. — Just last week I paid out more than a hundred and fifty. And I greased the assessor's palm too.

— Well, you see, my dear. But now just consider this: you no longer need to grease the assessor's palm, because now I'm paying for them; I am, not you; I'm taking all the obligations upon myself. I'll even register the deed at my own expense, do you understand?

The old woman fell into thought. She could see that the deal did indeed seem profitable, but it was far too new and unheard-of; and so she began to worry greatly that this buyer might somehow swindle her — he had turned up from God knows where, and at nighttime no less.

— So then, my dear, do we have a deal? — said Chichikov.

— Truly, my dear, I've never had occasion to sell dead ones before. Living ones I've let go — just the year before last I gave the archpriest two girls, a hundred rubles each, and he was very grateful, they turned out to be such fine workers: they weave napkins themselves.

— Well, it's not about the living; God be with them. I'm asking about the dead.

— Truly, I'm afraid at first that I might somehow take a loss. Perhaps you, my dear, are deceiving me, and they're worth… they're worth more somehow.

— Listen, my dear… ah, what are you like! What could they possibly be worth? Think about it: it's dust. Do you understand? It's simply dust. You take any worthless, last-resort thing, even a plain rag, and even a rag has a price: at least it can be bought for a paper mill, but this is of no use to anyone. Well, tell me yourself, what use is it?

— That's quite true, really. No use at all; but the one thing that stops me is that they're already dead.

"Look at her, what a blockhead!" — Chichikov said to himself, already beginning to lose patience. "Just try and manage her! The cursed old woman has me in a sweat!" At this point he pulled a handkerchief from his pocket and began wiping the sweat that had indeed broken out on his brow. However, Chichikov was angry in vain: many a respectable man, even a man of state, turns out in practice to be a perfect Korobochka. Once he's got something fixed in his head, there's no overcoming it; however many arguments you present, clear as day, they all bounce off him like a rubber ball bouncing off a wall. Having wiped away the sweat, Chichikov resolved to try whether he could lead her along by some other approach.

— You, my dear, — he said, — either don't want to understand my words, or you're speaking this way on purpose, just to say something… I'm giving you money: fifteen rubles in banknotes. Do you understand? That's money. You won't find it in the street. Come now, tell me, how much did you sell your honey for?

— Twelve rubles a pood.

— You've taken a little sin upon your soul, my dear. You didn't sell it for twelve.

— I swear to God, I did.

— Well, you see. But that's honey, after all. You gathered it, perhaps, for a whole year, with care, with diligence, with trouble; you traveled about, smoked the bees, fed them in the cellar all winter; but dead souls are a matter not of this world. Here you applied no effort of your own — it was God's will that they should depart this world, causing a loss to your household. At this point Chichikov lost all patience entirely, slammed his chair against the floor in frustration, and threatened her with the devil.

Here it is, the interior arrangement: right in the middle a soap dish, behind the soap dish six or seven narrow compartments for razors; then square recesses for a sandbox and an inkwell with a little boat hollowed out between them for quills, sealing wax, and everything of a longer sort; then various compartments with lids and without lids for shorter items, filled with visiting cards, funeral cards, theater tickets, and others kept as mementos. But why dwell so long on Korobochka? Korobochka or Manilova, a thrifty life or an unthrifty one — let them pass! It is a strange thing how the world is arranged: what is cheerful will in an instant turn to sadness if you stand before it too long, and then God knows what thoughts will come into your head. Perhaps you will even begin to think: but really, is Korobochka truly so low on the endless ladder of human perfection? Is the gulf that separates her from her sister truly so great — that sister who is inaccessibly sheltered behind the walls of an aristocratic house with fragrant cast-iron staircases, gleaming with copper, mahogany and carpets, yawning over an unfinished book while awaiting a witty society call, where she will have the chance to shine with her intellect and air her rehearsed thoughts, thoughts that by the laws of fashion occupy the whole town for a week, thoughts not about what is happening in her house and on her estates, tangled and ruined through ignorance of household management, but about what political upheaval is brewing in France, what direction fashionable Catholicism has taken. — Over there, — the girl answered, pointing with her hand.

Although the day was very fine, the ground had become so muddy that the wheels of the carriage, picking it up, were soon covered with it like felt, which considerably weighed down the vehicle; moreover, the soil was clayey and extraordinarily sticky. Both of these things were the reason they could not get out of the country lanes before midday. Without the girl it would have been hard to manage even that, for the roads spread out in all directions like crayfish tipped out of a sack, and Selifan would have had to wander about through no fault of his own. — There's the main road!

— And that building? — asked Selifan.

— A tavern, — said the girl.

— Well, we can find our own way from here, — said Selifan, — go on home.

He stopped and helped her down, muttering through his teeth: "Eh, you black-footed thing!"

Chapter Four

Pulling up to the tavern, Chichikov ordered a stop for two reasons. On one hand, to let the horses rest, and on the other, to have a little something to eat and refresh himself. The author must confess that he greatly envies the appetite and stomach of such people. He has absolutely no admiration for all those grand gentlemen living in Petersburg and Moscow, who spend their time pondering what to eat tomorrow and what dinner to devise for the day after, and who sit down to that dinner only after first swallowing a pill; who gulp down oysters, sea spiders, and other such wonders, and then set off to Karlsbad or the Caucasus. No, those gentlemen have never aroused any envy in him. But gentlemen of the middling sort, who at one station call for ham, at another for suckling pig, at a third for a slice of sturgeon or some baked sausage with onions, and then sit down at table as if nothing were the matter at whatever hour they please,and the sterlet soup with burbot and milt hisses and grumbles between their teeth, chased down with a rassteгай or a kulebyaka stuffed with catfish tail,[ Catfish tail — "the tail of a catfish, all fat." (From N. V. Gogol's notebook.)

] so that even a bystander is seized with appetite — these gentlemen, truly, enjoy an enviable gift from heaven! More than one grand gentleman would sacrifice on the spot half his serfs and half his estates, mortgaged or otherwise, just to have such a stomach as the middling gentleman possesses; but the trouble is that no amount of money, nor any estate, improved or unimproved, can purchase the kind of stomach that belongs to a gentleman of the middling sort.

The dark, weathered wooden inn received Chichikov under its narrow hospitable awning, supported by turned wooden pillars resembling old church candlesticks. The inn was something like a Russian peasant hut, only somewhat larger. Carved ornamental cornices of fresh wood around the windows and under the roof stood out sharply and vividly against its dark walls; on the shutters were painted jugs of flowers.

Having climbed a narrow wooden staircase up into a wide entryway, he was met by a creaking door and a stout old woman in colorful calico, who said: "This way, please!" The room contained all the old familiar fixtures found in every small wooden inn of the kind built in no small number along the roads — namely, a frost-covered samovar, smoothly planed pine walls, a triangular cupboard with teapots and cups in the corner, gilded porcelain eggs before the icons hanging on blue and red ribbons, a cat that had recently given birth, a mirror that showed four eyes instead of two and something like a flat cake instead of a face; and finally, bunches of fragrant herbs and cloves tucked near the icons, dried to such a degree that anyone wishing to smell them could only sneeze and nothing more.

"Do you have suckling pig?" Chichikov asked the woman standing there.

"We do."

"With horseradish and sour cream?"

"With horseradish and sour cream."

"Bring it here!"

The old woman went off to rummage about and brought a plate, a napkin starched so stiff it stood up like dried bark, then a knife with a yellowed bone handle, thin as a penknife, a two-tined fork, and a salt cellar that could not be made to stand straight on the table.

Our hero, as was his custom, immediately struck up a conversation with her and inquired whether she ran the inn herself or had a proprietor, how much income the inn brought in, whether her sons lived with her, whether the eldest son was a bachelor or a married man, what kind of wife he had taken, whether with a large dowry or not, whether the father-in-law had been pleased, and whether he had been cross about receiving too few gifts at the wedding — in short, he left nothing out. It goes without saying that he was curious to learn what landowners were to be found in the surrounding area, and he learned that there were all sorts: Plotin, Pochitaev, Mylnoy, Colonel Cheprakov, Sobakevich. "Ah! Do you know Sobakevich?" he asked, and immediately heard that the old woman knew not only Sobakevich but also Manilov, and that Manilov was more refined than Sobakevich: he would at once order a chicken to be boiled, ask for some veal as well; if there was lamb's liver, he would ask for lamb's liver too, and only taste a little of everything; whereas Sobakevich would order just one thing, but then eat every last bit of it and even ask for more at the same price.

While he was conversing in this manner, eating the suckling pig of which only the last piece remained, the rumble of wheels from an arriving carriage was heard. Glancing out the window, he saw a light chaise drawn by a troika of good horses pull up before the inn. Two men were climbing out of the chaise. One was fair-haired and tall; the other somewhat shorter and dark. The fair-haired one wore a dark blue Hungarian jacket, the dark one simply a striped arkhaluk. In the distance another small carriage trailed along, empty, drawn by some shaggy four-horse team with torn collars and rope harness. The fair-haired one immediately headed up the staircase, while the dark one remained below, feeling around for something in the chaise, talking to a servant, and at the same time waving to the carriage following behind them. His voice seemed somehow vaguely familiar to Chichikov.While he was looking it over, the fair-haired man had already managed to find the door and open it. He was a tall man, lean in the face, or what you might call worn, with reddish mustaches. "Give me a glass too!" said the fair-haired man.

"At the theater one actress, the rascal, sang like a canary! Kuvshinnnikov, who was sitting next to me, says, 'Now there, brother, wouldn't it be nice to have a taste of that little strawberry!' There must have been fifty booths alone, I think. Fenardi spun like a windmill for four hours." — Here he took the glass from the old woman's hands, who bowed low to him in return. "Ah, bring him here!" he cried, seeing Porfiry come in with the puppy. Porfiry was dressed, just like his master, in some sort of quilted cotton jacket, though somewhat greasier.

"Bring him over, put him down on the floor!"

Porfiry put the puppy on the floor, who, stretching out on all four paws, sniffed the ground.

"There's a puppy!" said Nozdrev, picking it up by lifting it with his hand. The puppy let out a rather pitiful whine.

"You didn't do what I told you, though," said Nozdrev, turning to Porfiry and examining the puppy's belly, "you didn't even think to comb him out?"

"No, I did comb him."

"Then why are there fleas?"

"I can't say. Could be they crawled in somehow from the carriage."

"You're lying, lying, you never even thought of combing him; I bet the fool even let his own in. Here, take a look, Chichikov, look at those ears, go on, feel them with your hand."

"What for, I can see well enough: it's a good breed!" answered Chichikov.

"No, go on, feel the ears on purpose!"

Chichikov, to oblige him, felt the ears, adding:

"Yes, it'll be a fine dog."

"And the nose, can you feel how cold it is? Go on, take it in your hand."

Not wishing to offend him, Chichikov took hold of the nose as well, saying:

"A good sense of smell."

"A real pug, that's what it is," continued Nozdrev, "and I confess, I've had my eye on a pug for a long time. Here, Porfiry, take it away!"

Porfiry, taking the puppy by the belly, carried it off to the carriage.

"Listen, Chichikov, you absolutely must come to my place now, it's only five versts, we'll fly there in no time, and then you can go on to Sobakevich if you like."

"Well, why not," thought Chichikov to himself, "I'll stop in at Nozdrev's after all. What makes him worse than the others, he's a man like any other, and he's lost at cards besides. He's clearly up for anything, so maybe I can get something out of him for free."

"All right, let's go," he said, "but mind you don't keep me, my time is precious."

"Well, my dear fellow, now that's the spirit! That's what I like, wait, I'll kiss you for that." — Here Nozdrev and Chichikov kissed. "Splendid: the three of us will ride together!"

"No, please, let me go," said the fair-haired man, "I need to get home."

"Nonsense, nonsense, brother, I won't let you."

"Really, my wife will be cross; besides, you can now move over into their carriage."

"No, no, no! Don't even think about it."

The fair-haired man was one of those people,whose character at first glance seems to have a certain stubbornness. Before you can even open your mouth, they are already ready to argue and seem as though they will never agree to anything clearly opposed to their way of thinking, that they will never call a fool wise and especially that they will never dance to someone else's tune; and it always ends with their character revealing a certain softness, that they agree to the very thing they had rejected, call the foolish wise, and then go dancing to someone else's tune as well as could be — in short, they start out smooth and end up rough.

— Nonsense! — said Nozdrev in response to some remark from the fair-haired one, put a cap on his head, and the fair-haired one set off after them.

— You haven't paid for the vodka, sir… — said the old woman.

— Ah, right, right, my dear. Listen, son-in-law! pay her, please. I haven't a kopeck in my pocket.

— How much do you need? — said the son-in-law.

— Why, just a twenty-kopeck piece, dear sir, — said the old woman.

— You're lying, you're lying. Give her fifty kopecks,[ A twenty-kopeck piece (in silver) was eighty kopecks in banknotes. Fifty kopecks — fifty kopecks in banknotes.

] that's more than enough for her.

— It's a bit little, sir, — said the old woman, yet she took the money with gratitude and even rushed to open the door for them. She was not at a loss, since she had asked four times what the vodka was worth.

The travelers settled in. Chichikov's carriage rode alongside the carriage in which Nozdrev and his son-in-law were sitting, and so all three of them could freely converse with one another along the way. Behind them followed, constantly falling behind, Nozdrev's small carriage drawn by scrawny hired horses. In it sat Porfiry with the puppy.

Since the conversation the travelers were having among themselves was not very interesting to the reader, it would be better if we said something about Nozdrev himself, who may yet play a not entirely minor role in our poem.

Nozdrev's face is surely already somewhat familiar to the reader. Everyone has had occasion to meet quite a few people like him. They are called lively fellows, known even in childhood and at school as good companions, and for all that they are very painfully thrashed. Their faces always show something open, straightforward, and daring. They make acquaintances quickly, and before you can look around they are already addressing you informally. They seem to strike up friendships for life: but it almost always happens that whoever befriends them ends up in a fight with them that very evening at a friendly gathering. They are always talkers, revelers, daredevils, conspicuous people. Nozdrev at thirty-five was exactly the same as he had been at eighteen and twenty: a lover of a good time. His marriage changed him not in the least, especially since his wife soon departed for the next world, leaving behind two little children who were decidedly of no use to him. The children were looked after, however, by a pretty nursemaid. He could never sit at home for more than a day. His keen nose could smell from dozens of miles away where there was a fair with all its gatherings and balls; he was there in the blink of an eye, arguing and stirring up confusion at the card table, for he had, like all such people, a little passion for cards. At cards, as we have already seen from the first chapter, he played not entirely without sin and in a clean manner, knowing many different tricks and other subtleties, and therefore the game very often ended with another kind of game: either they would beat him with their boots, or they would give a good working-over to his thick and very fine side-whiskers, so that he sometimes returned home with only one side-whisker, and that a rather thin one. But his healthy and full cheeks were so well made and contained so much vital force that the side-whiskers would soon grow back again, even better than before. And what is strangest of all, what could only happen in Russia alone, he would after a short time meet again with those very friends who had thrashed him, and meet them as though nothing had happened, and he, as they say, thought nothing of it, and they thought nothing of it.

Nozdrev was in a certain respect a historic figure. Not a single gathering he attended passed without incident. Some kind of story would inevitably occur: either the gendarmes would escort him out of the hall by the arms, or his own friends would be compelled to push him out.If that doesn't happen, something else will occur that wouldn't happen to anyone else: either he'll get so drunk at the buffet that he can only laugh, or he'll tell such outrageous lies that he ends up feeling ashamed himself. And he'll lie for absolutely no reason at all: he'll suddenly claim he had a horse with blue or pink fur, or some such nonsense, until all the listeners finally drift away, saying: "Well, brother, it seems you've started spinning yarns." There are people who have a little passion for making trouble for their neighbors, sometimes for no reason whatsoever. One man, for instance, even a man of rank, with a distinguished appearance, a star on his chest, will shake your hand, engage you in conversation on deep subjects that provoke reflection, and then, right before your eyes, he'll do you dirty. And he'll do it just like a simple collegiate registrar, not at all like a man with a star on his chest who talks about subjects that provoke reflection, so that you can only stand there in amazement, shrugging your shoulders, and nothing more. Nozdrev had this same strange passion. The closer someone got to him, the sooner he would do them wrong: he'd spread a rumor more foolish than anything you could invent, ruin a wedding, a business deal, and not consider himself your enemy at all; on the contrary, if chance brought him to meet you again, he'd treat you in a friendly manner once more and even say: "You're such a scoundrel, you never come to visit me." Nozdrev was in many respects a many-sided man, that is, a jack of all trades. At a moment's notice he'd propose going anywhere you liked, even to the ends of the earth, entering into any enterprise you wished, trading anything for anything. A rifle, a dog, a horse — everything was fair game for a trade, but not at all with the aim of gaining: it came simply from a kind of restless quickness and liveliness of character. If at a fair he was lucky enough to come across a simpleton and fleece him, he'd buy up a heap of everything that had caught his eye in the shops: horse collars, wax candles, kerchiefs for the nanny, a stallion, raisins, a silver washbasin, Dutch linen, fine flour, tobacco, pistols, herring, paintings, a sharpening tool, pots, boots, faience crockery — as far as his money would go. As a rule, it rarely happened that any of this made it home; almost that same day it would all be passed on to a luckier gambler, sometimes with his own pipe thrown in along with the tobacco pouch and mouthpiece, and other times even the whole four-in-hand with everything: the carriage and the coachman, so that the master himself would set off in a short little frock coat or an arkhaluk in search of some friend to borrow a ride from. Such was Nozdrev! Some may call him a worn-out character and say that there are no more Nozdrevs nowadays. Alas! Those who say so will be wrong. Nozdrev will not disappear from the world for a long time yet. He is everywhere among us and perhaps only wears a different coat; but people are frivolously unperceptive, and a man in a different coat seems to them a different man.

Meanwhile, three carriages had already pulled up to the porch of Nozdrev's house. No preparations had been made inside to receive them. In the middle of the dining room stood wooden sawhorses, and two peasants standing on them were whitewashing the walls, singing some endless song; the floor was all splattered with whitewash. Nozdrev immediately ordered the peasants and the sawhorses out and ran into another room to give instructions. The guests could hear him ordering dinner from the cook; taking this into account, Chichikov, who was already beginning to feel somewhat hungry, realized they would not sit down to eat before five o'clock. Nozdrev, upon returning, led the guests to inspect everything there was to see on his estate, and in just over two hours had shown them absolutely everything, so that there was nothing left to show. First they went to look over the stables, where they saw two mares, one dapple gray and the other a light bay, then a bay stallion, unremarkable in appearance, but for which Nozdrev swore he had paid ten thousand.

"You didn't pay ten thousand for him," remarked the brother-in-law. "He isn't worth even one."

"I swear to God, I paid ten thousand,"— said Nozdrev.

— You can swear all you want, — replied the brother-in-law.

— Well, do you want to make a bet! — said Nozdrev.

The brother-in-law had no desire to make a bet.

Then Nozdrev showed them the empty stalls, where previously there had also been fine horses. In this same stable they saw a goat, which, according to an old superstition, was considered necessary to keep among horses, and which seemed to be on good terms with them, wandering under their bellies as if at home. Then Nozdrev led them to look at a wolf cub that was kept on a leash. "Here's the wolf cub!" he said. "I feed him raw meat on purpose. I want him to be a proper wild beast!" They went to look at a pond, in which, according to Nozdrev, fish of such a size were found that two men could barely haul one out, something which the relative did not fail to doubt. "I'll show you, Chichikov, — said Nozdrev, — a splendid pair of dogs: the firmness of their black flesh is simply astonishing, the chest — like a needle!" — and he led them to a neat little house, very prettily built, surrounded by a large yard enclosed on all sides. Entering the yard, they saw all manner of dogs, both shaggy and smooth-coated, of every possible color and marking: tawny, black-and-tan, fallow-spotted, tawny-spotted, red-spotted, black-eared, grey-eared… Every sort of name was there, every imperative form: Strelyay, Obrugay, Porkhai, Pozhar, Skosyr, Cherkay, Dopekay, Pripekay, Severga, Kasatka, Nagrada, Popechitelnitsa. Nozdrev was among them entirely like a father among his family; all of them, immediately throwing up their tails, known among dog-keepers as pravila, flew straight toward the guests and began to greet them. Some ten of them put their paws on Nozdrev's shoulders. Obrugay showed the same affection to Chichikov and, rising up on his hind legs, licked him right on the lips with his tongue, so that Chichikov immediately spat. They inspected the dogs, whose firmness of black flesh was astonishing — fine dogs they were. Then they went to inspect a Crimean bitch, who was already blind and, according to Nozdrev, was soon to die, but two years ago had been a very good dog; they inspected the bitch — the bitch was indeed blind. Then they went to inspect a water mill, which was missing the rynd, in which the upper millstone is fixed, spinning rapidly on the spindle — "fluttering," in the wonderful expression of the Russian peasant.

— And here soon there will be a smithy! — said Nozdrev. Having walked a little further, they did indeed see a smithy, and inspected the smithy as well.

— Out on this field, — said Nozdrev, pointing his finger at the field, — there are so many hares you can't see the ground; I myself caught one with my own hands by its hind legs.

— Well, you can't catch a hare with your hands! — remarked the brother-in-law.

— But I did catch one, caught it on purpose! — replied Nozdrev. — Now I'll take you to see, — he continued, turning to Chichikov, — the boundary where my land ends.

Nozdrev led his guests across a field that in many places consisted of tussocks. The guests had to make their way between fallow strips and harrowed fields. Chichikov was beginning to feel tired. In many places their feet pressed water out from under them, so low-lying was the ground. At first they tried to be careful and stepped cautiously, but then, seeing that it was of no use, they waded straight ahead without distinguishing where the mud was deeper and where shallower. Having walked a considerable distance, they did indeed see the boundary, which consisted of a wooden post and a narrow ditch.

— Here is the boundary! — said Nozdrev. — Everything you see on this side is all mine, and even on that side, all that forest over there that shows blue in the distance, and everything beyond the forest, all of it is mine.

— But when did that forest become yours? — asked the brother-in-law. — Did you buy it recently? It wasn't yours before.

— Yes, I bought it recently, — replied Nozdrev.

— When did you manage to buy it so quickly?

— Why, I bought it just the day before yesterday, and paid a lot for it, devil take it.

— But you were at the fair at that time.

— Oh, Sofron! Can't one be at the fair and buy land at the same time? Well, I was at the fair, and my steward here bought it without me.

— Well, if it was the steward! — said the brother-in-law, but even here he doubted and shook his head.The guests returned by the same wretched road to the house. Nozdrev led them into his study, which showed none of the usual signs of a study — no books or papers — only sabers hanging on the walls and two rifles, one worth three hundred rubles and the other eight hundred. The brother-in-law looked them over and merely shook his head. Then Turkish daggers were produced, one of which had been mistakenly engraved with the words: "Master Saveliy Sibiryakov." After that, a barrel organ was brought out for the guests. Nozdrev cranked it for them on the spot. The organ played not unpleasantly, but somewhere in the middle something seemed to go wrong, for the mazurka ended with the tune "Marlborough Has Gone to War," and "Marlborough Has Gone to War" unexpectedly dissolved into some long-familiar waltz. Nozdrev had long since stopped cranking, but one pipe inside the organ was particularly lively and refused to settle down, so it went on whistling by itself for quite some time. Then pipes were brought out — wooden ones, clay ones, meerschaum ones, smoked and unsmoked, suede-covered and plain, a pipe stem with an amber mouthpiece recently won in a wager, and a tobacco pouch embroidered by some countess who had fallen head over heels for him at a coaching inn somewhere, whose little hands, by his account, were of the most "subditive superflew" — a word that in his usage apparently signified the highest degree of perfection. After a snack of dried sturgeon, they sat down to table around five o'clock. Dinner, as was evident, did not occupy the central place in Nozdrev's life; the dishes were not of great importance: some things were burnt, some not cooked through at all. Clearly the cook was guided more by a kind of inspiration and threw in whatever came to hand first: if pepper happened to be nearby, he tossed in pepper; if cabbage turned up, he stuffed in cabbage; he crammed in milk, ham, peas — in short, whatever came along, as long as it was hot, and some sort of flavor would surely come out of it. Nozdrev, however, made up for it with wine: before the soup had even been served, he had already poured his guests a large glass each of port and another of Sauterne — called "gosoternes" since plain Sauternes was not to be found in provincial and district towns. Then Nozdrev ordered a bottle of Madeira brought in, the like of which no field marshal himself had ever drunk. The Madeira did indeed burn the mouth, for the merchants, well acquainted with the taste of landowners who liked a good Madeira, laced it mercilessly with rum, and sometimes poured in aqua regia as well, trusting that Russian stomachs would endure anything. Then Nozdrev ordered yet another special bottle brought out, which he claimed was both Burgundy and Champagne in one. He poured very zealously into both glasses, to the right and to the left, for the brother-in-law and for Chichikov alike; Chichikov noticed, however, in passing, that Nozdrev did not add much to his own glass. This put him on his guard, and whenever Nozdrev got distracted in conversation or was pouring for the brother-in-law, he immediately tipped his own glass into his plate. Before long, a rowan-berry liqueur was brought to the table which, according to Nozdrev, tasted perfectly of cream, but in which, to everyone's astonishment, raw spirits could be detected in full force. Then they drank some sort of balsam with a name so difficult to remember that even the host himself called it by a different name the second time around. Dinner had long since ended and the wines had all been sampled, yet the guests still sat at the table. Chichikov had no wish to bring up the main subject with Nozdrev in the brother-in-law's presence. After all, the brother-in-law was an outsider, and the matter called for a private and friendly conversation. The brother-in-law, however, was hardly likely to be a dangerous man, for he had apparently had his fill and, sitting in his chair, was nodding off every minute. Noticing himself that he was not in a reliable condition, he finally began asking to be let go home, but in such a lazy and sluggish voice that it was as if, in the Russian expression, he were pulling a horse collar onto a horse with pincers.

— Not a chance! I won't let you go! — said Nozdrev.

"No, don't take offense, my friend, I really must go," said the son-in-law, "you'll greatly offend me if I stay."

"Nonsense, nonsense! We'll set up a little bank game this very minute."

"No, set it up yourself, brother, I can't, my wife will have a great grievance against me, I really must tell her about the fair. It's necessary, brother, truly necessary, I must give her that pleasure. No, don't hold me back!"

"To hell with your wife! What an important thing you'll be doing together!"

"No, brother! She's such a respectable and faithful woman! The services she renders… you'd believe it, I have tears in my eyes. No, don't hold me back; as an honest man, I must go. I assure you of this in all good conscience."

"Let him go, what good is he!" said Chichikov quietly to Nozdrev.

"And indeed!" said Nozdrev. "I can't stand such milksops!" and added aloud: "Well, the devil take you, go home and coddle your wife, you feeble thing![ Feeble thing — a word offensive to a man, derived from Fita — a letter considered by some to be an indecent letter. (Note by N. V. Gogol.) 

]

"No, brother, don't call me a feeble thing," replied the son-in-law, "I owe her my very life. She's truly so kind, so dear, shows such affection… it moves me to tears; she'll ask what I saw at the fair, I must tell her everything, she's truly so dear."

"Well, go on, talk nonsense to her! Here's your cap."

"No, brother, you really shouldn't speak of her that way; by doing so you're, one might say, offending me personally, she's so dear."

"Well then, get yourself to her quickly!"

"Yes, brother, I'll go, forgive me for not being able to stay. I'd be glad with all my soul, but I can't."

The son-in-law went on repeating his apologies for a long time, not noticing that he had long since been sitting in his carriage, had long since driven out through the gates, and that nothing but empty fields lay before him. One must suppose that his wife heard very few details about the fair.

"What rubbish!" said Nozdrev, standing at the window and watching the departing carriage. "Look how he's dragging along! The off-horse isn't bad, I've long wanted to get my hands on it. But there's simply no dealing with him. A feeble thing, plain and simple!"

After that they went into the room. Porfiry brought candles, and Chichikov noticed in the host's hands a deck of cards that had appeared from nowhere.

"So what do you say, brother," said Nozdrev, squeezing the sides of the deck with his fingers and bending it slightly so that it cracked and a bit of paper flew off. "Well, just to pass the time, I'll bank three hundred rubles!"

But Chichikov pretended as though he hadn't heard what was being said, and spoke as if suddenly remembering:

"Oh! So I don't forget: I have a request to make of you."

"What is it?"

"First give me your word that you'll fulfill it."

"But what is the request?"

"Well, give me your word!"

"Very well."

"Your word of honor?"

"My word of honor."

"Here is the request: you must have quite a few dead serfs who haven't yet been struck from the census rolls?"

"Well, I do, so what?"

"Transfer them to me, in my name."

"What do you need them for?"

"Well, I just need them."

"But what for?"

"Well, I just need them… it's my own business, in short, I need them."

"Well, you're surely up to something. Admit it, what?"

"What do you mean, up to something? There's nothing to be up to with such a trifle."

"But why do you need them?"

"Oh, what a curious fellow! He wants to poke at every bit of rubbish with his finger and give it a sniff too!"

"But why won't you say?"

"What profit is it to you to know? Well, it's simply a whim that came to me."

"Then here's the thing: I won't do it until you tell me!"

"Now you see, that's not honest of you: you gave your word and now you're backing out."

"Well, do as you like, but I won't do it until you say what for."

"What could I tell him?" thought Chichikov, and after a moment's reflection announced that the dead souls were needed to give him more standing in society, that he had no large estates, so for the time being at least some souls of any kind would do.

"You're lying, lying!" said Nozdrev, not letting him finish. "You're lying, brother!"

Chichikov himself noticed that he hadn't come up with something very clever and that the pretext was rather weak.

"Well then, I'll tell you more plainly," he said, correcting himself, "only please don't breathe a word to anyone. I've been thinking of getting married; but you should know that the bride's father and mother are exceedingly ambitious people. What a business it is, truly: I'm sorry I got mixed up in it, they absolutely insist that the groom have no fewer than three hundred souls, and since I'm short by nearly a hundred and fifty serfs…"

"You're lying! lying!"— cried Nozdrev again.

— Well, right here, — said Chichikov, — I haven't lied even this much, — and he showed with his thumb on his little finger the tiniest possible amount.

— I'd bet my head you're lying!

— But that's offensive! what am I really! why must I necessarily be lying?

— Well, I know you: you're a great scoundrel, allow me to tell you that as a friend! If I were your superior, I'd hang you from the first tree.

Chichikov was offended by such a remark. Any expression that was even slightly crude or offensive to decency was unpleasant to him. He even disliked allowing familiar treatment with himself under any circumstances, unless the person was of very high rank. And so now he was completely offended.

— By God, I'd hang you, — repeated Nozdrev, — I'm telling you this frankly, not to offend you, but simply speaking as a friend.

— Everything has its limits, — said Chichikov with a sense of dignity. — If you want to show off with such talk, then go to the barracks, — and then added: — If you don't want to give them as a gift, then sell them.

— Sell them! But I know you, you scoundrel, you won't give much for them?

— Come on, you're quite something yourself! look at you! are they made of diamonds or what?

— Well, there you have it. I knew you already.

— Good heavens, brother, what a miserly impulse you have. You ought to simply give them to me.

— Well, listen, to prove to you that I'm not some kind of miser, I won't take anything for them. Buy a stallion from me, and I'll throw them in as a bonus.

— Good heavens, what do I need a stallion for? — said Chichikov, genuinely astonished by such a proposal.

— What do you mean, what for? I paid ten thousand for him, and I'm letting you have him for four.

— But what do I need a stallion for? I don't run a stud farm.

— But listen, you don't understand: I'll only take three thousand from you now, and you can pay me the remaining thousand later.

— I don't need a stallion, God be with him!

— Well, buy the bay mare.

— I don't need a mare either.

— For the mare and the grey horse you saw at my place, I'll only take two thousand from you.

— I don't need any horses.

— You'll sell them, at the first fair they'll give you three times as much for them.

— Then you'd better sell them yourself, since you're sure you'll gain three times over.

— I know I'll gain, but I want you to profit as well.

Chichikov thanked him for the goodwill and flatly refused both the grey horse and the bay mare.

— Well then, buy some dogs. I'll sell you such a pair, it simply sends chills down your spine! shaggy, with whiskers, fur standing up like bristles. The barrel-roundness of the ribs is beyond comprehension, the paw all bunched up, won't even touch the ground.

— What do I need dogs for? I'm not a hunter.

— But I want you to have dogs. Listen, if you really don't want dogs, then buy a barrel organ from me, a wonderful barrel organ; it cost me, as an honest man, fifteen hundred, I'm letting you have it for nine hundred rubles.

— But what do I need a barrel organ for? I'm not a German, to drag it along the roads begging for money.

— But this isn't the kind of barrel organ Germans carry around. This is an organ; take a look at it on purpose: all made of mahogany. Here, let me show it to you! — Here Nozdrev, grabbing Chichikov by the hand, began dragging him into another room, and no matter how much the latter dug his feet into the floor and insisted that he already knew what kind of organ it was, he was made to hear once more how Marlborough went off to war. — Since you don't want to pay money, here's what: listen: I'll give you the barrel organ and all the dead souls I have, and you give me your carriage and three hundred rubles to boot.

— Well, that's something, and what am I supposed to travel in?

— I'll give you another carriage. Come on, let's go to the shed, I'll show it to you! You just repaint it, and it'll be a wonderful carriage.

"What a restless devil has gotten into him!" — Chichikov thought to himself and resolved at all costs to rid himself of all carriages, barrel organs, and every possible dog, despite the incomprehensible barrel-roundness of the ribs and the bunched-up paws.

— But it's the carriage, the barrel organ, and the dead souls, all together!

— I don't want it, — said Chichikov once more.

— But why don't you want it?

— Because I simply don't want it, and that's that.

— You really are something! with you, as I can see, it's impossible,as is customary between good friends and comrades, such a one, really!.. You can see right away that he's a two-faced man!

— Am I a fool or something? Think for yourself: why would I acquire something I absolutely don't need?

— Oh, please, don't talk like that. I know you very well now. Such a rascal, really! Well, listen, do you want to play a hand of faro? I'll stake all the dead souls on a card, and the barrel organ too.

— Well, to risk it at faro means to expose oneself to uncertainty, — said Chichikov, while glancing sidelong at the cards in his hands. Both decks seemed to him very much like marked ones, and the very pattern on the backs looked highly suspicious.

— What uncertainty? — said Nozdrev. — No uncertainty at all! If luck is on your side, you can win a devil of a lot. There it is! what luck! — he said, beginning to deal to stir up some excitement. — What luck! what luck! look: it just keeps hitting! there's that cursed nine that cost me everything! I felt it would betray me, but already, squinting my eyes shut, I think to myself: "The devil take you, betray me, you cursed card!"

While Nozdrev was saying this, Porfiry brought a bottle. But Chichikov flatly refused both to play and to drink.

— Why don't you want to play? — said Nozdrev.

— Well, because I'm not in the mood. And, to tell the truth, I'm simply not a gambling man.

— Why not a gambling man?

Chichikov shrugged his shoulders and added:

— Because I'm just not.

— You're worthless!

— What can one do? that's how God made me.

— A milksop, plain and simple! I thought before that you were at least somewhat a decent person, but you have no idea how to behave. There's no talking to you as to someone close… no straightforwardness, no sincerity! A perfect Sobakevich, such a scoundrel!

— Why are you scolding me? Is it my fault that I don't play? Sell me the souls alone, if you're such a person that you tremble over this nonsense.

— You'll get nothing of the sort! I was going to give them away for free, but now you won't get them! Even if you offered three kingdoms, I wouldn't give them up. Such a swindler, a nasty chimney sweep! From this day on I want nothing to do with you. Porfiry, go tell the stableman not to give his horses any oats, let them eat nothing but hay.

This last conclusion Chichikov had not expected at all.

— Better you'd never shown your face to me! — said Nozdrev.

Despite such a quarrel, however, the guest and host had supper together, though this time there were no wines with fanciful names on the table. There stood only a single bottle of some sort of Cypriot wine that was what one might call sour in every respect. After supper Nozdrev said to Chichikov, leading him into a side room where a bed had been prepared for him:

— Here's your bed! I don't even want to wish you good night!

Chichikov remained after Nozdrev's departure in a most disagreeable state of mind. He was inwardly vexed with himself, scolded himself for having stopped in and wasted his time for nothing. But he scolded himself even more for having brought up the matter with him, for having acted carelessly, like a child, like a fool: for the business was not at all of the sort to be entrusted to Nozdrev… Nozdrev is a worthless man, Nozdrev might lie, exaggerate, spread God knows what, some gossip might come of it — bad, bad. "I'm simply a fool," he said to himself. He slept very badly that night. Some small but exceedingly lively insects bit him unbearably painfully, so that he scratched the bitten spots with his whole fist, muttering: "Oh, the devil take you along with Nozdrev!" He woke early in the morning. His first order of business was, having put on his dressing gown and boots, to go across the yard to the stable to order Selifan to harness the chaise at once. Returning across the yard, he ran into Nozdrev, who was also in a dressing gown, with a pipe between his teeth.

Nozdrev greeted him in a friendly manner and asked how he had slept.

— So-so, — answered Chichikov very dryly.

— And I, brother, — said Nozdrev, — such vile things kept crawling into my head all night that it's disgusting to tell, and my mouth after yesterday feels exactly like a squadron spent the night in it. Just imagine: I dreamed that I was flogged, honestly! and imagine, by whom? You'd never guess: Staff-Captain Potseluev together with Kuvshinnikov.

"Yes,— thought Chichikov to himself, — it would be nice if you got a real thrashing in real life».

— Honest to God! and it hurt quite a bit! Woke up: damn it, something really is itching — must be the fleas, the little witches. Now go get dressed, I'll be with you in a moment. I just need to give that scoundrel of a steward a good scolding.

Chichikov went to his room to dress and wash up. When he came out to the dining room afterward, the tea service was already set on the table along with a bottle of rum. The room still bore traces of yesterday's dinner and supper; it seemed the floor brush had not been touched at all. Bread crumbs lay scattered on the floor, and tobacco ash was visible even on the tablecloth. The host himself, who was not long in appearing, had nothing under his robe except his bare chest, on which grew some kind of beard. Holding a pipe in his hand and sipping from his cup, he was a fine subject for a painter who has no great love for those sleek and curled gentlemen who look like barber-shop signs, or those cropped with a comb.

— Well, so what do you think? — said Nozdrev, after a brief silence. — Don't you want to play for the souls?

— I've already told you, brother, I don't gamble; to buy — by all means, I'll buy.

— I don't want to sell, that wouldn't be very friendly. I'm not going to skim the cream off God knows what. A card game — that's another matter. Let's play at least a hand!

— I've already said no.

— And you don't want to trade?

— I don't.

— Well, listen, let's play checkers, if you win they're all yours. I've got quite a few who need to be struck from the census rolls. Hey, Porfiry, bring the checkerboard over here.

— It's no use, I won't play.

— But this isn't faro; there's no luck or trickery involved here: it's all skill; I'll even warn you that I can't play at all, unless you give me some kind of head start.

«Let me — thought Chichikov to himself — play checkers with him! I used to play checkers rather well, and it'll be hard for him to pull any tricks here».

— Very well, so be it, I'll play checkers.

— The souls are worth a hundred rubles!

— What for? Fifty would be enough.

— No, what kind of a stake is fifty? Better yet, I'll throw in some middling sort of pup for that sum, or a gold watch seal.

— All right then! — said Chichikov.

— So how much of a head start will you give me? — said Nozdrev.

— What on earth for? Of course, nothing.

— At least let me have two moves.

— I don't want to, I play poorly myself.

— We know how poorly you play! — said Nozdrev, moving a checker.

— It's been a while since I've touched checkers! — said Chichikov, also moving a piece.

— We know how poorly you play! — said Nozdrev, moving a checker.

— It's been a while since I've touched checkers! — said Chichikov, moving a checker.

— We know how poorly you play! — said Nozdrev, moving a checker, and at the very same moment pushed another one forward with his cuff.

— It's been a while since I've touched them!.. Hey, hey! what's this, brother? Put it back! — said Chichikov.

— Which one?

— That checker, — said Chichikov, and at the same moment spotted another one right before his nose which seemed to be making its way toward the king's row; where it had come from only God knew. — No, — said Chichikov, rising from the table, — there's absolutely no way to play with you! That's not how you move, three checkers at once!

— Why three? That was a mistake. One moved by accident, I'll push it back, go ahead.

— And where did the other one come from?

— Which other one?

— That one there, making its way to the king's row?

— Well I never, as if you don't remember!

— No, brother, I counted every move and remember them all; you only just put it there. Its place is over there!

— How do you mean, over there? — said Nozdrev, turning red. — You, brother, are quite the storyteller, I see!

— No, brother, it seems you're the storyteller, and not a very good one at that.

— Who do you take me for? — said Nozdrev. — Would I really cheat?

— I don't take you for anyone in particular, but I simply will never play with you again from this point on.

— No, you can't back out, — said Nozdrev, growing heated, — the game has started!

— I have the right to back out, because you don't play the way that befits an honest man.

— No, you're lying, you can't say that!

— No, brother, you're the one who's lying!

— I didn't cheat, and you can't back out, you must finish the game!

— You won't make me do that, — said Chichikov coolly, and,approaching the checkerboard, mixed up the pieces.

Nozdrev flared up and stepped so close to Chichikov that the latter backed away a couple of paces.

"I'll make you play! It doesn't matter that you mixed up the pieces, I remember every move. We'll put them back the way they were."

"No, brother, it's over, I won't play with you."

"So you don't want to play?"

"You can see for yourself that it's impossible to play with you."

"No, tell me straight out, you don't want to play?" said Nozdrev, pressing even closer.

"I don't want to!" said Chichikov, and raised both hands closer to his face just in case, for things were getting genuinely heated.

This precaution was well placed, because Nozdrev swung his arm… and it could very well have happened that one of our hero's pleasant, full cheeks would have been covered with indelible disgrace; but, having luckily deflected the blow, he seized Nozdrev by both of his rowdy hands and held him firmly.

"Porfiry, Pavlushka!" cried Nozdrev in a frenzy, struggling to break free.

Hearing these words, Chichikov, so as not to make the household serfs witnesses to a scandalous scene, and at the same time feeling that holding Nozdrev was pointless, let go of his hands. At that very moment Porfiry entered, and with him Pavlushka, a sturdy lad with whom it was altogether unwise to tangle.

"So you don't want to finish the game?" said Nozdrev. "Answer me straight!"

"There's no way to finish the game," said Chichikov, and glanced out the window. He saw his carriage standing fully ready, with Selifan apparently waiting for a signal to pull up to the porch, but there was no way to get out of the room: two sturdy serf blockheads stood in the doorway.

"So you don't want to finish the game?" Nozdrev repeated, his face burning as if on fire.

"If you played the way an honest man should. But now I cannot."

"Ah! so you cannot, you scoundrel! When you saw things weren't going your way, suddenly you cannot! Beat him!" he cried frantically, turning to Porfiry and Pavlushka, while he himself grabbed a cherrywood pipe stem. Chichikov went pale as a sheet. He wanted to say something, but felt that his lips were moving without a sound.
