VOLUME ONE

Chapter One

Through the gate of the hotel in the provincial town of NN drove a rather beautiful spring-loaded little carriage, in which bachelors travel: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landlords, who had about a hundred peasants - in short, all those who are called gentlemen of the middle class. In the carriage sat a gentleman, not handsome, but neither of bad appearance, neither too fat nor too thin; it cannot be said that he is old, but not that he is too young. His entry made no noise in the city at all, and was not accompanied by anything special; only two Russian peasants, who were standing at the door of the tavern opposite the hotel, made some remarks, which, however, applied more to the carriage than to the one sitting in it. what do you think, will that wheel, if it happens, reach Moscow or not?" "And I don't think he will get to Kazan?" - "He won't get to Kazan," answered another. And when the carriage drove up to the hotel, there was a young man in white rosin pantaloons, very narrow and short, in a tailcoat with attempts at fashion, from under which one could see a shirt front, fastened with a Tula pin with a bronze pistol. The young man turned back, looked at the carriage, held his cap in his hand, which was almost blown away by the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage drove into the courtyard, the gentleman was met by the innkeeper, or polovoy, as they are called in Russian taverns, alive and fidgety to such an extent that it was impossible even to see what kind of face he had. He ran out nimbly, napkin in hand, all long and in a long demicotone frock coat with a back almost at the back of his head, shook his hair and led the gentleman nimbly up the entire wooden gallery to show him the peace sent to him by God. The peace was of a certain kind, for the inn was also of a certain kind, that is, just like there are hotels in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day the passers-by get a quiet room with cockroaches peeping out like prunes from all corners, and a door to the next room, always filled with a chest of drawers, where the neighbor, a silent and calm man, but extremely curious, settles down.  interested in knowing all the details of the passerby. The exterior façade of the hotel corresponded to its interior: it was very long, two stories high; the lower one was not carved out and remained covered with dark red bricks, darkened even more by the dashing weather changes and dirty in themselves; the upper one was painted with eternal yellow paint; downstairs there were benches with clamps, ropes and bagels. In one of these charcoal shops, or rather in the window, there was a sbitenshchik with a samovar of red copper and a face as red as a samovar, so that from a distance one might have thought that there were two samovars on the window, if one of the samovars had not been with a pitch-black beard.

While the visiting gentleman was inspecting his room, his belongings were brought in: first of all, a suitcase of white leather, somewhat tattered, showing that it was not the first time he had been on the road. The suitcase was brought in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a sheepskin coat, and the footman Petrushka, a small man of about thirty, in a spacious second-hand frock coat, as can be seen from the lord's shoulder, a little stern in appearance, with very large lips and nose. Following the suitcase, a small mahogany chest with piece layouts of Karelian birch, shoemaker's pads and fried chicken wrapped in blue paper were brought. When all this had been brought in, Selifan the coachman went to the stable to take care of the horses, and Petrushka, the footman, began to make himself comfortable in a small, very dark kennel, where he had already dragged his greatcoat and with it some smell of his own, which was communicated to the bag of various footmen's toilet that had been brought after that. In this kennel he fastened a narrow three-legged bed to the wall, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, dead and flat as a pancake, and perhaps as dirty as the pancake he had managed to extort from the innkeeper.

While the servants were managing and busying, the master went to the common room. Every passer-by knows very well what these common halls are: the same walls, painted with oil paint, darkened at the top by pipe smoke and glossed below by the backs of various passers-by, and still more by native merchants, for merchants came here on trading days to drink their well-known pair of tea; the same sooty ceiling; the same smoky chandelier with a multitude of hanging glasses that jumped and jingled every time the policeman ran over the worn-out oilcloths, waving briskly a tray on which sat the same abyss of teacups as birds on the seashore; the same paintings all over the wall, painted with oil paints — in a word, everything is the same as everywhere else; The only difference was that one picture depicted a nymph with such huge breasts as the reader has never seen. Such a play of nature, however, happens in various historical paintings, at no one knows at what time, from where and by whom they were brought to Russia, sometimes even by our nobles, lovers of art, who bought them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who brought them. The gentleman threw off his cap and unwound from his neck a woollen kerchief of rainbow colors, such as a married wife prepares with her own hands, providing decent instructions on how to wrap herself up, and for bachelors—I probably cannot say who makes them, God knows, I have never worn such kerchiefs. Unwinding his kerchief, the gentleman ordered dinner to be served. In the meantime, he was served various dishes usual in taverns, such as: cabbage soup with a puff pastry, specially saved for travelers for several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, fried pulyarka, pickled cucumber and the eternal puff sweet pie, always ready for service; While all this was served to him, both warm and cold, he forced the servant, or the policeman, to tell all sorts of nonsense, about who had formerly kept the inn and who was now, and whether he made much money, and whether their master was a great scoundrel; to which the policeman, as usual, answered: "Oh, great, sir, rascal." Both in enlightened Europe and in enlightened Russia there are now very many respectable people who cannot eat in a tavern without talking to a servant, and sometimes even making fun of him. However, the stranger did not ask all empty questions; he asked with extreme precision who was the governor in the city, who was the chairman of the chamber, who was the prosecutor — in short, he did not miss a single important official; but with even greater precision, if not with sympathy, he asked about all the important landowners: how many peasants they had, how far they lived from the city, what kind of character they had, and how often they came to the city; I asked attentively about the state of the region, whether there were any diseases in their province—fevers, fatal fevers, smallpox, and the like, and all in such detail and with such precision that it showed more than mere curiosity. The gentleman had something solid in his receptions and blew his nose extremely loudly. I don't know how he did it, but only his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, was a perfectly innocent virtue, however, which earned him a great deal of respect on the part of the innkeeper, so that whenever he heard the sound, he shook his hair, straightened up more respectfully, and, bending his head from above, asked, "Is there anything he wants?" After dinner, the gentleman ate a cup of coffee and sat down on the sofa, putting a pillow behind his back, which in Russian taverns instead of elastic wool is stuffed with something extremely similar to brick and cobblestone. Then he began to yawn and ordered himself to be taken to his room, where, lying down, he slept for two hours. After resting, he wrote on a piece of paper, at the request of the innkeeper, rank, name and surname to report to the police. On a piece of paper, going down the stairs, he read the following in the warehouses: "Collegiate councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, according to his needs." When the policeman was still sorting out the note in the warehouses, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself went to see the city, with which he seemed satisfied, for he found that the city was in no way inferior to other provincial towns: the yellow paint on the stone houses struck the eye strongly, and the gray on the wooden houses darkened modestly. The houses were one, two and one and a half floors, with an eternal mezzanine, very beautiful, in the opinion of provincial architects. In some places, these houses seemed lost among the wide street like a field and endless wooden fences; in some places they huddled together, and here the movement of the people and liveliness was more noticeable. There were signs almost washed away by the rain with pretzels and boots, here and there with painted blue trousers and the signature of some Arshava tailor; where there is a store with caps, caps and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fedorov"; where billiards was drawn with two players in tailcoats, such as guests dress in our theaters when they enter the stage in the last act. The players were depicted with aimed cues, slightly twisted back arms and slanting legs, having just made an antrasha in the air. Underneath it all was written, "And here is the establishment." In some places, just on the street, there were tables with nuts, soap and gingerbread that looked like soap; where there is a tavern with a thick fish drawn and a fork stuck in it. Most often, the darkened double-headed state eagles were noticeable, which were now replaced by the laconic inscription: "Drinking House". The pavement was bad everywhere. He also looked into the city garden, which consisted of thin trees, ill-received, with supports at the bottom, in the form of triangles, very beautifully painted with green oil paint. However, although these trees were no higher than reeds, they were said in the newspapers in the description of the illumination that "our city was adorned, thanks to the care of the civil ruler, with a garden consisting of shady, broad-branched trees, which give coolness on a hot day," and that at the same time "it was very touching to see how the hearts of the citizens trembled in excess of gratitude and streamed streams of tears in gratitude to the lord mayor." After asking the watchman in detail where he could go nearer, if necessary, to the cathedral, to the public offices, to the governor, he went to look at the river that flowed in the middle of the city, tore off the poster nailed to the post, so that when he came home he could read it thoroughly, he looked intently at a lady of good appearance walking along the wooden pavement, followed by a boy in military livery, with a bundle in his hand.  and, once more glancing at everything, as if to remember the position of the place well, he went home directly to his room, supported slightly on the stairs by the innkeeper. When he had eaten his tea, he sat down before the table, ordered a candle to be brought to him, took a poster from his pocket, held it up to the candle, and began to read, squinting his right eye a little. However, there was little remarkable in the poster: there was a drama by Mr. Kotzebue, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplevin, Cora was played by the girl Zyablov, and the other faces were even less remarkable; However, he read them all, even got to the price of the stalls, and learned that the poster had been printed in the printing house of the provincial government, then turned to the other side to see if there was anything there, but finding nothing, he rubbed his eyes, rolled them up neatly, and put them in his chest, where he was in the habit of putting everything he came across. The day, it seems, was concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour cabbage soup, and a sound sleep in the entire pump wrapper, as they say in other parts of the vast Russian state.

The whole next day was devoted to visits; The visitor went to pay visits to all the city dignitaries. He was respectfully visited by the governor, who, as it turned out, like Chichikov, was neither fat nor thin, had Anna around his neck, and it was even rumored that he had been presented to the star; [ The star is the Order of Stanislav.

] However, he was a great good-natured man and sometimes even embroidered on tulle himself. Then I went to the vice-governor, then I was with the prosecutor, with the chairman of the chamber, with the chief of police, with the farmer, with the head of the state factories... it is a pity that it is somewhat difficult to remember all the powerful of this world; but suffice it to say that the visitor showed unusual activity in connection with visits: he even came to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical board and the city architect. And then he sat in the carriage for a long time, thinking of someone else to pay a visit, but there were no more officials in the city. In conversations with these rulers, he was very skillful in flattering everyone. He hinted to the governor in passing that one enters his province as if it were paradise, the roads are velvet everywhere, and that those governments that appoint wise dignitaries are worthy of great praise. He said something very flattering to the chief of police about the city watchmen; and in conversations with the vice-governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were still only state councillors, he even mistakenly said twice: "Your Excellency," which pleased them very much. The consequence of this was that the governor invited him to a house party that day, and the other officials, too, for their part, some for dinner, some for Boston, some for a cup of tea.

The newcomer seemed to avoid talking much about himself; if he did, it was in some general places, with noticeable modesty, and his conversation on such occasions took a somewhat bookish turn: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and did not deserve to be cared for much, that he had experienced much in his lifetime, had suffered in the service of truth, had many enemies who had even made attempts on his life, and that now,  wishing to calm down, he finally sought to choose a place to live, and that, having arrived in this city, he considered it his indispensable duty to pay his respects to its first dignitaries. That was all that was known in the city about this new face, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor's party. Preparations for this party took more than two hours, and here the visitor showed such attention to the toilet that is not even seen everywhere. After a short afternoon nap, he ordered them to wash and rubbed both cheeks with soap for an extremely long time, propping them up from the inside with his tongue; then, taking a towel from the innkeeper's shoulder, he wiped his full face with it on all sides, starting from behind his ears and snorting twice in the very face of the innkeeper. Then he put on his shirt front of the mirror, plucked out two hairs that had come out of his nose, and immediately afterwards found himself in a lingonberry-colored tailcoat with a spark. Thus dressed, he rolled in his carriage through the infinitely wide streets, illuminated by the thin light from here and there flashing oceans. However, the governor's house was so illuminated, even if it were for a ball; A carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes in front of the entrance, the shouts of the foreman in the distance - in short, everything was as it should be. On entering the hall Chichikov had to close his eyes for a moment, for the glitter of the candles, lamps, and ladies' dresses was terrible. Everything was flooded with light. The black tailcoats flashed and rushed about separately and in heaps here and there, as flies rush about on a white shining refinery in the hot July summer, when the old housekeeper chops and divides it into glittering fragments in front of an open window; The children are all looking, gathered around, following curiously the movements of her stiff hands as they raise their hammers, and squadrons of flies, raised by the light air, fly in boldly like complete masters, and, taking advantage of the old woman's blindness and the sun disturbing her eyes, sprinkle the tasty morsels here and there in thick heaps Saturated with a rich summer, already at every step arranging dainty dishes, they flew in with the wrong  to eat, but only to show himself, to walk up and down the sugar heap, rub his hind or front legs against each other, or scratch them under his wings, or, stretching out both his front legs, rub them over his head, turn and fly away again, and again fly in with new annoying squadrons. No sooner had Chichikov looked around than he was already seized by the governor's arm, who immediately introduced him to the governor's wife. The visiting visitor did not lose his temper here either: he said a kind of compliment, very appropriate for a middle-aged man with a rank not too great and not too small. When the established pairs of dancers pressed everyone against the wall, he, putting his hands back, looked at them very attentively for a minute or two. Many ladies were well dressed and fashionable, others dressed in what God sent to the provincial city. The men here, as elsewhere, were of two kinds: one thin, who kept hovering around the ladies; some of them were of such a kind that it was hardly possible to distinguish them from those of St. Petersburg, had the same very deliberate and tasteful combed whiskers, or simply plausible, very clean-shaven ovals of faces, sat down just as carelessly next to the ladies, spoke French in the same way, and made the ladies laugh as well as in Petersburg. The other kind of men were fat or the same as Chichikov, that is, not so fat, but not thin either. These, on the contrary, squinted and backed away from the ladies, and looked only around to see if the governor's servant had set up a green table for whist. Their faces were full and round, some even had warts, some were pockmarked, they wore no hair on their heads, neither crested nor boucles, nor in the manner of "damn me," as the French say, their hair was either cut low or slicked, and their features were more rounded and strong. They were honorary officials in the city. Alas! fat people know how to do their affairs better in this world than thin ones. The thin ones serve more on special assignments or are only listed and wag here and there; their existence is somehow too easy, airy and completely unreliable. Fat people never occupy indirect places, but all are straight, and if they sit down somewhere, they will sit securely and firmly, so that the place will soon crack and bend under them, and they will not fly off. They do not like external shine; Their tailcoats are not as cleverly cut as those of the thin ones, but in the boxes there is the grace of God. A thin one at three years old does not have a single soul left that is not pawned in a pawnshop; Tolstoy is quiet, look, and somewhere at the end of the city there appeared a house bought in the name of his wife, then at the other end another house, then a village near the city, then a village with all its lands. Finally, Tolstoy, having served God and the sovereign, having earned universal respect, leaves the service, moves over and becomes a landowner, a glorious Russian lord, a hospitable man, and lives, and lives well. And after him, again, the thin heirs, according to the Russian custom, spend all their father's goods on couriers. It cannot be concealed that almost this kind of reflection occupied Chichikov while he was contemplating society, and the consequence of this was that at last he joined the Tolstoys, where he met almost all the familiar faces: the prosecutor with very black thick eyebrows and a somewhat winking left eye as if to say: "Come, brother, to the other room, there I will tell you something."  "A man, however, serious and silent; the postmaster, a short man, but a wit and a philosopher; the president of the chamber, a very sensible and amiable man, who all greeted him as an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov bowed a little to one side, though not without pleasantness. Immediately he met the very courteous and courteous landowner Manilov and the somewhat clumsy Sobakevich, who stepped on his foot the first time, saying: "I beg your pardon." Immediately he was thrust a whist card, which he accepted with the same polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not get up until dinner. All conversation has completely ceased, as always happens when at last one is engaged in a practical occupation. Although the postmaster was very clear, he, too, taking the cards in his hands, immediately expressed a thinking face on his face,

The next day Chichikov went to dinner and evening to the chief of police, where from three o'clock in the afternoon they sat down in whist and played until two o'clock in the morning. There, among other things, he made the acquaintance of the landowner Nozdrev, a man of about thirty, a broken fellow, who, after three or four words, began to say "you" to him. Nozdrev was also on a first-name basis with the chief of police and the prosecutor and treated him in a friendly way; but when they sat down to play the great game, the chief of police and the prosecutor examined his bribes with the utmost attention, and watched almost every card with which he went. The next day Chichikov spent the evening with the president of the chamber, who received his guests in a dressing-gown, somewhat oily, including two ladies. Then he was at the Vice-Governor's soiree, at the big dinner at the farmer's, at the little dinner at the prosecutor's, which, however, was worth a great deal; at the appetizer after the mass given by the mayor, which was also worth dinner. In short, he did not have to stay at home for a single hour, and he came to the hotel only to fall asleep. The newcomer somehow knew how to find himself in everything and showed himself to be an experienced socialite. Whatever the conversation was about, he always knew how to support it: whether it was a question of a horse farm, he also spoke of a horse farm; whether they talked about good dogs, and here he made very useful remarks; whether they had interpreted the investigation carried out by the Treasury Chamber, he showed that he was not unaware of the tricks of the judges; whether there was a discussion about the game of billiards, and in the game of billiards he did not miss; whether they talked about virtue, and about virtue he reasoned very well, even with tears in his eyes; about the manufacture of hot wine, and in hot wine he knew the use; of the customs inspectors and officials, and of them, he judged as if he himself were both an official and an overseer. But it is remarkable that he knew how to clothe all this with a kind of sedateness, knew how to carry himself well. He spoke neither loudly nor quietly, but exactly as he should. In short, wherever you turned, he was a very decent man. All officials were pleased with the arrival of the new face. The governor said of him that he was a well-meaning man; the prosecutor said that he was a good man; the gendarme colonel said that he was a learned man; the chairman of the chamber said that he was a knowledgeable and respectable man; the chief of police, that he was a respectable and amiable man; The chief of police's wife said that he was the kindest and most courteous man. Even Sobakevich himself, who seldom spoke kindly of anyone, having arrived rather late from the city, and having already completely undressed and lying down on the bed beside his thin wife, said to her: "I, my dear, was at the governor's party, and dined at the chief of police, and made the acquaintance of the collegiate councillor, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov: a pleasant man!"

Such an opinion, very flattering to the visitor, was formed of him in the city, and it persisted until a strange property of the guest and an enterprise, or, as they say in the provinces, a passage, of which the reader will soon learn, threw almost the whole city into utter perplexity.

Chapter Two

For more than a week the visiting gentleman had been living in the city, traveling to parties and dinners and thus spending, as they say, a very pleasant time. At last he decided to postpone his visits to the countryside and visit the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he gave his word. Perhaps he was prompted to do this by another, more significant reason, a more serious matter, close to his heart... But the reader will learn about all this gradually and in due time, if only he will have the patience to read the proposed story, which is very long, and will then expand wider and more spacious as we approach the end that crowns the matter. The coachman Selifan was ordered to put the horses in a well-known cart early in the morning; Petrushka was ordered to stay at home, to look after the room and the suitcase. It will not be superfluous for the reader to get acquainted with these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not so conspicuous persons, and what is called secondary or even tertiary, although the main moves and springs of the poem are not based on them, and only here and there touch and easily hook them, the author likes to be extremely thorough in everything from this point of view, in spite of the fact that the Russian man himself wants to be as neat as a German. This, however, will not take much time or space, because there is not much to add to what the reader already knows, that is, that Petrushka wore a somewhat wide brown frock coat from the lord's shoulder, and had, according to the custom of people of his rank, a large nose and lips. His character was more silent than talkative; he even had a noble incentive to enlightenment, that is, to read books, the content of which did not bother him: it did not matter to him at all whether it was the adventures of a hero in love, just a primer or a prayer book, he read everything with equal attention; If he had been given chemotherapy, he would not have refused it either. He liked not what he read about, but rather the reading itself, or rather the process of reading itself, that a word was always coming out of the letters, which sometimes the devil knows what it meant. This reading was performed more in a lying position in the hall, on the bed and on the mattress, which had become dead and thin as a cake from such a circumstance. In addition to his passion for reading, he had two other habits, which constituted two other characteristic features of him: to sleep without undressing, as he was, in the same frock-coat, and to always carry with him some peculiar air of his own, of his own smell, which resonated somewhat with a living peace, so that it was enough for him to build his bed somewhere, even in a room hitherto uninhabited.  and dragged his overcoat and belongings there, and it already seemed that people had lived in this room for ten years. Chichikov, who was a very delicate man, and even in some cases fastidious, drew air to his fresh nose in the morning, and only shook his head, saying: "You, brother, the devil knows you, are you sweating, or something. You should at least go to the bathhouse." To which Petrushka made no reply, and tried at once to attend to some business; Or he approached the hanging gentleman's tailcoat with a whip, or simply cleaned up something. What he was thinking at the time when he was silent – perhaps he was saying to himself: "You, too, are good, you are not tired of repeating the same thing forty times" – God knows, it is difficult to know what the serf man of the yard thinks at that time, the lord gives him instructions. So, here is what can be said about Petrushka for the first time. Coachman Selifan was a completely different person... But the author is very ashamed to keep his readers so long occupied by people of the lower class, knowing from experience how reluctant they are to get acquainted with the lower classes. Such is the Russian man: he has a strong passion for conceit with someone who would be at least one rank higher than him, and a casual acquaintance with a count or prince is better for him than any close friendly relations. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a collegiate adviser. The court councillors may become acquainted with him, but those who have already approached the rank of general, those, God knows, may even cast one of those contemptuous glances which a man proudly throws at everything that grovels at his feet, or, what is worse, perhaps pass through a disregard that is fatal to the author. But however deplorable both may be, it is nevertheless necessary to return to the hero. And so, having given the necessary orders in the evening, having woken up very early in the morning, having washed himself, having wiped himself from head to foot with a wet sponge, which was done only on Sundays, and on that day it was Sunday, having shaved himself in such a way that his cheeks became a veritable satin in the discourse of smoothness and gloss, having put on a lingonberry-colored tailcoat with a spark, and then an overcoat on the big bears,  He descended the stairs, supported by the arm of the innkeeper on one side and then on the other, and got into the carriage. With a thunder, the carriage drove out from under the gate of the hotel into the street. A passing priest took off his hat, several boys in soiled shirts stretched out their hands, saying: "Sir, give it to the orphan!" It was not without joy that he saw the striped barrier in the distance, letting him know that the pavement, like all other torment, would soon be finished; and a few more times striking his head quite the truck, Chichikov at last rushed over the soft ground. Scarcely had the city gone back than they began to write, according to our custom, nonsense and game on both sides of the road: hummocks, spruce forests, low liquid bushes of young pines, charred trunks of old ones, wild heather, and similar nonsense. There were villages stretched out along a string, building similar to old folded firewood, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations under them in the form of hanging wipers embroidered with patterns. Several peasants, as usual, yawned, sitting on the benches in front of the gate in their sheepskin coats. Women with thick faces and bandaged breasts looked out of the upper windows; a calf looked out of the lower ones, or a pig stuck out its blind muzzle. In short, the species are known. When he had gone the fifteenth mile, he remembered that, according to Manilov, his village must be here, but the sixteenth verst had passed by, and the village was still not visible, and if it had not been for two peasants. who came across, then it is unlikely that they would have had a chance to please in the mood.

When asked how far the village of Zamanilovka was, the peasants took off their hats, and one of them, who was smarter and wore a wedge-shaped beard, answered:

"Manilovka, perhaps, and not Zamanilovka?"

"Well, yes, Manilovka.

"Manilovka!" And as soon as you go another mile, there you are, that is, straight to the right.

"To the right?" The coachman replied.

"To the right," said the peasant. "This will be your way to Manilovka; and there is no Zamanilovka. It is called that, that is, its nickname is Manilovka, and there is no Zamanilovka here at all. There, right on the hill, you will see a two-storey stone house, in which, that is, the master himself lives. This is Manilovka for you, and there is no Zamanilovka here at all, and there never was.

We went to find Manilovka. Having traveled two versts, they met a turn into a country road, but it seemed that they had already made two, three, and four versts, and the stone house of two floors was still not visible. Then Chichikov remembered that if a friend invited him to his village fifteen versts away, it meant that there were thirty faithful people to it. The village of Manilovka could not attract many with its location. The manor's house stood alone on the jurassic, that is, on a hill open to all the winds that could blow; the slope of the mountain on which he stood was dressed in trimmed turf. Two or three flowerbeds with lilac and yellow acacia bushes were scattered on it in English; five or six birches in small clumps here and there raised their small-leaved thin tops. Under two of them was a gazebo with a flat green dome, blue wooden columns, and the inscription: "Temple of Solitary Meditation"; lower is a pond covered with greenery, which, however, is not a novelty in the Aglitsa gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this elevation, and partly along the slope itself, the gray log huts darkened up and down, which our hero, for some unknown reason, at that very moment began to count and counted more than two hundred; nowhere between them is there a growing tree or any greenery; Everywhere there was only one log. The view was enlivened by two women, who, having picturesquely picked up their dresses and stumbled on all sides, were wading knee-deep in the pond, dragging a tattered raving by two wooden nags, where two tangled crayfish were visible and a roach glittered; The women, it seemed, were in a quarrel with each other and quarreled over something. In the distance, to the side, a pine forest was darkening with a dull bluish color.
