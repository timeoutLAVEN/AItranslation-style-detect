Paragraph [1]: Preface to Border Town

For farmers and soldiers, I hold an inexpressible warmth and love. This feeling can be found throughout all my works, and I have never concealed it. I grew up in the kind of small country town described in my works. My grandfather, father, and brothers all served in the military: those who have died, died in the line of duty, and those still living will inevitably spend their lives in service as well. Drawing from the world I have come to know, I set out to describe their loves and hatreds, their joys and sorrows. Even if this pen of mine is clumsy, perhaps I have not strayed too far from the mark. For they are upright and honest people — their lives are in some ways profoundly great and in other ways utterly ordinary, their characters in some ways deeply beautiful and in other ways trivially petty — and when I sat down to write about them, in order to make them more human and more true to life, I naturally wrote with plain honesty. But because of this, the work may well turn out to be a futile endeavor.

Given the current climate, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are very easily put off by this kind of work. The former, wishing to appear "current," tell people that China has no need for such works; the latter, "too afraid of being left behind," are likewise unwilling to read them at present. This is, of course, the truth. What does it mean to be "left behind"? A person with any sense may never be able to understand, yet who among the majority is not afraid of it? I have something to say: "This book was not written for that majority." Those who have read three or five foreign-bound volumes on literary theory and criticism, or who have also read a great pile of classical and modern world masterpieces — their life experience rarely permits them, beyond their "erudition," to know even a little about another place and another kind of life in China. Therefore, even if this work happens to align with some literary theory and critics heap it with praise, such criticism would still amount to an insult to the author. Since they have no desire to understand the true loves and hatreds, the true joys and sorrows of this people, they are in no position to speak to the merits or shortcomings of this work — this book was not written for them. As for literary enthusiasts — whether university or middle school students, scattered across the more densely populated cities of the country — they often, with genuine sincerity and innocence, devote a portion of their most precious time to reading newly published literary works at home. They are controlled and directed by a set of habits cultivated through the combined efforts of theorists, critics, clever publishers, and gossip columnists accustomed to lying and rumor-mongering; and their lives are, in truth, far too removed from the world described in this work. They have no need for this kind of work, and this book does not seek their approval either. Theorists have literary theories from publications around the world to consult — they need never worry about having nothing to say. Critics have writers and works to whom they owe small favors or bear small grudges — enough to keep them busy praising and condemning for a lifetime. The majority of readers, whatever their tastes or beliefs, always have something to read. Is it not precisely out of concern for the reading public that so many people — supposedly on behalf of that public — spin endlessly like tops? Even if this book is not rejected by the theorists and critics who lead the majority, and even if the led majority does not entirely abandon it, the author of this book has long since made up his mind to abandon that "majority."

This book is intended only for those who "have left school behind, or who never had access to school in the first place, who can still read Chinese characters, who live and work in roles beyond the reach of literary theory, literary criticism, and the noise of lies and rumors, who live within that society, and who care deeply about all the strengths and weaknesses of this entire people across time and space." They truly know what the countryside is today, they want to know what it was in the past, and they will surely also wish to learn from this book something about the villages and soldiers of one small corner of the world. Even if the world I write about is entirely unfamiliar to them, their open-mindedness and their earnest desire to seek comfort and knowledge from a book will surely allow them to read through it with ease. Nor do I stop there — I also intend to offer them an opportunity for contrast, in another work, by addressing the civil wars of the past twenty years, showing what kind of new creature the farmers who bore the brunt of it all became after their characters and souls were crushed by overwhelming force, stripped of their original simplicity, diligence, peacefulness, and integrity. How poverty-stricken and idle they became under the ravages of extortion and opium! I will offer a plain account of the anxieties of ordinary people caught up in change as this people is carried forward by history toward an unknown fate, along with the ideas and desires around "surviving" and "how to survive" born of insufficient nourishment. My readers should be people of reason — reason grounded in concern for the changes in present-day Chinese society, in recognition of both the past greatness and the present degradation of this people, each quietly engaged in the great work of national renewal. This work may only offer them a touch of nostalgic melancholy, or only occasion a bitter laugh, or perhaps bring them a nightmare — but at the same time, who knows, it may yet give them a measure of courage and faith!

Written on April 24, 1934

Paragraph [2]: Chapter 1

I

Traveling from Sichuan into Hunan, there is an official road running along the eastern side. When this road approaches the border of western Hunan and reaches a small mountain town called Chadong, there is a small stream, and beside the stream stands a small white pagoda, beneath which lives a solitary household. This household consists of only one old man, one young girl, and one yellow dog.

The stream flows on, winding around the hills, and after about three li it joins the main river at Chadong. If one crosses the stream and goes over the small hill on foot, it is only one li to the edge of Chadong. The stream curves like the back of a bow, while the mountain path runs straight like the bowstring, so there is a slight difference in distance. The stream is about twenty zhang wide, and its bed is made of large flat stones. The still water, even where it is too deep for a punting pole to reach the bottom, remains clear and transparent, and the fish swimming back and forth in the river can be counted one by one. Since the stream serves as a thoroughfare between Sichuan and Hunan, the water level rises and falls frequently, and for lack of funds no bridge could be built, so a flat-headed ferry boat was arranged instead. The boat can carry about twenty passengers at a time, along with people and horses, making multiple trips when the number is large. At the bow of the ferry stands a small bamboo pole with a movable iron ring hanging from it. A length of old cable is strung between wooden troughs on both banks of the stream. When someone needs to cross, the iron ring is hooked onto the old cable, and the person on the boat pulls hand over hand along the cable, slowly drawing the boat to the other bank. As the boat nears the shore, the ferryman shouts "Slow down, slow down," then leaps ashore in one swift motion, grabs the iron ring, and people, goods, cattle, and horses all disembark and disappear over the small hill. The ferry crossing is public property, so passengers need not pay. When someone felt uneasy and tossed a handful of coins onto the deck, the ferryman would pick them up one by one and press them back into the person's palm with the earnest air of someone in an argument: "I have my rations — three dou of rice and seven hundred coins — that's enough. Who needs this!"

But it was no use. Everyone wants a clear conscience, and it feels wrong to put in effort without receiving any payment, so no matter what, there were always people who paid. The ferryman, unable to refuse out of his own desire for peace of mind, would entrust the money to someone going to Chadong to buy tea and tobacco. He would hang bundles of the finest tobacco produced in Chadong from his own belt, and generously give some to any traveler who needed it. Sometimes, when he sensed from a traveler's expression that they had taken a particular interest in the tobacco at his side, he would tie a small bundle of it to the person's pack and say, "Don't you smoke? This is good stuff, wonderful stuff, the flavor is really something — makes a fine gift too!" As for the tea, in the sixth month he would put it in a large vat, steep it in boiling water, and offer it to passersby to quench their thirst.

The ferryman was none other than the old man who lived beneath the pagoda. He had lived for seventy years, and had kept watch by this little stream since the age of twenty — for fifty years he had ferried countless people back and forth across the water. Old as he was, he should have been resting, but heaven would not let him rest, and he seemed unable to part from this way of life. He never reflected on the meaning of his work to himself, but simply lived on quietly and faithfully. What took the place of heaven in making him feel the strength of life when the sun rose, and keeping him from wishing to die along with the setting sun, was the young girl at his side. His only companions were a ferry boat and a yellow dog; his only family was that girl.

The girl's mother — the old ferryman's only daughter — had, fifteen years ago, secretly carried on a clandestine affair with a soldier from Chadong behind the back of her honest father. After the child was conceived, the soldier wanted to take her and flee downstream together. But looking at the act of running away, it would mean one person betraying a soldier's duty, and the other having to abandon her lonely father. After much deliberation, the soldier, seeing that she lacked the courage to leave and unwilling himself to ruin his reputation as a soldier, thought: since living together was impossible, dying together could not be stopped by anyone, and he took poison first. The woman, mindful of the life growing inside her, could not bring herself to do it and could not make up her mind. The matter had already come to the knowledge of her father the ferryman, yet he did not utter a single weighty word about it, and went on living each day as calmly as if he had never heard a thing. His daughter, filled with both shame and pity, stayed by her father's side, and after the child in her womb was born, she went to the edge of the stream, drank a great deal of cold water, and died. In something close to a miracle, this orphan had somehow grown up, and in the blink of an eye was already thirteen years old. Because the hills around their home were thick with bamboo, their green pressing in from all sides, the old ferryman casually chose a fitting name for this poor little orphan, calling her Cuicui.

Cuicui grew up in the wind and sun, her skin turned dark, her eyes always met with green hills and clear water, and her gaze was as bright and clear as crystal. Nature both nurtured and educated her, making her innocent and lively, in every way like a small wild creature. She was gentle too, like a mountain deer, never given to cruelty, never troubled, never roused to anger. When strangers on the ferry paid her particular attention, she would fix them with her bright eyes, ready at any moment to bolt into the deep hills — but once she understood that the person meant no harm, she would go back to playing by the water with easy composure.

The old ferryman kept watch at the bow of the boat in all weather, rain or shine. When someone needed to cross, he would bend slightly at the waist, grip the bamboo cable with both hands, and pull the boat across the stream. Sometimes when he was tired and had fallen asleep on the large rock by the stream, and someone on the far bank was waving and calling for the ferry, Cuicui would not let her grandfather get up — she would jump onto the boat herself and nimbly ferry the traveler across in his place, handling everything smoothly and without a hitch. At other times she and her grandfather and the yellow dog would all be on the boat together; when ferrying passengers she would work alongside her grandfather, and as the boat neared the bank, while her grandfather called out to the passengers "Slow down, slow down," the yellow dog would take the rope in its mouth, leap ashore first, and, as if it truly understood what it meant to do its job, hold the rope firmly in its teeth and drag the boat to the bank.

On clear and pleasant days, when no one needed to cross and the whole day stretched out in idleness, grandfather and Cuicui would sit on the large rock in front of the door and bask in the sun. Or they would throw a piece of wood from a height into the water and urge the yellow dog to leap from the rock and retrieve it. Or Cuicui and the yellow dog would both prick up their ears and listen to grandfather tell stories of battles fought in the town many years ago. Or grandfather and Cuicui would each take a small bamboo flute and play wedding tunes together. When a traveler came to cross, the old ferryman would set down his flute and go alone to the boat to ferry the person across, while the one left on the rock would call out in a clear voice:

"Grandpa, grandpa, listen to me play — you sing!"

Out in the middle of the stream, grandfather would happily break into song, his hoarse voice and the sound of the bamboo flute mingling and reverberating in the still air, making the stream seem a little more lively. (In truth, the echo of the singing only made everything a little more quiet.)

Sometimes what crossed on the ferry was a small calf coming from eastern Sichuan to Chadong, or a flock of sheep, or a bridal sedan chair. Cuicui would always scramble to act as ferryman, standing at the bow, lazily pulling the cable, letting the boat drift slowly across. After the cattle, sheep, and sedan chair had gone ashore, Cuicui would follow along, stand on top of the small hill, and watch until they had gone far into the distance before returning to the boat and pulling it back to the bank near home. She would softly imitate the bleating of a lamb or the lowing of a cow, or pick a handful of wildflowers and tie them to her head, playing at being a bride all by herself.

The mountain town of Chadong was only one li from the ferry crossing. When oil or salt needed to be bought, or when a festival came and grandfather wanted a cup of wine, grandfather would not go into town — the yellow dog would accompany Cuicui into town to get what was needed. At the general goods shop, there were great bundles of vermicelli, large vats of white sugar, firecrackers, and red candles, all of which made a deep impression on Cuicui, and she would come back and talk about these things to her grandfather for a long time. Along the riverbank there were also many upstream-bound boats, with hundreds of boatmen busy loading and unloading all manner of goods. These boats were far larger and far more interesting than the little ferry,Cuicui is not easy to forget either. Two

The town of Chadong was built along the water and against the hills. On the side facing the mountains, the city wall wound like a long snake along the hillside. On the riverside, space was left outside the walls for docks where small canopied boats could moor. Downstream, the boats carried tung oil, green salt, and dye-stuff. Upstream, they carried cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, sundry goods, and dried seafood. Running through all the docks was a riverside street, where most of the houses stood half on land and half over the water. Space being limited, nearly every house had stilted overhanging rooms. When the spring floods rose and the water gradually crept into the street, the people along the riverside would each bring out long ladders, propping one end against the eaves and the other against the city wall, and with much cursing and shouting, they would carry their bundles, bedding, and rice jars up the ladders into the city. Only when the water receded would they come back out through the city gates. In years when the floods came with particular ferocity, one or two of the stilted houses along the river would be swept away by the current, and everyone would stand on the city wall staring blankly. Those who suffered losses stared just as blankly, as if there were nothing to say about what they had lost, much as one might watch some other inevitable misfortune unfold under nature's arrangement. During a flood, standing on the wall, one could look out over the suddenly widened river, the water surging and rolling, carrying down from upstream houses, cattle, sheep, and great trees. At the calmer stretches of water, in front of the customs pontoon, there were often men in small skiffs who, upon spotting a beast, a piece of timber, or an empty boat drifting in the current — perhaps with a woman or child crying out from aboard — would row out urgently, intercept the object downstream, lash it with a long rope, and row it back to shore. These honest and courageous men loved profit and upheld righteousness alike, much like the other locals. Whether rescuing people or salvaging goods, they acted with the same swift boldness in what was a kind of joyful adventure, and anyone who witnessed it could not help but cheer.

The river is the historically renowned You River, now called the Bai River. Downstream, where the Bai River meets the Yuan River at Chenzhou, the water becomes somewhat murky, with the quality of a mountain spring finding its way out. But traveling upstream, the deep pools of three to five zhang are clear to the bottom. Lit by the daylight, the small white pebbles and agate stones with their patterns on the riverbed are all perfectly visible. Fish swimming in the water look as if they are floating in air. Both banks are lined with tall mountains, and the mountains are full of fine bamboo used for papermaking, which stays a deep jade green all year round, vivid to the eye. Homes near the water are mostly nestled among peach and apricot blossoms, and in spring one need only remember: wherever there are peach blossoms, there are people, and wherever there are people, wine can be bought. In summer, the purple-flowered cloth trousers and jackets hung out to dry in the blazing sun serve as flags marking where people live. When autumn and winter come, the houses perched on cliffs or along the water all stand out clearly. Yellow mud walls, black tiles — their placement is always just right, in perfect harmony with the surrounding landscape, leaving anyone who approaches with a genuinely pleasant impression. A traveler with even a passing interest in poetry and painting, curled up in a small boat on this little river for a thirty-day journey, would never grow bored, for there are wonders at every turn, and nature's boldness and delicacy alike never fail to captivate the heart.

The Bai River has its source along the Sichuan border, and small boats traveling upstream can reach Xiushan in Sichuan when the spring floods are high. But within Hunan's borders, Chadong is the last river dock. Though the river here is about half a li wide at Chadong, when the water drops in autumn and winter, the flowing channel is less than twenty zhang across, the rest being nothing but a beach of green stone. Boats can go no further from here, so all goods entering and leaving eastern Sichuan are loaded and unloaded at this spot. Outgoing goods are carried in on the shoulders of porters using fir-wood shoulder poles, and incoming goods are likewise hauled away in bundles and loads by human labor.

The town itself was garrisoned by one battalion of soldiers, reformed from the old Green Standard troops, along with about five hundred households. (Among these households, apart from a small number of petty capitalists who owned some hill fields and oil presses, or who lent money and stockpiled oil, rice, and cotton yarn, most were families of soldiers who had come here on military duty in earlier times.) There was also a likin office, whose working quarters were in a small temple below the riverside street outside the city, where a long pennant was always hanging. The bureau chief lived inside the city. The battalion was quartered in the old brigadier's yamen, and apart from the bugler who went up to the city wall each day to blow his horn — reminding people that troops were still stationed here — the rest of the soldiers seemed not to exist at all. On winter days, walking into town, one would see clothes and green vegetables drying in front of every doorway. Sweet potatoes, still on their vines, hung from the eaves. Bags made of palm fiber, stuffed with chestnuts, hazelnuts, and other hard-shelled nuts, also hung from the eaves. In every corner, chickens of all sizes clucked and pecked about. Here and there a man might be seen sitting on his own doorstep sawing wood, or splitting logs with an axe and stacking the finished pieces in the open yard in pagoda-like piles. One might also catch sight of a few middle-aged women in stiffly starched blue cotton jackets, white embroidered aprons tied at the chest, bent over in the sunlight, talking and working at the same time. Everything was always so quiet, and all the people passed each day in this simple, solitary way. A measure of stillness deepens one's capacity to reflect on human affairs, and deepens one's dreams. Those living in this small town must each, within their allotted days, have carried some inevitable longing — love or resentment — toward the things of this world. But what did these people think about? Who could say. Those living on the higher parts of town could stand at their doors and look out over the river and its scenery. When a boat came, they could see from far across the opposite bank the many trackers hauling it along. Some of those trackers, coming from downstream, would bring fine pastries and foreign sweets ashore and carry them into town to sell. When a boat arrived, the children's imaginations were all with the trackers. And the adults? Hatching a brood of chicks, raising a couple of pigs, asking a downstream boatman to bring back a pair of gold earrings, two zhang of official blue cloth, a crock of good soy sauce, or a double-grade Mefo lamp chimney — these things occupied most of a housewife's heart.

Though this small town was so peaceful and quiet, it was also a hub of trade between Sichuan's east and the outside world, and so the little riverside street outside the city had a somewhat different character. There were inns for traveling merchants, and barbershops that never moved. Beyond these, restaurants, general stores, oil dealers, salt depots, and cotton goods shops each had their place, giving the riverside street its particular color. There were also shops selling the sandalwood pulleys, bamboo cables, and iron pots used on boats, and households that made their living introducing sailors to work at the docks. In front of the small restaurants, on the long counters by the door, golden-fried carp and tofu lay in shallow earthen bowls, garnished with red chili shreds, with a large bamboo tube full of red chopsticks standing beside the bowl. Anyone willing to spend a little money could sit down at that counter by the door, pull out a pair of chopsticks, and a woman with pencil-thin plucked eyebrows and a powdered face would come over and ask: "Brother, sir, would you like sweet rice wine? Or spirits?" A man with a bit of fire in him, or a playful sort, or one who had a soft spot for the proprietress, would put on an air of indignation and say: "Sweet wine? I'm not a child — why are you asking if I want sweet wine!" And then a strong, sharp measure of spirits would be ladled from a large crock with a bamboo tube, poured into an earthen bowl, and set before him on the counter in no time. The general stores sold Mefo oil and the foreign lamps that burned it, along with incense, candles, and paper offerings. The oil dealers stored tung oil. The salt depots stocked the green salt from fire wells. The cotton goods shops sold white cotton yarn, broadcloth, raw cotton, and black crepe for head wrapping. The shops selling boat supplies stocked everything imaginable, and iron anchors weighing over a hundred jin were sometimes left outside by the roadside, waiting for a buyer to inquire about the price. Those who made their living introducing sailors to work at the docks kept their doors wide open all day long, with boat owners in blue satin mandarin jackets and rough-handed sailors coming and going. The place looked like a teahouse but sold no tea, was not an opium den yet smoking was permitted. Those who came here spoke of the business of boats, but since the comings and goings of vessels and the routines of oarsmen and trackers all followed established customs, there was no need to haggle over figures. Most of them came here simply to socialize. With the "dragon head manager" at the center, they talked about local news, the state of trade between the two provinces, and "new developments" downstream. Rotating savings clubs were mostly organized here, and the dice rolls to determine the order of club leaders were often held here as well. Two matters most often became the subject of their business talk: buying and selling boats, and buying and selling brides.

The kind of parasitic figures that large cities produce alongside commercial growth — needed by merchants and sailors alike — had found their way even to this small border town's riverside street, gathering in a number of houses with stilted overhanging rooms. These women were either brought in from nearby villages or had drifted here with Sichuan troops and stayed on. They wore imitation foreign silk blouses and printed cotton trousers, plucked their eyebrows into thin lines, and dressed their large buns with heavily scented, common-smelling oil. During the day, with nothing to do, they sat at the door making shoes, embroidering double phoenixes in red and green silk thread on the toes, or stitching embroidered chest pouches for their sailor sweethearts, all while watching the passersby to pass the long hours. Or they leaned on the riverside windows watching sailors unload cargo, listening to sailors climb the masts and sing. In the evenings, they took turns receiving merchants and sailors, faithfully fulfilling every duty that a woman of their trade was expected to fulfill.

Because the customs of this border region were simple and honest, even the women of this trade were always straightforward and genuine. With strangers, business required payment first, then the door was shut and things took their course; but once a man was well known to them, money became almost beside the point. These women mostly relied on Sichuan merchants for their livelihood, but their deepest affections were generally given to the sailors. When feelings ran strong, they would bite each other's lips and necks and swear oaths, agreeing that "after we part, neither of us is allowed to fool around." For forty or fifty days, the one floating on the river and the one left on shore would each drag through those days in a daze, keeping their hearts tightly bound to that one distant person. The women especially were sincere in their feelings, devoted beyond description. If a man did not return by the agreed time, she would dream constantly of the boat pulling up to shore, a figure swaying down the gangplank onto the bank and running straight toward her. Or if doubt crept in during the day, she would dream at night of the man singing from the mast, turned away, paying her no mind. The weaker ones would then, in the dream, throw themselves in the river or swallow opium; the stronger ones would seize a kitchen knife and charge straight at the sailor. Though their lives were so removed from ordinary society, when tears and joy, love and resentment, gain and loss worked their way into these lives, it was much the same as with young lives on any other patch of earth — the whole body and soul soaked through by that love and resentment, mistaking cold for heat, forgetting everything else. If there was any difference, it was only that these people were a little more genuine, and perhaps a little more muddled. Short-term arrangements, long-term marriages, brief encounters behind closed doors — these transactions involving a woman's body were, owing to the simplicity of local customs, felt by those involved to carry no particular shame, and onlookers never applied the judgments of the educated class to criticize or look down upon them. These people valued loyalty over profit and kept their word, and even the prostitutes among them were often more trustworthy than the morality-preaching, shame-professing people of the cities.

The man who presided over the water docks was named Shunshun, a figure who had spent his days in the military during the late Qing, and at the time of the revolution had served as a squad leader in the famous 49th Infantry Regiment. Among those who had been squad leaders, some became great men and famous figures through the revolution, others were executed and dismembered — he, however, returned to his hometown carrying a foot ailment he had picked up in his youth, took the small savings he had accumulated, bought a six-oar wooden boat, rented it to a poor boat owner to haul cargo between Chadong and Chenzhou. Luck was with him, and within half a year the boat had not met with any mishap, so with the money he had earned he also took a fair-skinned, black-haired young widow who had a little property of her own. A few years later, on this river, he had four boats of various sizes, a shop, and two sons.

But this generous and free-spirited man, though his business went very smoothly, loved making friends and was openhanded and quick to help others in need, so he could never accumulate wealth the way the oil merchants did. Having spent his own days among the soldiers, he understood the hardships of those away from home and sympathized with those down on their luck, so whenever boat owners ruined by shipwrecks, passing demobilized soldiers, or traveling scholars and writers came to him having heard his name and seeking help, he did his best to assist them all. Money earned on the water was spent just as freely. Though he had a slight trouble with his foot, he could still swim; his walk was never quite steady, but his character was utterly fair and selfless. Affairs on the water were by nature quite simple, everything governed by custom — whenever boats collided, or one boat's actions harmed another's interests, there were established customary ways of settling the matter. But to apply these customs and mediate all disputes, a central figure of seniority and virtue was needed. One autumn, the man who had previously held this role died, and Shunshun became his replacement. He was only fifty at the time, but he was clear-headed and reasonable, upright and even-tempered, and not greedy for money, so no one questioned him on account of his age.

By now, his elder son was already eighteen and his younger son sixteen. Both young men were as sturdy as young bulls — they could handle a boat, swim, and walk long distances. There was nothing that a young man raised in a small country town could do that they could not, and whatever they did, they did well. The elder, like his father, was bold and open-natured, unconstrained by convention or small formalities. The younger took after his fair-skinned, black-haired mother in temperament — quiet and not given to talk, yet with eyes and brows of exceptional refinement,At a glance you could tell he was clever and full of feeling.

Now that the two brothers were grown, their father felt it necessary to train them in all aspects of life, so he took turns sending the two boys out to travel. When traveling downstream, they would often work as hands on his own boats, sharing hardships with the crew. When rowing, they took the heaviest oar; when hauling the towline, they pulled the lead or second rope; they ate dried fish, chili peppers, and sour pickled vegetables, and slept on the hard planks of the cabin. When traveling upstream by land, they would follow merchants from eastern Sichuan, passing through Xiushan, Longtan, and Youyang on trading runs, and no matter the season or weather, rain or snow, they always wore straw sandals and kept pace from stop to stop. They also carried short knives, and when a fight was unavoidable, they would flash the blade out, step into an open space, face their opponent, and settle things with their bare hands. The code of the gang held that "a knife is needed to deal with enemies, and a knife is needed to bind friends," so whenever a knife was called for, they never let the moment pass. They learned trade, learned social dealings, learned to make a life in new places, and learned to use a knife to protect their bodies and their honor. The aim of this upbringing seemed to be to teach the two boys the courage and righteousness it takes to be a man. The result of this kind of education was that both grew as solid as tigers, yet remained warm and approachable, free of arrogance and laziness, free of vanity and the habit of bullying others with their status. So whenever the names of the father and his two sons were mentioned along the Chadong border, everyone spoke of them with respect.

When his sons were still very young, the father could already see that the elder was in every way like himself, yet he found himself showing a slight, unconscious favoritism toward the second boy. Because of this private feeling he could not quite help, he named the elder son Tianbao and the younger Nuosong. The idea was that one protected by heaven might still run into friction in human affairs, but as for one delivered by the Nuo god, local custom held that no one could afford to take him lightly. Nuosong was strikingly handsome. The boatmen of Chadong were poor at putting such beauty into words and could only give him the nickname "Yue Yun." Though no one had ever actually seen Yue Yun, the general impression of him came from the young male lead Yue Yun on the opera stage, and the name captured something of the same spirit. Three

At the border where the two provinces met, the military authority that had governed the region for more than ten years focused on keeping the peace and maintaining order, handled affairs with a steady hand, and saw no upheaval arise. River and overland trade was neither halted by war nor troubled by bandits; everything ran in good order, and the people lived contentedly within their lot. For these people, unless a family's ox died, a boat capsized, or some other great misfortune struck and knocked them down with grief, the suffering and struggle going on elsewhere in China seemed as though it would never reach the people of this border town.

The liveliest days of the year in the border town were the Dragon Boat Festival, the Mid-Autumn Festival, and the New Year. After the boat races, the garrison commander of the town, wishing to share in the people's joy and add to the festivity of the holiday, would have thirty large male ducks with green heads and long necks, each with a strip of red cloth tied around its neck, released into the river, and any soldier or civilian who was a good swimmer could jump in and chase the ducks. Whoever caught a duck became its owner. The long pool then took on a whole new look, with ducks everywhere on the water and people chasing them in every direction.

The races between boats and the races between people and ducks went on until evening before it was all over.

Shunshun, the big brother who ran the main dock, had been an expert swimmer in his youth and never came up empty-handed when he went into the water after a duck. I don't need to get in the water anymore." And from then on he truly stopped competing with others to catch ducks. Jumping in to save someone's life, though, was another matter entirely. Helping people out of danger — even if it meant walking into fire — remained an inescapable duty for a man, even at the age of eighty.

Both Tianbao and Nuosong were fine swimmers and boatmen in the local area.

The Dragon Boat Festival was coming again soon. The fifth day was the race, and on the first day the river street held a meeting and decided that the boat belonging to the river street would be put in the water that same day. Tianbao happened to be setting out that day to travel overland with merchants from eastern Sichuan to Longtan, delivering holiday goods, so only Nuosong took part. Sixteen young men, as solid as young bulls, brought incense, candles, firecrackers, and a tall drum with a head of raw cowhide painted with a vermilion taiji diagram, and made their way to the cave upstream where the boat was stored. They burned the incense and candles, dragged the boat into the water, all climbed aboard, lit the firecrackers, beat the drum, and the boat shot off like an arrow, racing swiftly downstream toward the long pool.

It was still morning then. By afternoon, the dragon boat from the fishermen across the river had also been put in the water, and the two dragon boats began rehearsing various racing techniques. The drum was heard on the water for the first time that season, and many people, hearing it, felt the joy of the approaching holiday. Those living in the stilted houses along the river who were waiting and hoping for someone far away could not help but think of that distant person when they heard the drum. During this festival, many boats would surely make it back in time, while many others could only spend the holiday somewhere along the way — and in between, there were joys and sorrows of human affairs that eyes could not see, playing out along the river street of this small mountain town, giving some people something to talk about and causing others to furrow their brows.

When the booming drumbeat skimmed over the water and crossed the hills to reach the ferry crossing, the first to notice it was the yellow dog. The yellow dog barked and barked, running in circles around the house as if startled, and when someone came to cross, it followed the ferry to the eastern bank and ran up to a small hilltop, barking loudly in the direction of the town.

Cuicui was sitting on the big stone outside the door, weaving grasshoppers and centipedes out of palm leaves to play with. She saw the yellow dog sleeping in the sun, then suddenly wake and run about like mad, crossing the river and coming back again, and she scolded it:

"Dog, dog, what are you doing! Stop that!"

But in a moment she heard the sound herself, and she too began running around the house, and together with the yellow dog crossed the little stream and stood on the small hilltop listening for a long while, letting that enchanting drumbeat carry her back to a festival from the past.

The old ferryman's maneuvering had only been meant to clear things up a little, but from the very start his way of telling the story had gone wrong, and so Nuosong had misunderstood him. He was just trying to explain what had happened that night properly when the boat reached the bank. Nuosong leaped ashore and made to leave. The old ferryman, looking even more flustered on the boat, called out:

"Nuosong, Nuosong, wait, I have something to tell you — didn't you just mention that — that business of you playing the fool? You weren't the fool at all, it was someone else who was truly made a fool by your singing!"

The young man stopped, but said quietly, "Enough, that's enough, don't say any more."

The old ferryman said, "Nuosong, I heard you don't want the mill — you want the ferry. That's what Yang Mabing said. It's not true, is it?"

The young man said, "And what if I want the ferry?"

The old ferryman looked at Nuosong's expression and suddenly felt a surge of joy. He couldn't help himself and called out loudly for Cuicui to come down to the water's edge. But for some reason — whether Cuicui was deliberately staying inside or had gone somewhere else — there was no sign of her for a long while, and not a sound from the girl. Nuosong waited a moment, looked at the old ferryman's expression, said nothing, smiled, and strode off with a porter carrying a load of vermicelli and sugar.

Past the small hill at Bixi Ju, the two of them walked along a winding path through a bamboo grove, and the porter spoke up:

"Nuosong, the way that ferryman looks at you, he's very fond of you!"

Nuosong said nothing, so the man continued:

"Nuosong, he asked whether you want the mill or the ferry — are you really planning to become his granddaughter's husband and take over that ferry of his?"

Nuosong laughed. The man went on:

"Nuosong, if it were up to me, I'd take the mill. A mill brings in seven sheng of rice and three dou of bran every day."

Nuosong said, "When I get back I'll talk to my father about acting as matchmaker for you with the Zhongzhai people, so you can get that mill. As for me, I think running a ferry would be just fine. Only that old fellow is too crooked and slippery — he's the one who got Tianbao killed."

The old ferryman watched Nuosong walk away like that, and Cuicui still hadn't come out — he felt very unhappy. He went back home to look, but Cuicui wasn't there. After a while, Cuicui came back from behind the small hill carrying a basket, and he realized she had gone out early that morning to dig bamboo shoot rhizomes.

"Cuicui, I called for you for a long time — you didn't hear me!"

"What did you call me for?"

"Someone crossing the ferry... someone we know, we were talking about you... I called and you didn't answer!"

"Who was it?"

"Guess, Cuicui. Not a stranger... you know him!"

Cuicui thought of the words she had overheard from the bamboo grove just a little while ago, and her face turned red. She said nothing for a long time.

The old ferryman asked, "Cuicui, how many bamboo shoots did you get?"

Cuicui tipped her basket upside down on the ground — besides ten or so small shoots, there was a large bunch of tiger-ear grass.

The old ferryman looked at Cuicui, and Cuicui, her cheeks flushed crimson, ran off.

Eighteen

A month passed quietly, and all the aches in people's hearts seemed to have been healed by those long, bright days. The weather was especially hot, and everyone was busy sweating, drinking cool rice wine, with no room for worries — and worries, finding no place in a life like that, simply couldn't stay. Every day Cuicui went to nap in the shade on the north side of the white pagoda. It was cool up high, and the bamboo groves on both hills were full of bamboo sparrows and other birds whose calls made you go limp with ease, so that even in her sleep she was carried along by birdsong, and the dreams she had were often the most absurd dreams imaginable.

This was no fault of anyone's. Poets can write whole volumes of verse over a small thing, sculptors can carve figures of flesh and blood from a block of stone, painters with a stroke of green, a stroke of red, a stroke of grey, can produce painting after painting of magical power — who among them is not driven by the memory of a smiling shadow, or the mark of a furrowed brow, to produce such remarkable things? Cuicui could not use words, could not use stone, could not use color to transfer the love and longing in her heart onto something else — she could only let her heart run wild through the most absurd of things. From this secret world she often drew a thrilling, joyful excitement. The unknowable future shook her feelings deeply, and she had no way of hiding all that tender foolishness from her grandfather.

As for grandfather — one could say he knew everything. Yet in truth he knew nothing at all. He understood that Cuicui did not dislike Nuosong, but he did not know how Nuosong felt. He had been rebuffed by both the boat boss and Nuosong, yet he was not discouraged.

"Things have to be arranged properly to be right — everything has its fate!" He thought this way, and so the whole affair seemed all the more fraught with obstacles. With his eyes wide open, the dreams he dreamed were even more wild and far-reaching than those of his granddaughter Cuicui. He asked every local who crossed the ferry about the lives of Nuosong and his father, caring about them as if they were his own family. But strangely, for this very reason he had come to dread running into the boat boss and Nuosong. Whenever he saw them he didn't know what to say, and fell back on his old habit of rubbing his hands together, losing all composure. Both Nuosong and his father understood what the old man meant, but the one who had died had left a sorrowful impression carved into both their hearts, and so the two of them acted as though they had no idea what the old ferryman was getting at, and simply let the days go by.

Clearly having had no dreams at night, the grandfather would say to Cuicui in the morning:

"Cuicui, Cuicui, I had such a frightening dream last night!"

Cuicui would ask, "What kind of frightening dream?"

And he would pretend to be recalling the dream, all the while studying Cuicui's small face and long eyebrows, and describe the good dreams he had dreamed with his eyes wide open at some other hour. Needless to say, those dreams were never truly frightening at all.

All rivers must flow to the sea — no matter how far the talk wandered at the start, it always came back in the end to the thing that made Cuicui blush. When Cuicui began to look a little displeased, showing a trace of embarrassment, the old ferryman would seem to grow a little alarmed himself, and quickly explain himself, covering over the real meaning of what he had said with idle chatter.

"Cuicui, I didn't mean it that way, I didn't mean it that way. Grandpa is old and muddled, full of nonsense."

But sometimes Cuicui would quietly listen to all her grandfather's jokes and rambling, and by the end she would still be smiling with pursed lips.

Cuicui would sometimes suddenly say:

"Grandpa, you really are a little muddled!"

Grandfather would hear this and fall silent. He was about to say, "I have a whole lot on my mind," but before he could, someone calling for the ferry took him away.

The weather was hot, and travelers coming from far away with seventy-jin loads on their shoulders would reach the bank and, craving the cool, not move on right away. They would squat by the rocks near the tea jar, drink cool tea, swap the "chuichui bang" pipe with their companions, and chat with the ferryman.Many fabricated stories were told here, overheard by the old ferryman. Sometimes travelers, finding the stream so clear, would sit by the bank washing their feet or bathing, staying longer and talking more. Grandfather would pass these stories on to Cuicui, and Cuicui came to understand a great deal about the world. The rise and fall of goods prices, the cost of sedan chairs and boat rides, how the raft men worked their dozen or so big oars as they floated their rafts down the rapids, how the big-footed women on the small opium boats prepared their pipes... nothing was left out.

Nuosong the Second Young Master returned to Chadong from escorting goods in eastern Sichuan. It was nearly dusk, the stream was very still, and Grandfather and Cuicui were in the vegetable garden checking on the radish seedlings. Cuicui had slept too long during the day and felt a little restless. Thinking she heard someone calling hoarsely for the ferry, she rushed down to the bank ahead of everyone else. As she came down the slope, she saw two people standing at the dock in the slanting evening light, their backs clearly visible — it was none other than Nuosong the Second Young Master and his family's hired hand! Cuicui was startled, and like a small animal spotting a hunter, she turned and ran off into the bamboo grove on the hillside. But the two men by the stream, hearing footsteps, turned around and understood exactly what had happened. They waited a while and still no one came, so the hired hand called out hoarsely for the ferry again.

The old ferryman heard it perfectly clearly, yet he stayed crouched in the radish patch counting vegetables, finding it rather amusing. He had already seen Cuicui walk over, and he knew she must have recognized who was calling for the ferry and was now crouching up on that high rock, ignoring them. Cuicui was young and irresponsible — the travelers couldn't make her come, and there was nothing they could do about it, so they had no choice but to shout themselves hoarse calling for the ferry. The hired hand called out several times, and seeing no one come, he stopped and said to the Second Young Master, "What's going on here? Don't tell me the old man's fallen ill and keeled over, leaving only Cuicui?" The Second Young Master said, "Wait and see, it's nothing." So they waited a while. While they stood quietly waiting, the old ferryman in the garden was thinking to himself, "Could that be the Second Young Master?" He seemed worried about disturbing Cuicui, so he stayed crouched there without moving.

But after a while, someone called for the ferry again from the bank, with a slightly different voice — this time it was truly the Second Young Master's voice. Annoyed? Tired of waiting? Had they quarreled? The old ferryman made his guesses as he ran down to the bank. When he got there, he saw the two men had already boarded the boat, and one of them was indeed the Second Young Master. The old ferryman called out in surprise:

"Oh, Second Young Master, you're back!"

The young man seemed quite displeased. "Back. — What's wrong with this ferry? We waited forever and not a soul came!"

"I thought —" The old ferryman looked around and saw no sign of Cuicui, only the yellow dog running down from the bamboo grove on the hill. Knowing Cuicui had gone up the hill, he changed his words. "I thought you'd already crossed."

"Already crossed! Without you getting on the boat, who'd dare to set off?" said the hired hand. Just then a water bird skimmed across the surface of the stream. "The kingfisher's heading home to roost, and we still need to get back for supper!"

"Plenty of time, plenty of time to get to the river street," said the old ferryman, already jumping onto the boat. In his heart he was saying, "So you don't want to take over this ferry after all!" He pulled the mooring rope and the boat left the bank.

"Second Young Master, you must be tired from the road!..."

The old ferryman kept talking, while the Second Young Master listened without expression, neither agreeing nor disagreeing. When the boat reached the other bank, the young man and the hired hand shouldered their carrying poles and headed off over the hill. That faint impression of indifference stayed with the old ferryman. He stood watching their backs, shook his fist at them three times in mock threat, let out a low growl, and pulled the boat back.

Nineteen

From Nuosong the Second Young Master's perspective, the fact that Cuicui had run into the bamboo grove and the old ferryman had taken so long to come down to the boat did not bode well for the future. Although the old ferryman's words had all been meant to convey that "this matter has a chance," his fumbling, awkward manner of saying so was entirely off-putting. The Second Young Master thought of his brother and began to misread the whole situation. He felt a measure of indignation and irritation. On the third day after he returned home, someone came from Zhongzhai to sound things out, staying at Shunshun's place on the river street. He put the question to Shunshun, wanting to know whether the Second Young Master still intended to accept the new mill. Shunshun then asked the Second Young Master what he himself thought.

The Second Young Master said, "Father, if you think this is for your sake — that having another mill and another person in the family would make you happy — then go ahead and agree to it. But if it's for my sake, I need to think it over carefully and talk about it another day. I still don't know whether I deserve a mill or a ferry: maybe my fate only allows me to pole a ferry!"

The man who had come to sound things out memorized these words and went back to Zhongzhai to report. When he crossed at Bixi Ju, he came to the old ferryman, and remembering what the Second Young Master had said, he couldn't help smiling to himself. The old ferryman asked him where he was from and learned he was from Zhongzhai, then asked what business had brought him to Chadong.

The discreet man from Zhongzhai said:

"No particular business — just went to sit a while at the boat chief Shunshun's place on the river street."

"Nobody goes to the temple for nothing — you must have had something to say!"

"A few words were exchanged."

"What was said?" The man said no more, but the old ferryman pressed on. "I heard that you Zhongzhai people are thinking of giving the mill by the big river along with your daughter to Shunshun on the river street — has that come to anything?"

The man from Zhongzhai smiled. "It's settled. I asked Shunshun, and Shunshun is very willing to form a family bond with the Zhongzhai people. And I asked the young fellow too..."

"What did the young fellow say?"

"He said: I've got a mill in front of me and a ferry — I was thinking I wanted the ferry, but now I'll take the mill. A ferry moves around, not as stable as a mill. That boy knows how to reckon."

The man from Zhongzhai was a grain broker, and his words were carefully weighed. He knew perfectly well what "ferry" referred to, but he didn't say it outright. He saw the old ferryman's lips moving, about to speak, so he quickly added:

"Everything is fate, not half a point is up to us. Poor Shunshun's eldest son — such a fine-looking young man, to drown in the water like that!"

That remark stabbed the old ferryman in the heart, and he swallowed the question he had been about to ask. After the man from Zhongzhai went ashore and walked away, the old ferryman stood at the bow of the boat in a daze for a long time. He turned over in his mind the Second Young Master's cold, distant manner when he had crossed the ferry the other day, and felt deeply unhappy.

Cuicui had been playing happily by the pagoda and came up to the high rock by the stream wanting Grandfather to sing her a song. Seeing that Grandfather paid her no attention, she grumbled all the way down to the bank. When she got there she found Grandfather looking utterly dejected, and she couldn't understand why. When Cuicui arrived, Grandfather looked at her bright, cheerful dark face and gave a rough laugh. Someone from across the stream was calling for the ferry with a load of goods, so he said nothing, silently pulled the boat across the stream, and then in the middle of the river suddenly burst into loud song. After ferrying the passengers across, Grandfather jumped up onto the dock and walked over to Cuicui, still laughing that rough laugh of his, and patted her on the forehead.

Cuicui said:

"Grandpa, what's the matter with you? Are you having a heatstroke? Go lie down in the shade and rest — I'll mind the boat!"

"You mind the boat — fine, this boat is yours to mind!"

The old ferryman seemed to have truly come down with heatstroke, feeling a heaviness in his chest. Though he kept up a tough front in front of Cuicui, once he went back inside alone he found some broken bits of porcelain, pricked his arms and legs a few times to let out some dark blood, then lay down on the bed and slept.

Cuicui kept watch over the boat by herself, feeling a strange and happy lightness in her heart. She thought, "If Grandpa won't sing for me, I'll sing for myself!"

She sang many songs. The old ferryman lay on the bed with his eyes closed, listening to every line, his mind in turmoil. But he knew this was not the kind of illness that could lay him low — he would be up again tomorrow. He thought about going into town the next day, going to the river street to have a look, and then thought of many other things.

But the next day, though he got out of bed, his head still felt heavy. Grandfather had truly fallen ill. Cuicui seemed to have grown more sensible — she brewed a pot of herbal medicine for Grandfather and made him drink it, and out in the vegetable garden behind the house she picked garlic shoots and soaked them in rice water to make pickled garlic. She tended the boat while also slipping back to the house at every spare moment to check on Grandfather, asking him this and that. Grandfather said nothing, suffering alone with a secret. After three days in bed, he was somehow better. He walked around in front of and behind the house — his bones were still firm — and with one matter weighing on his mind, he prepared to go into town and over to the river street. Cuicui couldn't see any urgent reason why Grandfather had to go into town that very day, and asked him not to go.

The old ferryman rubbed his hands together, weighing whether he should explain his reason. Cuicui's dark oval face and her bright, liquid eyes made him let out a long sigh.

He said, "I have important business — I have to go today!"

Cuicui said with a wry smile, "How important can it be — isn't it just..."

The old ferryman knew Cuicui's temperament, and hearing the slight displeasure in her tone, he stopped insisting on going. He set down the bamboo tube and the embroidered pouch he had prepared to take with him on the side table, then said with a somewhat ingratiating smile, "All right, I won't go. You're worried I'll fall and hurt myself, so I won't go. I thought the morning air wasn't too hot and I could finish my business in town and come back — but it's fine not to go. I'll go tomorrow!"

Cuicui said softly and gently, "Tomorrow is fine too. Your legs are still weak — lie down properly for another day before you get up."

The old ferryman seemed still reluctant at heart. He walked out with his hands swinging loose, and nearly tripped over a mallet for making straw sandals lying by the threshold. When he steadied himself, Cuicui said with a wry smile, "Grandpa, see? Still won't admit it!" The old ferryman picked up the mallet, flung it into the corner of the room, and said, "Grandpa's old! In a few days I'll catch a leopard for you to see!"

That afternoon a passing shower fell, and the old ferryman talked it over properly with Cuicui and went into town after all. Cuicui couldn't go with him, so she sent the yellow dog along instead. In town, an acquaintance caught the old ferryman and talked with him for a long time about the price of salt and rice. He also stopped by the garrison commander's office to look at some newly purchased mules and horses before finally making his way to Shunshun's place on the river street. When he got there, he found Shunshun playing cards with three other men and couldn't bring himself to interrupt, so he stood behind them watching the game for a while. Later Shunshun invited him to have a drink, but he declined, using his recent illness as an excuse. The card game showed no sign of breaking up, and the old ferryman didn't want to leave just yet. Shunshun didn't seem to understand why he was waiting and kept his attention on the cards in his hand. Eventually another person noticed the old ferryman's manner and asked if he had something on his mind. Only then did the old ferryman fidget awkwardly in his usual way, rubbing his two big hands together, and say there was nothing else — he just wanted a word with the boat chief.

Only then did the boat chief understand why the old man had been standing there watching the game for so long. He turned to the old ferryman with a laugh.

"Why didn't you say so sooner? I thought you were watching me play to pick up some tricks!"

"It's nothing much — just three or five words. I didn't want to spoil the fun, so I didn't dare speak up." The boat chief tossed his cards down on the table and walked laughing toward the back room, with the old ferryman following behind.

"What is it?" the boat chief asked. His manner suggested he already knew what the old man had come to say, and he looked faintly sympathetic.

"I heard from a man from Zhongzhai that you're planning to form a family bond with the Zhongzhai headman — is that true?"

The boat chief saw the old ferryman's eyes fixed on his face, waiting for a satisfying answer, and said, "There is such a thing." The tone of his reply carried the unspoken meaning: "And what of it?"

The old ferryman said, "Really?"

The other man said just as naturally, "Really." The meaning was still the same: "And what if it is?"

The old ferryman asked with a show of composure, "And the Second Young Master?"

The boat chief said, "The Second Young Master took a boat down to Taoyuan quite a few days ago!"

The Second Young Master's departure for Taoyuan had in fact come after a quarrel with his father. Though the boat chief was an unusually open-hearted man, he was not willing to have the girl who had indirectly caused his first son's death come and be his second son's wife — that much was plain. Under local custom, such matters were considered the business of the young people alone, not for adults to interfere with. If the Second Young Master truly loved Cuicui and Cuicui loved the Second Young Master, he would not have objected to such a love-entangled marriage. But somehow, the old ferryman's anxious meddling in the affair had created a misunderstanding between the boat chief and his son on one side and the old ferryman on the other. The boat chief, thinking over recent family events, felt they were all connected to this meddlesome old ferryman. He showed nothing outwardly, but there was a knot in his heart.

The boat chief cut the old ferryman off before he could say another word, and said in a somewhat blunt tone:

"Uncle, let it go. Our mouths should only be for drinking — stop trying to sing songs for the young ones. I understand your intentions perfectly, and they're good. But I ask you to understand mine as well. I think we should only talk about matters that concern ourselves, and stop trying to find paths for the young people."

After being knocked flat by that blow, the old ferryman still wanted to say a few words, but the boat chief gave him no chance to speak and pulled him back out to the card table.

The old ferryman had nothing more to say. He looked at the boat chief, who was still smiling and joking about various things, but whose heart seemed heavy as he slapped his cards down hard on the table. The old ferryman said nothing, picked up his bamboo hat, and left on his own.

It was still early. The old ferryman was in low spirits and went back into town to find Yang Mabing. The soldier was in the middle of drinking, and though the old ferryman pleaded illness, he couldn't avoid having three or five cups himself. On the way back to Bixi Ju he had walked fast enough to work up a sweat, so he washed himself down with stream water. Feeling very tired, he asked Cuicui to mind the boat,went home to sleep.

At dusk the air was heavy and oppressive, with red dragonflies darting all over the surface of the stream. Clouds had already gathered in the sky, and a hot wind sent the bamboo groves on both hillsides rustling loudly. By the look of things, there would be a heavy rain that night. Cuicui stood watch at the ferry, watching the dragonflies skimming back and forth over the water, her mind in turmoil. Seeing how pale and drawn her grandfather looked, she couldn't set her heart at ease, and hurried back home. "Grandpa, how many pairs of straw sandals do you need? Aren't there already fourteen pairs by the bed? Why won't you just lie down and rest?"

"Cuicui, there's going to be a heavy rain and loud thunder tonight. Later on, tie our boat down under the cliff — this rain is going to be fierce."

Cuicui said: "Grandpa, I'm really scared!" But what Cuicui feared didn't seem to be the thunderstorm coming that night.

The old ferryman seemed to understand what she meant, and said: "What is there to be afraid of? Whatever is meant to come will come — no need to be afraid!"

Twenty

That night, sure enough, a heavy rain fell, accompanied by terrifying thunder. When lightning flashed across the rooftop, a tremendous crack of thunder followed right after. Cuicui had cried all day and been busy all day, and by now was utterly exhausted. She leaned her head against the coffin and dozed off. The two farmhands and Mabing had eaten their late meal and drunk a couple of cups of wine, and were still full of energy, taking turns singing the funeral songs. But after a short while Cuicui woke again, as if from some dream, and realizing upon waking that her grandfather was truly dead, began to weep softly once more.

"Cuicui, Cuicui, don't cry — crying won't bring the dead back!"

Bald Chen Sisi then told a joke about a new bride who wouldn't stop crying, laced with three or five crude words, which set the two farmhands chuckling for quite some time. The yellow dog barked outside. Cuicui opened the front door and went out to stand for a moment. All around she could hear the sound of insects, the moonlight was beautiful overhead, and great stars were set into the deep blue sky — everything utterly still and gentle. Cuicui thought:

"Is this real? Is Grandpa really dead?"

Old Mabing had followed behind her, for he knew that a young girl's heart could be narrow, and that a smoldering fire buried under ash might show no trace on the surface — it was entirely possible that, seeing her grandfather gone and all hope lost, she might throw herself off a cliff or hang herself, wanting to follow her grandfather. So he kept a careful watch on Cuicui at all times.

Seeing Cuicui standing there in a daze, not turning back even after a long while, old Mabing cleared his throat and called to her:

"Cuicui, the dew is falling — aren't you cold?"

"Not cold."

"What fine weather!"

"Ah…" A large shooting star made Cuicui cry out softly.

Then another shooting star streaked across the sky to the south. From across the stream came the call of an owl.

"Cuicui," old Mabing said gently, now standing side by side with her, "go inside and sleep — don't let your mind wander."

Cuicui silently returned to stand before her grandfather's coffin, sat down on the ground, and began to sob again. The two farmhands keeping watch inside had already fallen asleep.

Yang Mabing said softly: "Don't cry. Don't cry. Your grandfather is grieving too — what good does it do to cry your eyes swollen and your throat raw. Listen to me, I know everything that was on your grandfather's mind, and I'll take care of it all. I'll arrange everything properly, in a way that does right by your grandfather. I know how to arrange things — all kinds of things. I'll find someone your grandfather would be happy about and you'd be happy about too, to take over this ferry. If things don't go as we wish, old as I am, I can still pick up a sickle and fight them to the death. Cuicui, don't worry — I'll take care of everything!…"

Somewhere far away a rooster crowed. The old Taoist murmured to himself from the bed over there, half asleep: "Is it dawn? Too early!"

Twenty-one

Early in the morning, helpers came hurrying from town with ropes and carrying poles.

When the old ferryman's small plain wooden coffin was carried by six men up to the hillside behind the collapsed pagoda for burial, boat master Shunshun, Mabing, Cuicui, the old Taoist, and the yellow dog all followed behind. At the edge of the grave that had been dug in advance, the old Taoist first jumped down according to custom, placed a few grains of cinnabar and white rice at the four corners and center of the pit, burned some paper money, then climbed out and told the pallbearers to lower the coffin. Cuicui, her voice hoarse, wailed and threw herself over the coffin, refusing to move. Only after Mabing forcibly pulled her away could the coffin be moved. In a moment the coffin was lowered into the pit, the ropes were pulled free, the direction was adjusted, and it was covered with fresh earth. Cuicui still sat on the ground weeping. The old Taoist needed to return to town to perform rites for another family, and crossed over on the ferry. Shunshun entrusted everything to old Mabing and also hurried back to town. The helpers all went to the stream to wash their hands. Each had their own affairs to attend to at home, and knowing the situation of this household, not wanting to impose further, they left without disturbing the mistress of the house and crossed back home on the ferry. And so Bixi Gorge was left with only three people: Cuicui, old Mabing, and bald Chen Sisi, who had been sent by Shunshun's household to help tend the ferry for the time being. The yellow dog, having been hit by a stone thrown by the bald man, seemed quite displeased with him and kept up a low, steady barking.

That afternoon, Cuicui discussed things with old Mabing and asked him to go back to town to arrange for someone at the garrison to look after his horse, then return to Bixi Gorge to keep her company. When old Mabing came back to Bixi Gorge, bald Chen Sisi was sent back to town.

Cuicui still worked the ferry herself with the yellow dog, letting old Mabing sit on the high bank above the stream to pass the time, or sing to her in his raspy old voice.

Three days later, Shunshun came to discuss taking Cuicui back to stay at his home, but Cuicui wanted to watch over her grandfather's grave and was unwilling to go into town just yet. She only asked Shunshun to put in a word at the town office on her behalf, to allow Yang Mabing to stay with her for the time being. Shunshun agreed to this and left.

Yang Mabing was a man past fifty, and his gift for storytelling was a cut above Cuicui's grandfather's. On top of that, he was especially attentive to everything, quick and neat in all he did. Living with Cuicui this way, it was as though she had lost one grandfather but gained a new uncle. When someone crossing the ferry asked about her poor grandfather, or when she thought of him at dusk, Cuicui's heart would ache with a deep sense of loneliness. But as the lonely days wore on, that feeling gradually grew a little lighter. Each day at dusk and in the evenings, the two of them would sit together on the high bank in front of the house by the stream, talking about the old man lying in the damp earth — many things Cuicui had not known before, and hearing them now made her heart grow tender. They also spoke of Cuicui's father, that soldier who wanted both love and honor, and how in those days, dressed in the uniform of a Green Standard soldier, he had captured a young woman's heart. And they spoke of Cuicui's mother, how gifted she was at singing, and how the songs she sang were so popular at the time.

Times had changed, and naturally everything was different now — the emperor no longer sat on the throne, to say nothing of ordinary people. Yang Mabing thought of himself as a young groom, leading his horse to Bixi Gorge to sing to Cuicui's mother, only to be ignored by her — and now here he was, the sole support and sole trusted guardian of this orphaned girl. He couldn't help but smile bitterly.

Because the two of them talked every evening about the grandfather and everything connected to this family, they eventually came to speak of all that had happened before the old ferryman's death, and Cuicui came to understand many things her grandfather had never mentioned while he was alive. The Second Master's singing, the death of Shunshun's eldest son, the coldness of Shunshun and his son toward her grandfather, the people from Zhongjia village using a mill as a dowry to tempt Nuosong the Second Master, the Second Master still haunted by the memory of his brother's death, and unable to get any response from Cuicui, being pressed by his family to accept that mill,The meaning was still on the ferry, so he left in a fit of anger — how Grandpa's death was connected to Cuicui… everything Cuicui had not understood before, she now understood completely. After piecing it all together, Cuicui cried through an entire night.

　　After the twenty-eighth day of mourning, the boat chief Shunshun sent someone to invite the soldier into town to discuss bringing Cuicui to his home as a wife for the second son. But since the second son was still in Chenzhou, there was no point in raising the matter yet — she could move to the riverside street first and wait to see what the second son thought when he returned. The soldier felt this was something that had to be decided by Cuicui herself. When he came back, he relayed Shunshun's intentions to Cuicui, and then offered his own suggestion: since no formal arrangement had been settled, it would not be right to move into a stranger's household — it would be better to wait at Bixi Ridge, and when the second son sailed back, they could see what he had to say.

　　Once this plan was decided, the old soldier believed the second son would return before long. He entrusted his horse to someone at the garrison and stayed at Bixi Ridge to keep Cuicui company, passing one day after another.

　　The white pagoda at Bixi Ridge was connected to the feng shui of Chadong, and now that it had collapsed, it naturally had to be rebuilt. In addition to donations from the garrison in town, the tax office, various merchant houses, and ordinary residents, people from the larger villages also went around with ledgers collecting money. Since rebuilding the pagoda was not for any one person's benefit but an act of virtue and blessing that everyone should take part in, a large bamboo tube sealed at both ends and sawn open in the middle was placed on the ferry so that passengers could freely drop money in as they crossed. When the tube was full, the soldier would carry it into town to the organizers and bring back an empty one. Passengers who crossed and saw that the old ferryman was gone, and noticed the white thread braided into Cuicui's hair, understood that the old man had finished his share of work in this world and was lying quietly in the earth. They would look at Cuicui with sympathetic eyes and reach into their pockets to push coins into the bamboo tube. "May heaven protect you — may the departed go to the Western Paradise, and may the living be forever safe." Cuicui understood what those donors meant, and her heart ached; she quickly turned away and pulled the ferry rope.

　　By winter, the collapsed white pagoda had been rebuilt. But the young man who had sung in the moonlight — whose voice had made Cuicui's soul float gently upward in her dreams — had still not returned to Chadong.
