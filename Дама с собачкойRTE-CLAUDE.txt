Anton Pavlovich Chekhov

The Lady with the Dog

     

I

     

     People were saying that a new face had appeared on the promenade: a lady with a dog. Dmitri Dmitrich Gurov, who had already spent two weeks in Yalta and had settled in, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in the pavilion at Verné, he watched a young lady pass along the promenade — a short blonde in a beret, with a white Pomeranian trotting behind her.

     After that he kept running into her in the town garden and on the square several times a day. She walked alone, always in the same beret, with the white Pomeranian; no one knew who she was, and people simply called her: the lady with the dog.

     "If she's here without a husband and without anyone she knows," Gurov reasoned, "it wouldn't hurt to get acquainted with her."

     He was not yet forty, but he already had a twelve-year-old daughter and two sons in secondary school. He had been married off young, while he was still a second-year student, and his wife now seemed half as old again as he was. She was a tall woman with dark eyebrows, erect, dignified, imposing, and, as she called herself, a thinking person. She read a great deal, used the new spelling in her letters, called her husband not Dmitri but Dimitri, and he secretly considered her narrow-minded, limited, inelegant; he was afraid of her and disliked being at home. He had begun cheating on her long ago, cheated often, and probably for that reason spoke of women almost always disparagingly, and when they were mentioned in his presence he would call them:

     — The inferior race!

     It seemed to him that he had been taught enough by bitter experience to call them whatever he liked, yet without the "inferior race" he could not have lasted even two days. In the company of men he felt bored and ill at ease; with them he was taciturn and cold, but when he was among women he felt free and knew what to talk about with them and how to carry himself; even silence with them came easily to him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature there was something attractive, elusive, that drew women to him, charmed them; he knew this, and some force drew him toward them as well.

     Repeated experience — truly bitter experience — had long since taught him that every intimacy, which at first so pleasantly varies life and seems a sweet and easy adventure, among decent people, especially among Muscovites who are slow to move and irresolute, inevitably grows into a whole problem of extraordinary complexity, and the situation in the end becomes unbearable. But at every new meeting with an interesting woman this experience somehow slipped from his memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

     And so one evening he was dining in the garden, and the lady in the beret came walking over unhurriedly to take the table next to his. Her expression, her walk, her dress, her hair told him that she was from respectable society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here… There was a great deal of untruth in the stories about the looseness of local morals; he despised them and knew that such stories were for the most part made up by people who would have been glad to sin themselves if they had known how, but when the lady sat down at the next table three steps away from him, he recalled those stories of easy conquests, of trips into the mountains, and the tempting thought of a quick, fleeting affair, a romance with an unknown woman whose name and surname you don't know, suddenly took hold of him.

     He gently beckoned to the Pomeranian, and when it came over he shook his finger at it. The Pomeranian growled. Gurov shook his finger again.

     The lady glanced at him and immediately looked down.

     — He doesn't bite, — she said and blushed.

     — May I give him a bone? — And when she nodded in agreement, he asked amiably: — Have you been in Yalta long?

     — About five days.

     — And I'm already dragging through my second week here.

     They were silent for a moment.

     — Time passes quickly, and yet it's so dull here! — she said, not looking at him.

     — It's just the thing to say, that it's dull here. An ordinary person lives somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra — and he's not bored, but then he comes here: "Oh, how dull! Oh, the dust!" You'd think he'd come from Granada.

     She laughed.Then both continued eating in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked side by side — and a playful, easy conversation began, the kind shared by free, contented people who don't care where they go or what they talk about. They strolled and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was lilac-colored, so soft and warm, and a golden streak ran across it from the moon. They talked about how stuffy it was after the hot day. Gurov told her he was from Moscow, a philologist by education, but worked at a bank; had once trained to sing in a private opera, but gave it up, and owned two houses in Moscow… From her he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg, but had married in S., where she had already lived for two years, that she would stay in Yalta for about another month, and that her husband, who also wanted a rest, might come for her. She could not quite explain where her husband worked — in the provincial administration or the provincial zemstvo board — and she found that funny herself. Gurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

Afterward, back in his room, he thought about her, about how she would probably run into him tomorrow. That was how it should be. As he was falling asleep, he recalled that she had so recently been a schoolgirl, studying, just like his daughter was now; he recalled how much shyness and awkwardness there still was in her laugh, in her conversation with a stranger — this must have been the first time in her life she had been alone, in such a situation, where she was followed, looked at, and spoken to with one secret purpose only, which she could not help but sense. He remembered her slender, delicate neck, her beautiful gray eyes.

"There's something pathetic about her, all the same," he thought, and drifted off to sleep.

II

A week had passed since they met. It was a holiday. The rooms were stuffy, and out in the streets the dust swirled in gusts, blowing hats off. All day long there was a thirst for something, and Gurov kept stopping by the pavilion to offer Anna Sergeyevna either water with syrup or ice cream. There was nowhere to escape.

In the evening, when it had quieted down a little, they went to the pier to watch the steamer come in. There were many people strolling on the quay; they had gathered to meet someone and were holding bouquets. Two features of the festive Yalta crowd stood out clearly: the older women were dressed like young ones, and there were a great many generals.

Because of the rough sea, the steamer arrived late, after the sun had already set, and it turned about for a long time before pulling up to the pier. Anna Sergeyevna watched the steamer and the passengers through her lorgnette, as if searching for someone she knew, and whenever she turned to Gurov her eyes were shining. She talked a great deal, her questions were abrupt, and she herself immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

The festive crowd began to disperse, faces were no longer visible, the wind had died down completely, and Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood there as if waiting to see whether anyone else would come off the steamer. Anna Sergeyevna had fallen silent and was smelling her flowers, not looking at Gurov.

"The weather has turned nicer toward evening," he said. "Where shall we go now? Shall we drive somewhere?"

She said nothing.

Then he looked at her intently and suddenly put his arms around her and kissed her on the lips, and he was enveloped by the fragrance and dampness of the flowers, and at once he glanced around nervously — had anyone seen?

"Let's go to your place…" he said quietly.

And they both walked away quickly.

Her room was stuffy and smelled of the perfume she had bought at a Japanese shop. Gurov, looking at her now, thought: "What strange encounters life brings!" From his past he had kept the memory of carefree, good-natured women, made cheerful by love, grateful to him for their happiness, however brief; and of women like his wife, for instance, who loved without sincerity, with too much talk, affectedly, with hysteria, with an expression as though it were not love or passion but something far more significant; and of two or three others, very beautiful and cold, whose faces would suddenly flash a predatory look, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it could give — and these were women no longer in their first youth, capricious, unreasoning, domineering, unintelligent, and when Gurov grew cold toward them,their beauty stirred hatred in him, and the lace on their undergarments seemed to him like fish scales.

     But here there was that same timidity, the awkwardness of inexperienced youth, an uneasy feeling; and there was an impression of confusion, as if someone had suddenly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the little dog," treated what had happened in a peculiar way, very seriously, as though it were her own downfall — so it seemed, and it was strange and out of place. Her features had drooped and faded, and on either side of her face her long hair hung sadly down; she sat lost in thought in a dejected pose, like a sinner in an old painting.

     "It's not right," she said. "You're the first one not to respect me now."

     On the table in the room there was a watermelon. Gurov cut himself a slice and began eating it unhurriedly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

     Anna Sergeyevna was touching; she had about her an air of purity belonging to a decent, naive woman who had seen little of life; a solitary candle burning on the table barely lit her face, but it was clear that her heart was troubled.

     "Why would I stop respecting you?" asked Gurov. "You don't know what you're saying."

     "May God forgive me!" she said, and her eyes filled with tears. "This is terrible."

     "You sound as though you're making excuses."

     "What excuse could I make? I am a bad, wicked woman, I despise myself and have no thought of making excuses. It's not my husband I've deceived, but myself. And not just now — I've been deceiving myself for a long time. My husband may be an honest, good man, but he is a lackey! I don't know what he does there, how he serves, I only know that he is a lackey. When I married him I was twenty years old, I was consumed by curiosity, I wanted something better; for there must be, I told myself, another kind of life. I wanted to live! To live and to live… Curiosity was burning me… you don't understand this, but I swear to God, I could no longer control myself, something was happening to me, I could not be held back, I told my husband I was ill and came here… And here I've been going about as if in a daze, like a madwoman… and now I've become a vulgar, worthless woman whom anyone may despise."

     Gurov was already bored with listening; he was irritated by her naive tone, by this repentance, so unexpected and so out of place; had it not been for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a part.

     "I don't understand," he said quietly, "what is it you want?"

     She hid her face against his chest and pressed close to him.

     "Believe me, believe me, I beg you…" she said. "I love an honest, pure life, and sin is loathsome to me, I don't know myself what I'm doing. Simple people say: the devil led me astray. And I can say the same of myself now, that I was led astray by the devil."

     "There, there…" he murmured.

     He looked into her still, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke to her softly and gently, and little by little she calmed down, and her cheerfulness returned; they both began to laugh.

     Afterwards, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment, the town with its cypresses looked quite dead, but the sea still roared and beat against the shore; a single launch rocked on the waves, and on it a lantern glimmered sleepily.

     They found a cab and drove to Oreanda.

     "I just now learned your surname downstairs in the lobby: it says von Dideritz on the board," said Gurov. "Is your husband German?"

     "No, I believe his grandfather was German, but he himself is Russian Orthodox."

     In Oreanda they sat on a bench not far from the church, looked down at the sea, and were silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist, white clouds stood motionless on the mountaintops. The leaves did not stir on the trees, cicadas called, and the monotonous, muffled sound of the sea rising from below spoke of rest, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. So it had roared below when there was yet no Yalta, no Oreanda here; so it roars now and will go on roaring, just as indifferently and hollowly, when we are gone. And in this constancy, in this utter indifference to the life and death of each of us, there lies perhaps the pledge of our eternal salvation, the unceasing movement of life on earth, the unceasing advance toward perfection. Sitting beside a young woman who at dawn seemed so beautiful,calmed and enchanted by this fairy-tale setting — the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the wide sky — Gurov thought about how, in essence, if you really think about it, everything in this world is beautiful, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget about the higher purposes of existence, about our own human dignity.

Some man approached — a watchman, probably — looked at them and walked away. And this small detail seemed so mysterious and also beautiful. You could see a steamer coming from Feodosia, lit by the morning glow, already without its lights.

     — There's dew on the grass, — said Anna Sergeyevna after a silence.

     — Yes. It's time to go home.

     They returned to the city.

     Then every afternoon they met on the embankment, had lunch together, dined, strolled, and marveled at the sea. She complained that she slept badly and that her heart beat anxiously, asking the same questions over and over, stirred now by jealousy, now by fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or in the garden, when no one was nearby, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. The complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with glances over the shoulder and fear of being seen, the heat, the smell of the sea, and the constant flicker of idle, well-dressed, well-fed people before his eyes seemed to transform him entirely; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how alluring, he was impatiently passionate, never left her side, while she often fell into thought and kept asking him to admit that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, and only saw in her a vulgar woman. Almost every evening they drove somewhere out of town, to Oreanda or to the waterfall; and the excursions were a success, the impressions invariably beautiful and majestic every time.

     They expected her husband to arrive. But a letter came from him in which he informed her that his eyes had been giving him trouble and begged his wife to come home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeyevna grew hurried.

     — It's a good thing I'm leaving, — she told Gurov. — It's fate itself.

     She traveled by horse-drawn carriage, and he saw her off. They rode the whole day. When she took her seat in the express train and the second bell rang, she said:

     — Let me look at you once more… One more look. Just like this.

     She did not cry, but she was sad, as if she were ill, and her face was trembling.

     — I will think of you… remember you, — she said. — God be with you, stay well. Don't think badly of me. We are saying goodbye forever, it has to be this way, because we should never have met at all. Well, God be with you.

     The train departed quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and within a minute the noise could no longer be heard, as if everything had conspired on purpose to end this sweet oblivion, this madness, as quickly as possible. And, left alone on the platform, gazing into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the chirping of the crickets and the humming of the telegraph wires with a feeling as though he had just woken up. And he thought about how here again in his life there had been another affair or adventure, and it too was already over, and all that remained now was a memory… He felt moved, sad, and experienced a slight remorse; after all, this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he had been friendly and warm toward her, yet all the while in his manner with her, in his tone and his caresses, there had flickered a shadow of light mockery, the coarse arrogance of a happy man who was, besides, nearly twice her age. She had called him kind, extraordinary, noble the whole time; evidently he had seemed to her not what he actually was, which meant he had unwittingly deceived her…

     Here at the station autumn was already in the air, the evening was cool.

     "It's time for me to head north too," thought Gurov, leaving the platform. "Time!"

III

Back home in Moscow everything was already wintry, the stoves were being heated, and in the mornings when the children were getting ready for school and drinking their tea it was dark, and the nanny would light a lamp for a little while. The frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sleigh rides, it is pleasant to see the white earth, the white rooftops, the air is soft and fine to breathe, and at such times one remembers younger years. The old linden trees and birches, white with frost, have a good-natured look about them, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms,and near them one no longer feels like thinking about mountains and the sea.

     Gurov was a Muscovite, and he returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and strolled along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the ringing of the bells, the recent trip and the places he had been lost all their charm for him. Little by little he plunged back into Moscow life, already reading three newspapers a day with eagerness and saying that he never read Moscow papers on principle. He was drawn again to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties, anniversaries, and he felt flattered that well-known lawyers and actors came to see him and that at the doctors' club he played cards with a professor. He could already eat a full serving of solyanka in a skillet…

     In a month or so, it seemed to him, Anna Sergeyevna would fade into a haze in his memory and would only occasionally appear in his dreams with a touching smile, as the others did. But more than a month passed, deep winter set in, and everything was still as clear in his memory as if he had parted with Anna Sergeyevna only yesterday. And the memories grew more and more vivid. Whether the voices of his children doing their lessons drifted into his study in the evening quiet, whether he heard a romance or an organ in a restaurant, or whether a blizzard howled in the fireplace, suddenly everything came back to life in his memory: what had happened on the pier, the early morning with mist on the mountains, the steamship from Feodosiya, and the kisses. He would pace the room for a long time, remembering and smiling, and then the memories would turn into daydreams, and in his imagination the past blended with what was yet to come. Anna Sergeyevna did not appear to him in dreams, but followed him everywhere like a shadow, watching him. With his eyes closed he could see her as if she were alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed better than he had been back then, in Yalta. In the evenings she gazed at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the soft rustle of her dress. On the street he would follow women with his eyes, searching for someone who looked like her…

     And already a strong longing tormented him to share his memories with someone. But at home it was impossible to speak of his love, and outside the home there was no one to talk to. Not the tenants, and not at the bank. And what would he say? Had he really been in love back then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or even simply interesting in his relationship with Anna Sergeyevna? And he was reduced to speaking vaguely about love, about women, and no one guessed what it was about, and only his wife raised her dark eyebrows and said:

     — Dimitry, the role of a ladies' man really doesn't suit you.

     One night, leaving the doctors' club with his card partner, a civil servant, he could not help himself and said:

     — If you only knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!

     The civil servant got into his sleigh and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

     — Dmitry Dmitrich!

     — What?

     — You were right earlier: the sturgeon was a bit off!

These words, so ordinary, suddenly for some reason irritated Gurov, seemed to him degrading and unclean. What savage manners, what faces! What senseless nights, what dull and unremarkable days! Frenzied card playing, gluttony, drunkenness, endless conversations always about the same things. Pointless affairs and talk always about the same things consume the best part of one's time, the best of one's energy, and in the end what remains is some stunted, wingless life, some kind of nonsense, and there is no escaping it, no running away, as if you were sitting in a madhouse or a prison ward!

     Gurov did not sleep all night and felt indignant, and then spent the whole next day with a headache. And on the following nights he slept badly, sitting up in bed thinking, or pacing from corner to corner. He was tired of his children, tired of the bank, he had no desire to go anywhere or talk about anything.

     In December, during the holidays, he prepared for a journey and told his wife he was going to Petersburg to do a favor for a certain young man — and he set off for S. Why? He himself did not quite know. He wanted to see Anna Sergeyevna and talk with her, to arrange a meeting if possible.

     He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room at the hotel, where the entire floor was covered with gray military cloth and there was an inkstand on the table, gray with dust, bearing a horseman with one hand raised holding a hat, the head broken off. The porter gave him the information he needed: von Dideritz lived on Staro-Goncharnaya Street, in his own house — not far from the hotel, lived well, comfortably, kept his own horses, everyone in town knew him. The porter pronounced it: Drydiriz.

     Gurov walked unhurriedly to Staro-Goncharnaya and found the house. Directly opposite the house stretched a long gray fence studded with nails.

     "A fence like that would make anyone want to run away," thought Gurov, glancing now at the windows, now at the fence.

     He reasoned: today is not a working day, so the husband is probably at home. And besides, it would be tactless to go in and cause embarrassment. If he sent a note, it might fall into the husband's hands and ruin everything. The best thing was to trust to chance. And so he kept walking up and down the street, waiting near the fence for that chance. He watched a beggar enter the gate and the dogs set upon him, then an hour later he heard piano playing, the sounds faint and indistinct. It must have been Anna Sergeyevna playing. The front door suddenly opened and an old woman came out, followed by the familiar white Pomeranian trotting behind her. Gurov wanted to call the dog, but his heart suddenly began to pound, and in his agitation he could not remember the Pomeranian's name.

     He walked on and grew more and more to hate the gray fence, and now thought with irritation that Anna Sergeyevna had forgotten about him and was perhaps already amusing herself with someone else, and that was so natural in the situation of a young woman who was forced from morning to night to look at that cursed fence. He went back to his room and sat for a long time on the sofa, not knowing what to do, then had dinner, then slept for a long time.

     "How stupid and tiresome all this is," he thought, waking up and looking at the dark windows; it was already evening. "So I've gone and slept. Now what am I going to do with myself tonight?"

     He sat on the bed, covered with a cheap gray blanket that looked like a hospital blanket, and taunted himself with vexation:

     "So much for the lady with the little dog… So much for the adventure… Just sit here and stew."

     That very morning at the station a poster with very large letters had caught his eye: "The Geisha" was being performed for the first time. He remembered this and drove to the theater.

     "It's quite possible she goes to opening nights," he thought.

     The theater was full. And here, as in all provincial theaters, there was a haze above the chandelier, the gallery was noisy and restless; in the front row before the performance began stood the local dandies with their hands clasped behind their backs; and here too, in the governor's box, in the seat of honor, sat the governor's daughter wearing a boa, while the governor himself modestly hid behind the curtain with only his hands visible; the curtain swayed, the orchestra took a long time tuning up. All the while the audience was filing in and taking their seats, Gurov searched eagerly with his eyes.

     Anna Sergeyevna came in too. She sat down in the third row, and when Gurov looked at her his heart tightened, and he understood clearly,that there was now no one in the whole world closer, dearer, or more important to him than this woman; she, lost in a provincial crowd, this unremarkable little woman with a vulgar lorgnette in her hands, now filled his entire life, was his grief, his joy, the only happiness he now wished for himself; and to the sounds of the wretched orchestra, the trashy townspeople's violins, he thought about how beautiful she was. He thought and dreamed.

     Together with Anna Sergeyevna, a young man came in and sat down beside her — tall, stooped, with small side-whiskers; at every step he nodded his head and seemed to be constantly bowing. This was probably the husband whom she had once called a lackey back in Yalta, in a moment of bitter feeling. And indeed, there was something lackey-like and meek about his tall figure, his side-whiskers, his small bald patch; he smiled sweetly, and in his buttonhole gleamed some academic badge, exactly like a lackey's number.

     During the first intermission the husband went out to smoke, and she remained in her seat. Gurov, who was also sitting in the stalls, went up to her and said in a trembling voice, forcing a smile:

     — Hello.

     She glanced at him and turned pale, then looked again in horror, not believing her eyes, and clutched her fan and lorgnette tightly together in her hands, clearly struggling with herself not to faint. Both were silent. She sat, he stood, frightened by her confusion, not daring to sit beside her. The violins and flute being tuned began to sing out, and suddenly it felt terrifying — it seemed as though everyone in all the boxes was watching. But then she rose and walked quickly toward the exit; he followed her, and they both walked aimlessly through corridors and up and down staircases, and before their eyes flashed people in the uniforms of judges, teachers, and civil servants, all wearing badges; ladies flashed by, fur coats on coat racks, a draft blew through carrying the smell of cigarette stubs. And Gurov, whose heart was pounding, thought:

     "Good Lord! What are all these people for, this orchestra…"

     And at that moment he suddenly remembered how that evening at the station, after seeing Anna Sergeyevna off, he had told himself that it was all over and they would never see each other again. But how far away the end still was!

     On a narrow, gloomy staircase with a sign reading "To the Upper Circle," she stopped.

     — How you frightened me! — she said, breathing heavily, still pale, still stunned. — Oh, how you frightened me! I'm barely alive. Why did you come? Why?

     — But understand, Anna, understand… — he said in a low voice, hurrying. — I beg you, understand…

     She looked at him with fear, with entreaty, with love, looked intently, so as to fix his features more firmly in her memory.

     — I suffer so! — she went on, not listening to him. — I have thought of nothing but you all this time, I have lived by thoughts of you. And I wanted to forget, to forget, but why, why did you come?

     Higher up, on the landing, two schoolboys were smoking and looking down, but Gurov did not care; he drew Anna Sergeyevna to him and began kissing her face, her cheeks, her hands.

     — What are you doing, what are you doing! — she said in horror, pushing him away. — We have both lost our minds. Leave today, leave right now… I implore you by all that is holy, I beg you… Someone is coming!

     Someone was coming up the staircase from below.

     — You must leave… — Anna Sergeyevna continued in a whisper. — Do you hear me, Dmitri Dmitrich? I will come to you in Moscow. I have never been happy, I am unhappy now, and I will never, never be happy, never! Don't make me suffer even more. I swear I will come to Moscow. But now let us part! My dear, kind, darling, let us part!

     She pressed his hand and began walking quickly down the stairs, glancing back at him all the while, and it was plain from her eyes that she truly was not happy. Gurov stood for a moment, listened, then, when all was quiet, found his coat and left the theater.

IV

     And Anna Sergeyevna began coming to see him in Moscow. Once every two or three months she would leave S. and tell her husband she was going to consult a professor about a female complaint — and her husband believed her, and did not believe her. Arriving in Moscow,she would stay at the Slavyansky Bazar and immediately send a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov would go to her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

     One day he was on his way to her like this on a winter morning (the messenger had come to him the evening before and not found him in). His daughter was walking with him; he wanted to see her to school, which was on the way. Heavy wet snow was falling.

     "It's three degrees above zero, and yet it's snowing," Gurov said to his daughter. "But that warmth is only on the surface of the earth — in the upper layers of the atmosphere the temperature is quite different."

     "Papa, why isn't there thunder in winter?"

     He explained that too. He talked and thought about the fact that he was on his way to a rendezvous and not a single living soul knew about it, and probably never would. He had two lives: one open, seen and known by everyone who needed to know — full of conventional truth and conventional deception, exactly like the lives of his friends and acquaintances; and another that ran in secret. And by some strange coincidence, perhaps accidental, everything that was important, interesting, and necessary to him, everything in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, everything that formed the very core of his life, happened in secret from others; while everything that was his lie, his shell in which he hid to conceal the truth — his job at the bank, his arguments at the club, his talk of "the inferior race," going with his wife to anniversary dinners — all of that was out in the open. He judged others by himself, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that every person's real, most interesting life passed under the cover of secrecy, as under the cover of night. Every individual existence is sustained by secrecy, and perhaps that is partly why civilized people are so anxiously concerned that personal secrets should be respected.

     After seeing his daughter to school, Gurov made his way to the Slavyansky Bazar. He took off his coat downstairs, went up, and knocked quietly at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, worn out from the journey and the waiting, had been expecting him since the evening before; she was pale, looked at him without smiling, and the moment he entered she pressed herself to his chest. Their kiss was long and lingering, as though they had not seen each other for two years.

     "Well, how are things there?" he asked. "What's new?"

     "Wait, I'll tell you in a moment… I can't."

     She could not speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed a handkerchief to her eyes.

     "Let her have a cry, and I'll sit down in the meantime," he thought, and sat down in an armchair.

     Then he rang and ordered tea; and then, while he was drinking it, she went on standing with her back to him, facing the window… She was crying from agitation, from the sorrowful awareness that their life had turned out so sadly; they only saw each other in secret, hiding from people like thieves! Was their life not ruined?

     "Come now, stop it!" he said.

     It was clear to him that this love of theirs would not end any time soon — there was no telling when it would end. Anna Sergeyevna was growing more and more attached to him, she adored him, and it would have been unthinkable to tell her that it all had to come to an end someday; she would not have believed it anyway.

     He went over to her and took her by the shoulders to comfort her, to joke with her, and at that moment he caught sight of himself in the mirror.

     His hair was already beginning to turn gray. And it struck him as strange that he had aged so much in these last years, had grown so much less handsome. The shoulders on which his hands rested were warm and trembling. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already close to the point of beginning to fade and wither, as his own life had. Why did she love him so? Women had always seen him as something other than what he was, and what they loved in him was not himself but a man their imagination had created, a man they had been eagerly searching for all their lives; and then, when they noticed their mistake, they loved him still. And not one of them had been happy with him. Time passed, he met women, became close to them, parted from them, but not once had he been in love; there had been everything else, but not love.

     And only now, when his hair had turned gray, had he fallen in love properly, truly — for the first time in his life.

     Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other,as very close, dear people, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had destined them for each other, and it was incomprehensible why he was married and she was married too; and it was as if they were two migratory birds, a male and a female, who had been caught and made to live in separate cages. They forgave each other what they were ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love had changed them both.

Before, in sad moments, he would calm himself with whatever reasoning came to mind, but now he had no time for reasoning — he felt a deep compassion, he wanted to be sincere, tender…

— Stop it, my dear, — he said. — You've had your cry — that's enough… Now let's talk, let's think of something.

Then they spent a long time deliberating, talking about how to free themselves from the necessity of hiding, deceiving, living in different cities, going so long without seeing each other. How to break free from these unbearable shackles?

— How? How? — he asked, clutching his head. — How?

And it seemed that just a little more — and a solution would be found, and then a new, wonderful life would begin; and it was clear to both of them that the end was still very, very far away, and that the most complex and difficult part was only just beginning.

The Black Monk

I

Andrei Vasilyich Kovrin, a master's graduate, was exhausted and had worn his nerves to a frazzle. He did not seek treatment, but in passing, over a bottle of wine, he mentioned it to a doctor friend, who advised him to spend the spring and summer in the country. It so happened that a long letter arrived from Tanya Pesotskaya, asking him to come to Borisovka and stay for a while. And he decided that he really did need to get away.

At first — this was in April — he went to his own estate, his ancestral Kovrinka, and lived there in solitude for three weeks; then, waiting for the roads to improve, he set off by horse to visit his former guardian and mentor Pesotsky, a horticulturist well known throughout Russia. From Kovrinka to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, was no more than seventy versts, and traveling along the soft spring road in a comfortable sprung carriage was a true pleasure.

The Pesotsky house was enormous, with columns, with lions whose plaster had peeled away, and a liveried footman at the entrance. The old park, gloomy and austere, laid out in the English style, stretched nearly a full verst from the house to the river, where it ended at a steep, abrupt clay bank on which grew pines with exposed roots resembling shaggy paws; below, the water gleamed inhospitably, sandpipers darted about with plaintive cries, and the mood of the place was always such that one felt like sitting down and writing a ballad. But near the house itself, in the courtyard and in the orchard, which together with the nurseries occupied some thirty acres, the atmosphere was cheerful and full of life even in bad weather. Such remarkable roses, lilies, camellias, tulips of every conceivable color from bright white to soot black — such a wealth of flowers as Pesotsky had, Kovrin had never seen anywhere else. Spring was only just beginning, and the true splendor of the flower beds was still hidden away in the greenhouses, but even what was blooming along the paths and here and there in the flower beds was enough, as one walked through the garden, to feel oneself in a kingdom of delicate colors, especially in the early hours, when every petal sparkled with dew.

What formed the ornamental part of the garden, which Pesotsky himself contemptuously called trifles, had once made a fairy-tale impression on Kovrin in childhood. What whimsical fancies, refined oddities, and mockeries of nature were to be found here! There were espaliers of fruit trees, a pear tree shaped like a Lombardy poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an apple tree canopy, arches, monograms, candelabras, and even 1862 spelled out in plum trees — the year when Pesotsky first took up horticulture. There were also beautiful, slender little trees with trunks as straight and sturdy as palm trees, and only by looking closely could one recognize them as gooseberry or currant bushes. But what delighted one most in the garden and gave it its lively appearance,so it is constant motion. From early morning until evening, around the trees, bushes, along the paths and flower beds, people with wheelbarrows, hoes, and watering cans swarmed like ants…

     Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys' in the evening, around ten o'clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semyonych, in a state of great anxiety. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer were forecasting frost by morning, yet the gardener Ivan Karlych had gone into town and there was no one to rely on. At supper they spoke of nothing but the morning frost, and it was decided that Tanya would not go to bed and would walk through the garden at around one o'clock to check that everything was in order, while Yegor Semyonych would get up at three, or even earlier.

     Kovrin sat with Tanya all evening and after midnight went with her into the garden. It was cold. The yard already smelled strongly of smoke. In the large orchard, which was called the commercial one and brought Yegor Semyonych several thousand in clear profit each year, a black, thick, acrid smoke spread along the ground, wrapping around the trees and saving those thousands from the frost. The trees stood in a checkerboard pattern, their rows straight and regular, like ranks of soldiers, and this strict, pedantic regularity, along with the fact that all the trees were the same height and had perfectly identical crowns and trunks, made the scene monotonous and even dull. Kovrin and Tanya walked along the rows where bonfires of manure, straw, and all kinds of refuse smoldered, and now and then they came across workers drifting through the smoke like shadows. Only the cherry trees, plum trees, and certain varieties of apple trees were in bloom, but the entire garden was drowned in smoke, and only near the nurseries did Kovrin breathe freely.

     – I used to sneeze from the smoke here as a child, – he said, shrugging his shoulders, – but I still don't understand how smoke can protect against frost.

     – Smoke takes the place of clouds when there are none… – Tanya replied.

     – And what are clouds needed for?

     – On overcast and cloudy nights there are no morning frosts.

     – Is that so!

     He laughed and took her by the hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with its thin black eyebrows, the raised collar of her coat that kept her from turning her head freely, and her whole self — slender, graceful, her dress gathered up away from the dew — moved him deeply.

     – Lord, she's already grown up! – he said. – When I left here for the last time, five years ago, you were still quite a child. You were so thin, long-legged, bareheaded, you wore a short little dress, and I used to tease you and call you a heron… What time does!

     – Yes, five years! – Tanya sighed. – A lot of water has flowed under the bridge since then. Tell me, Andryusha, honestly, – she began speaking quickly, looking him in the face, – have you grown apart from us? Though why do I even ask? You're a man, you already live your own interesting life, you're someone of importance… Estrangement is so natural! But whatever the case, Andryusha, I want you to think of us as your own. We have a right to that.

     – I do think of you that way, Tanya.

     – Word of honor?

     – Yes, word of honor.

     – You were surprised today that we have so many photographs of you. You know, my father adores you. Sometimes it seems to me that he loves you more than he loves me. He is proud of you. You are a scholar, an extraordinary person, you have made a brilliant career for yourself, and he is convinced that you turned out the way you did because he raised you. I don't stop him from thinking that. Let him.

     Dawn was already beginning, and this was especially noticeable in the sharpness with which the billows of smoke and the crowns of the trees had begun to stand out in the air. Nightingales were singing, and from the fields came the cry of quails.

"It's time to sleep," said Tanya. "And it's cold." She took his arm. "Thank you for coming, Andryusha. We have uninteresting acquaintances, and few of them at that. We have only the garden, the garden, the garden — and nothing else. Standard, half-standard," she laughed, "aport, ranet, borovinka, budding, grafting… Our whole, entire life has gone into the garden, I never even dream of anything except apple trees and pear trees. Of course, it's good, it's useful, but sometimes you want something else for a change. I remember when you used to come to us for the holidays or just like that, the house would become somehow fresher and brighter, as if the dust covers were being taken off the chandelier and the furniture. I was a little girl then and yet I understood."

She spoke for a long time and with great feeling. For some reason it suddenly occurred to him that over the course of the summer he might grow attached to this small, frail, talkative creature, become carried away and fall in love — in the situation they were both in, it was so possible and natural. The thought moved and amused him; he leaned toward her dear, anxious face and sang softly:

Onegin, I will not conceal it,

I am madly in love with Tatiana…

When they came home, Yegor Semyonych had already risen. Kovrin had no desire to sleep, he fell into conversation with the old man and went back with him to the garden. Yegor Semyonych was tall, broad-shouldered, with a large belly and suffered from shortness of breath, but always walked so quickly that it was hard to keep up with him. He had an extremely anxious look about him, was always rushing somewhere, with an expression as though if he were even a minute late, everything would be lost.

"Now here's a thing, brother…" he began, stopping to catch his breath. "On the surface of the ground, as you can see, there's frost, but raise a thermometer on a stick two fathoms above the ground and it's warm up there… Why is that?"

"I really don't know," said Kovrin and laughed.

"Hm… You can't know everything, of course… However vast the mind may be, you can't fit everything into it. You're mostly into philosophy, aren't you?"

"Yes. I read psychology, and I work in philosophy generally."

"And you don't get bored of it?"

"On the contrary, it's the only thing I live by."

"Well, God grant…" said Yegor Semyonych, thoughtfully stroking his grey sideburns. "God grant… I'm very glad for you… glad, brother…"

But suddenly he listened intently and, making a terrible face, ran off to one side and soon disappeared among the trees in clouds of smoke.

"Who tied a horse to an apple tree?" came his desperate, soul-rending cry. "What scoundrel and villain dared to tie a horse to an apple tree? My God, my God! They've ruined it, frozen it, defiled it, fouled it all up! The garden is done for! The garden is lost! My God!"

When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted and aggrieved.

"Well, what can you do with these accursed people?" he said in a tearful voice, throwing up his hands. "Stepka was hauling manure at night and tied the horse to an apple tree! The scoundrel wound the reins so tight that the bark was rubbed off in three places. How do you like that! I tell him, and he just stands there like a blockhead, blinking his eyes! Hanging's too good for him!"

Having calmed down, he embraced Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

"Well, God grant… God grant…" he muttered. "I'm very glad you came. Unspeakably glad… Thank you."

Then, with that same quick step and anxious face, he walked through the entire garden and showed his former ward all the greenhouses, hothouses, storage sheds, and his two apiaries, which he called the wonder of our century.

While they were walking, the sun rose and brightly lit up the garden. It grew warm. Sensing a clear, cheerful, long day ahead, Kovrin remembered that it was only the beginning of May and that the whole summer still lay ahead, just as clear, cheerful, and long, and suddenly a joyful, youthful feeling stirred in his chest, the kind he had felt in childhood when he used to run through this garden. And he himself embraced the old man and kissed him tenderly. Both of them, moved, went into the house and began drinking tea from old porcelain cups, with cream and rich, buttery pretzels — and these small things again reminded Kovrin of his childhood and youth. The beautiful present and the impressions of the past awakening within him merged together; his soul felt crowded with them, but it was good.

He waited until,when Tanya woke up, and had coffee with her, went for a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read attentively, made notes, and occasionally raised his eyes to glance at the open windows or at the fresh flowers, still wet with dew, standing in vases on the table, then lowered his eyes back to the book, and it seemed to him that every fiber in his body trembled and played with pleasure.

     

II

     

     In the country he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city. He read and wrote a great deal, studied Italian, and when he went for walks, he thought with pleasure about how he would soon sit down to work again. He slept so little that everyone was amazed; if he happened to doze off for half an hour during the day, he would not sleep at all that night, and after a sleepless night he felt cheerful and lively, as if nothing had happened.

     He talked a great deal, drank wine, and smoked expensive cigars. Young ladies from the neighborhood came to the Pesotskys' often, almost every day, and played the piano and sang with Tanya; sometimes a young man, a neighbor who played the violin well, would come by. Kovrin listened to the music and singing with avidity and was exhausted by it, and this exhaustion expressed itself physically in his eyes drooping and his head tilting to one side.

     One evening after tea he was sitting on the balcony reading. In the drawing room at that time Tanya — soprano, one of the young ladies — contralto, and the young man on the violin were rehearsing the well-known serenade by Braga. Kovrin listened to the words — they were in Russian — and could make no sense of them. At last, putting down his book and listening carefully, he understood: a girl, sick with imagination, heard some mysterious sounds in the garden at night, so beautiful and strange that she had to acknowledge them as a sacred harmony, incomprehensible to us mortals, and therefore flying back up to the heavens. Kovrin's eyes began to droop. He got up and walked wearily through the drawing room, then through the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya by the arm and went out with her onto the balcony.

     "I've been preoccupied all day with a certain legend," he said. "I can't remember whether I read it somewhere or heard it, but the legend is a strange one, quite unlike anything else. To begin with, it is not particularly clear. A thousand years ago some monk dressed in black was walking through the desert, somewhere in Syria or Arabia… Several miles from where he walked, fishermen saw another black monk moving slowly across the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend apparently does not recognize, and listen further. From the mirage came another mirage, then from that one a third, so that the image of the black monk began to be transmitted endlessly from one layer of the atmosphere to another. He was seen now in Africa, now in Spain, now in India, now in the Far North… At last he passed beyond the bounds of the earth's atmosphere and now wanders throughout the universe, never finding the conditions under which he might fade away. Perhaps he is seen somewhere on Mars now, or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the very heart of the legend lies in this: that exactly a thousand years after the monk walked through the desert, the mirage will enter the earth's atmosphere again and appear to people. And it seems that thousand years is nearly up… According to the legend, we should expect the black monk any day now.

     "A strange mirage," said Tanya, who did not like the legend.

     "But the most remarkable thing," laughed Kovrin, "is that I simply cannot remember where this legend came into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Hear it? Or perhaps the black monk appeared to me in a dream? I swear to God, I don't remember. But the legend fascinates me. I've been thinking about it all day."

     Letting Tanya go back to the guests, he went outside and walked pensively around the flower beds. The sun was already setting. The flowers, having just been watered, gave off a damp, pungent smell. In the house they began singing again, and from a distance the violin gave the impression of a human voice. Kovrin, straining his mind to remember where he had heard or read the legend, walked unhurriedly into the park and gradually made his way down to the river.He descended to the water along a path that ran down the steep bank past bare roots, startling some sandpipers and flushing two ducks. The last rays of the setting sun still glinted here and there on the gloomy pines, but on the surface of the river it was already true evening. Kovrin crossed to the other side on the footbridge. Before him now lay a wide field covered with young rye, not yet in bloom. No human dwelling, no living soul in the distance, and it seemed as though the path, if one followed it, would lead to that very unknown and mysterious place where the sun had just gone down and where the evening glow blazed so broadly and majestically.

     "How spacious, free, and quiet it is here!" thought Kovrin, walking along the path. "And it seems as though the whole world is watching me, hiding and waiting for me to understand it…"

     But then waves ran through the rye, and a light evening breeze touched his bare head tenderly. A minute later another gust of wind, stronger this time — the rye rustled, and from behind came the muffled murmur of the pines. Kovrin stopped in amazement. On the horizon, like a whirlwind or a waterspout, a tall black column rose from the earth to the sky. Its outlines were indistinct, but in the very first instant one could tell that it was not standing still but moving with terrifying speed, moving this way, straight toward Kovrin, and the closer it came, the smaller and clearer it became. Kovrin rushed aside into the rye to get out of its way, and barely managed to do so…

     A monk in black robes, with a gray head and black eyebrows, his arms folded across his chest, swept past… His bare feet did not touch the ground. Having already flown some three fathoms past, he glanced back at Kovrin, nodded his head, and smiled at him warmly and at the same time slyly. But what a pale, terribly pale, gaunt face! Growing large again, he flew across the river, struck the clay bank and the pines without a sound, passed through them, and vanished like smoke.

     "Well, there you have it…" muttered Kovrin. "So the legend is true."

     Making no attempt to explain the strange phenomenon to himself, content simply that he had managed to see not only the black robes but even the face and eyes of the monk so closely and so clearly, he returned home in a state of pleasant agitation.

     People were strolling quietly through the park and garden, and music was being played in the house — which meant he alone had seen the monk. He very much wanted to tell Tanya and Yegor Semyonych everything, but he realized they would surely take his words for delirium, and that would frighten them; better to say nothing. He laughed loudly, sang, danced the mazurka, and was in high spirits, and everyone — the guests and Tanya — felt that his face today had something special about it, radiant and inspired, and that he was very interesting.

III

After supper, when the guests had left, he went to his room and lay down on the sofa: he wanted to think about the monk. But a minute later Tanya came in.

     "Here, Andryusha, read father's articles," she said, handing him a bundle of pamphlets and reprints. "They're wonderful articles. He writes beautifully."

     "Oh, beautifully indeed!" said Yegor Semyonych, coming in after her and laughing awkwardly; he felt embarrassed. "Please don't listen to her, don't read them! Although if you want to fall asleep, go ahead — an excellent sleeping remedy."

     "In my opinion they are magnificent articles," said Tanya with deep conviction. "Do read them, Andryusha, and persuade papa to write more often. He could write a complete course on horticulture."

     Yegor Semyonych laughed tensely, turned red, and began saying the sort of things that embarrassed authors usually say. At last he began to give in.

     "In that case read Gaucher's article first, and these Russian pieces," he muttered, leafing through the pamphlets with trembling hands, "otherwise you won't understand. Before reading my objections you need to know what I'm objecting to. Though it's all nonsense… deadly dull. And it's time to sleep, I think."

     Tanya left. Yegor Semyonych sat down beside Kovrin on the sofa and sighed deeply.

     "Yes, my friend…" he began after a brief silence. "That's how it is, my dearest magister. Here I am writing articles, taking part in exhibitions, receiving medals… Pesotsky's apples, they say, are the size of a head, and Pesotsky, they say, has made his fortune from his garden."In a word, Kochubey is rich and renowned. But the question is: what is the point of it all? The garden is truly magnificent, exemplary… It is not merely a garden but an entire institution of great national importance, a stepping stone, so to speak, into a new era of Russian agriculture and Russian industry. But what for? What is the purpose?

     – The work speaks for itself.

     – That is not what I mean. I want to ask: what will become of the garden when I am gone? In the state you see it now, it will not last a single month without me. The whole secret of its success lies not in the size of the garden or the number of workers, but in the fact that I love what I do – do you understand? – I love it, perhaps more than I love myself. Just look at me: I do everything myself. I work from morning till night. I do all the grafting myself, the pruning myself, the planting myself, everything myself. When people help me, I grow jealous and irritable to the point of rudeness. The whole secret lies in love, that is, in the keen eye of a true master, in the hands of a true master, and in that feeling when you go off somewhere for an hour as a guest, you sit there, but your heart is not at ease, you are not yourself: you are afraid something might go wrong in the garden. And when I die, who will look after it? Who will do the work? The gardener? The laborers? Is that it? Well, let me tell you this, my dear friend: the first enemy in our business is not the hare, not the cockchafer, and not the frost, but the outsider.

     – What about Tanya? – asked Kovrin, laughing. – Surely she cannot be more harmful than a hare. She loves and understands the work.

     – Yes, she loves and understands it. If after my death the garden passes to her and she becomes its mistress, then of course one could not wish for anything better. But what if, God forbid, she gets married? – Yegor Semyonych whispered, glancing at Kovrin with a frightened look. – There you have it! She gets married, children come along, and then there is no time to think about the garden.

     – What I fear most of all is this: she marries some fine fellow, and he gets greedy and leases the garden out to market women, and the whole thing goes to the devil in the very first year! In our business, women are the scourge of God!

     Yegor Semyonych sighed and was silent for a moment.

     – Perhaps this is selfish, but I will say it plainly: I do not want Tanya to get married. I am afraid! There is some dandy who comes around here with a violin and scrapes away at it; I know Tanya would never marry him, I know it well, but I cannot stand the sight of him! In general, brother, I am quite an eccentric. I admit it.

     Yegor Semyonych stood up and paced the room in agitation, and it was clear that he wanted to say something very important but could not bring himself to do so.

     – I am deeply fond of you and will speak to you frankly, – he finally resolved, thrusting his hands into his pockets. – On certain delicate matters I take a straightforward approach and say exactly what I think, and I cannot abide what people call hidden thoughts. I will say it plainly: you are the only man to whom I would not be afraid to give my daughter. You are intelligent, warm-hearted, and you would not let my beloved work perish. And the main reason is that I love you like a son… and I am proud of you. If something were to develop between you and Tanya, well – I would be very glad, even happy. I say this plainly, without affectation, as an honest man.

     Kovrin laughed. Yegor Semyonych opened the door to leave and stopped on the threshold.

     – If you and Tanya were to have a son, I would make a horticulturist out of him, – he said after a moment's thought. – Though that is an idle dream… Good night.

     Left alone, Kovrin settled himself more comfortably and set about reading the articles. One was titled "On Intercropping", another "A Few Words on Mr. Z.'s Note Regarding the Double-Digging of Soil for a New Garden", a third "More on Budding with a Dormant Eye" – and so on in the same vein. But what a restless, uneven tone, what a nervous, almost morbid combativeness! Here is an article with what seems the most peaceable title and unremarkable content: it concerns the Russian Antonovka apple. Yet Yegor Semyonych opens it with "audiatur altera pars" 1 and closes with "sapienti sat" 2, and between these maxims there is a whole fountain of venomous words directed at "the learned ignorance of our certified gentlemen horticulturists, who observe nature from the heights of their lecterns", or at Mr. Gaucher, "whose success was created by laymen and dilettantes", alongside an ill-timed and insincere expression of regret,that men who steal fruit and break trees in the process can no longer be flogged with birch rods.

     "It's a fine, lovely, wholesome business, yet here too there are passions and war," thought Kovrin. "People with ideals must be nervous and highly sensitive in every field and walk of life. Presumably that's how it has to be."

     He thought of Tanya, who liked Yegor Semyonych's articles so much. Small in stature, pale, thin, with her collarbones showing; wide-open, dark, intelligent eyes that were always peering somewhere and searching for something; a gait like her father's, quick and hurried. She talked a great deal, loved to argue, and accompanied even the most insignificant remark with expressive gestures and facial expressions. She must be highly strung in the extreme.

     Kovrin went on reading but understood nothing and gave up. The pleasant excitement with which he had danced the mazurka and listened to the music earlier now weighed on him and stirred up a multitude of thoughts. He got up and began pacing the room, thinking about the black monk. It occurred to him that if he alone had seen this strange, supernatural monk, then he must be ill and had already reached the point of hallucinations. This thought frightened him, but not for long.

     "But I feel well, and I am doing no harm to anyone; so there is nothing bad in my hallucinations," he thought, and he felt well again.

     He sat down on the sofa and clasped his head in his hands, holding back the inexplicable joy that filled his entire being, then paced the room again and sat down to work. But the thoughts he was drawing from the book did not satisfy him. He wanted something gigantic, boundless, staggering. Toward morning he undressed and reluctantly got into bed: he had to sleep, after all.

     When he heard the footsteps of Yegor Semyonych going out to the garden, Kovrin rang and ordered the servant to bring wine. He drank several glasses of Lafite with pleasure, then pulled the covers over his head; his consciousness grew hazy, and he fell asleep.

IV

     Yegor Semyonych and Tanya often quarreled and said unpleasant things to each other.

     One morning they had a falling-out over something. Tanya burst into tears and went to her room. She did not come out for dinner or tea. Yegor Semyonych at first walked around looking important and puffed up, as if wishing to make it clear that for him the interests of justice and order came above everything in the world, but he soon lost his resolve and his spirits sank. He wandered sadly through the park, sighing constantly: "Oh, Lord, Lord!" and at dinner he did not eat a single crumb. At last, feeling guilty and tormented by his conscience, he knocked on the locked door and called out timidly:

     "Tanya! Tanya?"

     And in reply there came from behind the door a faint voice, exhausted from weeping yet at the same time resolute:

     "Leave me alone, I beg you."

     The distress of the hosts was reflected throughout the entire house, even among the people working in the garden. Kovrin was absorbed in his interesting work, but in the end he too began to feel bored and ill at ease. To somehow dispel the general low spirits, he decided to intervene and toward evening knocked on Tanya's door. He was let in.

     "Oh, oh, how shameful!" he began playfully, looking with surprise at Tanya's tear-stained face, blotched with red and full of grief. "Is it really so serious? Oh, oh!"

     "But if you only knew how he torments me!" she said, and tears, burning, abundant tears, gushed from her large eyes. "He has worn me out!" she went on, wringing her hands. "I said nothing to him… nothing… I only said there was no need to keep… extra workers, if… if one could, whenever one liked, hire day laborers. After all… after all, the workers have done nothing for a whole week already… I… I only said that, and he started shouting and said… many hurtful, deeply offensive things to me. What for?"

     "Come now, come now," said Kovrin, tidying her hair. "You quarreled, you cried, and that's enough. You can't stay angry for long, it's not good… especially since he loves you endlessly."

     "He has… has ruined my whole life," Tanya went on, sobbing. "All I ever hear are insults and… and grievances. He considers me a burden in his house. Well then? He is right. I'll leave tomorrow, I'll become a telegraph operator… Let him…"

     "Now, now, now… Don't cry, Tanya. Don't,"милая… You're both hot-headed, that's all

Kovrin spoke gently and persuasively, while she kept crying, her shoulders trembling and her hands clasped together, as though some terrible misfortune had truly befallen her. He felt all the more sorry for her because her grief was not serious, yet she suffered deeply. How little it took to make this creature miserable for an entire day, and perhaps for her whole life! As he comforted Tanya, Kovrin thought about how, apart from this girl and her father, you could search the whole world and not find people who loved him as one of their own, as family; if not for these two people, having lost his father and mother in early childhood, he might never have known until his dying day what sincere affection was, or that naive, unquestioning love that one feels only for those who are very close, bound by blood. And he felt that his half-sick, frayed nerves responded to the nerves of this weeping, trembling girl the way iron responds to a magnet. He could never have fallen in love with a healthy, robust, rosy-cheeked woman, but pale, frail, unhappy Tanya appealed to him.

     And he willingly stroked her hair and shoulders, squeezed her hands, and wiped away her tears… At last she stopped crying. She went on for a long time complaining about her father and her hard, unbearable life in this house, imploring Kovrin to understand her situation; then she gradually began to smile and sigh that God had given her such a bad temper, and finally burst out laughing, called herself a fool, and ran out of the room.

     When Kovrin came out into the garden a little while later, Yegor Semyonych and Tanya were already strolling side by side along the path as if nothing had happened, and both were eating rye bread with salt, for both were hungry.

V

     Pleased that he had played the role of peacemaker so successfully, Kovrin went into the park. Sitting on a bench lost in thought, he heard the clatter of carriages and women's laughter — guests had arrived. As the evening shadows began to fall across the garden, the sounds of a violin and singing voices drifted faintly to him, and this reminded him of the black monk. In what land, or on what planet, was this optical absurdity now wandering?

     He had barely recalled the legend and conjured in his imagination that dark apparition he had seen in the rye field, when from behind a pine tree directly opposite, a man of medium height emerged silently, without the slightest rustle — bareheaded, with gray hair, dressed all in dark clothing and barefoot, resembling a beggar — and on his pale, death-like face his black eyebrows stood out sharply. Nodding his head in a friendly manner, this beggar or wanderer approached the bench without a sound and sat down, and Kovrin recognized in him the black monk. For a moment they looked at each other — Kovrin with astonishment, and the monk with warmth and, as before, a slight cunning, with a knowing expression.

     – But you are a mirage, – said Kovrin. – Why then are you here, sitting in one place? That doesn't fit the legend.

     – It makes no difference, – the monk replied after a pause, in a quiet voice, turning his face toward him. – The legend, the mirage, and I — all of this is a product of your excited imagination. I am a phantom.

     – So you don't exist? – asked Kovrin.

     – Think as you wish, – said the monk, smiling faintly. – I exist in your imagination, and your imagination is a part of nature, which means I exist in nature as well.

     – You have a very old, wise, and supremely expressive face, as though you have truly lived more than a thousand years, – said Kovrin. – I did not know my imagination was capable of creating such phenomena. But why do you look at me with such rapture? Do you like me?

     – Yes. You are one of the few who are rightly called God's chosen. You serve the eternal truth. Your thoughts, your intentions, your remarkable scholarship, and your entire life bear a divine, heavenly mark, for they are devoted to the rational and the beautiful — that is, to what is eternal.

     – You said: eternal truth… But is eternal truth accessible to people, or even necessary, if there is no eternal life?

     – Eternal life exists, – said the monk.

     – You believe in the immortality of people?

     – Yes, of course. A great and brilliant future awaits you, humankind. And the more people like you there are on earth, the sooner that future will come to pass. Without you,servants of a higher principle, living consciously and freely, humanity would be insignificant; developing in its natural course, it would wait much longer for the end of its earthly history. But you will lead it into the kingdom of eternal truth several thousand years sooner — and that is your great merit. You embody the divine blessing that has come to rest upon mankind.

     "And what is the purpose of eternal life?" asked Kovrin.

     "The same as any life — enjoyment. True enjoyment lies in knowledge, and eternal life will offer countless and inexhaustible sources of knowledge, and in that sense it is written: In my Father's house are many mansions.

     "If only you knew how pleasant it is to listen to you!" said Kovrin, rubbing his hands with pleasure.

     "I am very glad."

     "But I know: when you are gone, I will be troubled by the question of your nature. You are a phantom, a hallucination. Does that mean I am mentally ill, abnormal?"

     "Even if that is so, what is there to be troubled about? You are ill because you worked beyond your strength and grew exhausted, which means you have sacrificed your health to an idea, and the time is near when you will give your very life to it as well. What could be better? That is what all noble natures gifted from above strive for."

     "If I know that I am mentally ill, can I trust myself?"

     "And how do you know that the men of genius whom the whole world trusts did not also see phantoms? Scientists say now that genius is akin to madness. My friend, only ordinary, herd-like people are healthy and normal. Concerns about a nervous age, overexertion, degeneration, and so on can only seriously trouble those who see the purpose of life in the present — that is, the herd people."

     "The Romans said: mens sana in corpore sano."

     "Not everything the Romans or Greeks said is true. Elevated mood, excitement, ecstasy — all that distinguishes prophets, poets, and martyrs for an idea from ordinary people — is contrary to the animal side of man, that is, to his physical health. I repeat: if you want to be healthy and normal, go join the herd."

     "Strange, you are repeating what often comes to my own mind," said Kovrin. "It is as though you have spied upon and overheard my most secret thoughts. But let us not talk about me. What do you mean by eternal truth?"

     The monk did not answer. Kovrin looked at him and could not make out his face: its features grew hazy and blurred. Then the monk's head and hands began to disappear; his body merged with the bench and the evening dusk, and he vanished altogether.

     "The hallucination is over!" said Kovrin, and laughed. "What a pity."

     He walked back to the house cheerful and happy. The little that the black monk had said to him flattered not his vanity but his whole soul, his entire being. To be one of the chosen, to serve eternal truth, to stand among those who would make humanity worthy of the kingdom of God several thousand years sooner — that is, to spare people several extra thousand years of struggle, sin, and suffering — to give everything to an idea: youth, strength, health, to be ready to die for the common good — what a lofty, what a happy destiny! His past flashed through his memory, pure, chaste, full of labor; he recalled what he had learned and what he had taught others, and decided that there had been no exaggeration in the monk's words.

     Tanya was coming toward him through the park. She was wearing a different dress now.

     "Is that you?" she said. "We have been looking and looking for you… But what is the matter with you?" she wondered, glancing at his rapturous, radiant face and his eyes full of tears. "How strange you are, Andryusha."

     "I am content, Tanya," said Kovrin, placing his hands on her shoulders. "I am more than content, I am happy! Tanya, dear Tanya, you are an extraordinarily lovely creature. Dear Tanya, I am so glad, so glad!"

     He warmly kissed both her hands and went on:

     "I have just lived through bright, wonderful, unearthly moments. But I cannot tell you everything, because you would call me mad or would not believe me. Let us talk about you. Dear, darling Tanya! I love you and have grown accustomed to loving you. Your nearness, our meetings ten times a day, have become a need of my soul. I do not know,"how I will manage without you when I leave for home.

     – Well! – Tanya laughed. – You will forget about us in two days. We are small people, and you are a great man.

     – No, let us speak seriously! – he said. – I will take you with me, Tanya. Yes? Will you come with me? Do you want to be mine?

     – Well! – said Tanya and wanted to laugh again, but the laugh did not come, and red blotches appeared on her face.

     She began to breathe rapidly and walked quickly,quickly away, but not toward the house, farther into the park.

     – I never thought about this… never thought! – she said, as if wringing her hands in despair.

     And Kovrin followed her, speaking with the same radiant, rapturous expression:

     – I want a love that would consume me entirely, and only you, Tanya, can give me that love. I am happy! Happy!

     She was overwhelmed, hunched over, shrank into herself and seemed to age ten years at once, while he found her beautiful and loudly expressed his delight:

     – How lovely she is!

     

VI

     

     Upon learning from Kovrin that not only had a romance taken shape but that there would even be a wedding, Yegor Semyonych paced from corner to corner for a long time, trying to conceal his agitation. His hands began to tremble, his neck swelled and turned crimson, he ordered the racing droshky to be harnessed and drove off somewhere. Tanya, who had seen how he lashed the horse and how deeply he had pulled his cap down almost over his ears, understood his mood, locked herself in her room and wept all day.

     In the greenhouses the peaches and plums were already ripe; packing and shipping this delicate and finicky cargo to Moscow required a great deal of attention, labor, and effort. Because the summer had been very hot and dry, every tree had to be watered, which took up much time and manpower, and caterpillars appeared in great numbers, which the workers and even Yegor Semyonych and Tanya, to Kovrin's great disgust, crushed with their bare fingers. On top of all that, orders for autumn fruit and trees had to be taken and a large correspondence maintained. And at the very busiest time, when it seemed no one had a free minute, the field work began, drawing away more than half the garden's laborers; Yegor Semyonych, deeply tanned, worn out, and irritable, galloped back and forth between the garden and the fields, shouting that he was being torn to pieces and that he would put a bullet in his head.

     And then there was the fuss over the trousseau, to which the Pesotskys attached no small importance; the clinking of scissors, the clatter of sewing machines, the fumes of irons, and the whims of the dressmaker, a nervous and touchy woman, made everyone in the house dizzy. And as if on purpose, guests arrived every day who had to be entertained, fed, and even put up for the night. But all this ordeal passed unnoticed, as if in a fog. Tanya felt as though love and happiness had caught her off guard, even though since the age of fourteen she had been somehow certain that Kovrin would marry her and no one else. She was astonished, bewildered, could not believe herself… Then suddenly such joy would wash over her that she wanted to soar up into the clouds and pray to God there, and then she would suddenly remember that in August she would have to leave her home and her father, or, from out of nowhere, the thought would come that she was insignificant, petty, and unworthy of such a great man as Kovrin, and she would go to her room, lock the door, and weep bitterly for hours. When there were guests, she would suddenly feel that Kovrin was extraordinarily handsome and that all the women were in love with him and envied her, and her soul would fill with rapture and pride, as though she had conquered the whole world, but the moment he smiled warmly at some young lady, she would tremble with jealousy, retreat to her room, and again there were tears. These new sensations took complete hold of her, she helped her father mechanically and noticed neither the peaches nor the caterpillars nor the workers nor how swiftly time was passing.

     Much the same was happening with Yegor Semyonych. He worked from morning till night, was always rushing somewhere, losing his temper, getting irritated, but all of it in some kind of magical half-dream. It was as though two men now lived inside him: one was the real Yegor Semyonych, who, listening to the gardener Ivan Karlych report on the disorder, grew indignant and clutched his head in despair, and the other,not real, as if half-drunk, who would suddenly break off mid-sentence in the middle of a business conversation, touch the gardener on the shoulder and start muttering:

     – Say what you will, but blood means a great deal. His mother was a remarkable woman, the most noble, the most intelligent. It was a joy to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel's. She painted beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang… Poor thing, God rest her soul, she died of consumption.

     The not-real Yegor Semyonych would sigh and, after a pause, continue:

     – When he was a boy growing up with me, he had that same angelic face, clear and kind. His gaze, his movements, his manner of speaking are as tender and graceful as his mother's. And his mind! He always astonished us with his mind. And no wonder he's a master's graduate! No wonder! But just wait, Ivan Karlych, what he'll be in ten years' time. You won't be able to reach him!

     But then the real Yegor Semyonych, catching himself, would make a terrible face, clutch his head and cry out:

     – Devils! They've ruined it, fouled it, frozen it all! The garden is done for! The garden is lost!

     And Kovrin worked with his usual diligence and noticed none of the commotion. Love had only added fuel to the fire. After each meeting with Tanya, happy and elated, he would go to his room and with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and declared his love for her, he would take up a book or his manuscript. What the black monk had said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, the brilliant future of mankind and so on, lent his work a special, extraordinary significance and filled his soul with pride and a sense of his own greatness. Once or twice a week, in the park or in the house, he would meet the black monk and talk with him at length, but this did not frighten him — on the contrary, it delighted him, for he was already firmly convinced that such visions visited only the chosen, outstanding people who had devoted themselves to the service of an idea.

     One day the monk appeared during dinner and sat down by the window in the dining room. Kovrin was glad and very deftly steered the conversation with Yegor Semyonych and Tanya toward topics that might interest the monk; the dark guest listened and nodded amiably, while Yegor Semyonych and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, not suspecting that Kovrin was speaking not to them but to his hallucination.

     The Dormition fast crept up unnoticed, and soon after came the wedding day, which, at Yegor Semyonych's insistent wish, was celebrated "with a bang" — that is, with a senseless revelry that went on for two days. They ate and drank to the tune of some three thousand rubles, but between the wretched hired musicians, the loud toasts, the scurrying waiters, the noise and the crush, no one could appreciate the taste of the fine wines or the remarkable hors d'oeuvres ordered from Moscow.

     

VII

     

     One night during the long winter nights, Kovrin lay in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, whose head ached in the evenings from the unaccustomed city life, had long since fallen asleep and occasionally murmured some incoherent phrases in her delirium.

     Three o'clock struck. Kovrin put out the candle and lay down; he lay for a long time with his eyes closed but could not fall asleep, it seemed to him, because the bedroom was very hot and Tanya was delirious. At half past four he lit the candle again and at that moment saw the black monk sitting in an armchair beside the bed.

     – Hello, said the monk and, after a brief silence, asked: – What are you thinking about now?

     – About glory, answered Kovrin. – The French novel I was just reading portrays a young scholar who does foolish things and wastes away from a longing for fame. I can't understand that longing.

     – Because you are intelligent. You regard fame with indifference, as a toy that holds no interest for you.

     – Yes, that's true.

     – Renown holds no appeal for you. What is flattering, or amusing, or instructive about having your name carved on a gravestone, only for time to wear away the inscription along with the gilding? And besides, fortunately, there are far too many of you for weak human memory to retain your names.

     – Of course, Kovrin agreed. – And why remember them anyway? But let's talk about something else. About happiness, for instance. What is happiness?When the clock struck five, he was sitting on the bed with his feet hanging over the carpet, speaking to the monk:

     "In ancient times, a fortunate man was eventually frightened by his own happiness — so great was it! — and, to appease the gods, he sacrificed his beloved ring to them. You know the story? And I, like Polycrates, am beginning to feel a little uneasy about my own happiness. It seems strange to me that from morning to night I experience nothing but joy — it fills me entirely and drowns out all other feelings. I don't know what sadness, sorrow, or boredom are. Here I am, not sleeping, suffering from insomnia, and yet I'm not bored. I say this seriously: I'm beginning to be puzzled."

     "But why?" the monk asked in astonishment. "Is joy a supernatural feeling? Should it not be the normal state of a person? The higher a person is in intellectual and moral development, the freer they are, the more pleasure life brings them. Socrates, Diogenes, and Marcus Aurelius experienced joy, not sorrow. And the apostle says: rejoice always. So rejoice and be happy."

     "But what if the gods grow angry?" Kovrin joked and laughed. "If they take away my comfort and make me shiver and go hungry, I doubt that would suit me."

     Tanya had meanwhile woken up and was staring at her husband with amazement and horror. He was speaking to the armchair, gesturing and laughing: his eyes were gleaming and there was something strange in his laughter.

     "Andryusha, who are you talking to?" she asked, grabbing his hand, which he had stretched out toward the monk. "Andryusha! Who?"

     "Hm? Who? " Kovrin was flustered. "Why, him… He's sitting right there," he said, pointing to the black monk.

     "There's no one here… no one! Andryusha, you're ill!"

     Tanya embraced her husband and pressed herself against him, as if shielding him from the visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

     "You're ill!" she sobbed, trembling all over. "Forgive me, my dear, my darling, but I noticed long ago that something has been troubling your soul… You are mentally ill, Andryusha…"

     Her trembling passed to him as well. He glanced once more at the armchair, which was now empty, suddenly felt a weakness in his arms and legs, grew frightened, and began to dress.

     "It's nothing, Tanya, nothing…" he muttered, trembling. "I really am a little unwell… it's time to admit it."

     "I noticed it long ago… and Papa noticed it too," she said, trying to hold back her sobs. "You talk to yourself, you smile in a strange way… you don't sleep. Oh, my God, my God, save us!" she cried out in horror. "But don't be afraid, Andryusha, don't be afraid, for God's sake, don't be afraid…"

     She too began to dress. Only now, looking at her, did Kovrin grasp the full danger of his situation and understand what the black monk and his conversations with him meant. It was clear to him now that he was mad.

     Both of them, not knowing why, got dressed and went to the drawing room — she first, he behind her. There, awakened by the sobbing, stood Yegor Semyonych in his dressing gown with a candle in his hands; he had been staying with them.

     "Don't be afraid, Andryusha," Tanya said, trembling as if with fever, "don't be afraid… Papa, it will all pass… it will all pass…"

     Kovrin was too agitated to speak. He wanted to say to his father-in-law in a joking tone:

     "Congratulations — I seem to have gone mad," but he only moved his lips and smiled bitterly.

     At nine o'clock in the morning they put a coat and fur coat on him, wrapped him in a shawl, and drove him in a carriage to the doctor. He began treatment.

VIII

Summer came again, and the doctor ordered him to go to the country. Kovrin had already recovered, had stopped seeing the black monk, and all that remained was to restore his physical strength. Living at his father-in-law's estate in the country, he drank a lot of milk, worked only two hours a day, neither drank wine nor smoked.

On the eve of Elijah's Day, an all-night vigil was held in the house. When the deacon handed the priest the censer, the old, enormous hall filled with a smell like a cemetery, and Kovrin grew bored. He went out into the garden. Paying no attention to the luxuriant flowers, he wandered through the garden, sat on a bench, then strolled through the park; reaching the river, he went down to the bank and stood there lost in thought, gazing at the water. The sullen pines with their shaggy roots, which the year before had seen him here so young, joyful, and full of life, were no longer whispering now — they stood motionless and silent, as though they did not recognize him. And indeed, his head was close-cropped, his long beautiful hair was gone, his gait was listless, and his face, compared to the previous summer, had grown fuller and paler.

     He crossed to the other bank by the footbridge. Where rye had grown the year before, mown oats now lay in rows. The sun had already set, and on the horizon a wide red glow blazed, foretelling windy weather for the next day. It was quiet. Peering in the direction where the black monk had first appeared the year before, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes, until the evening glow began to fade…

     When he returned home, listless and unsatisfied, the vigil was already over. Yegor Semyonych and Tanya were sitting on the terrace steps, drinking tea. They had been talking about something, but upon seeing Kovrin they suddenly fell silent, and from their faces he concluded that their conversation had been about him.

     "It seems it's time for you to drink your milk," Tanya said to her husband.

     "No, it's not time yet…" he answered, sitting down on the lowest step. "Drink yours. I don't want any."

     Tanya exchanged an anxious glance with her father and said in a guilty voice:

     "You know yourself that milk is good for you."

     "Yes, very good!" Kovrin smirked. "Congratulations: since Friday I've put on another pound. " He pressed his head hard between his hands and said with anguish: "Why, why did you treat me? Bromide preparations, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear over every sip, every step — all of this will eventually reduce me to idiocy. I was going mad, I had delusions of grandeur, but at least I was cheerful, full of life, and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I've become more sensible and solid, but I'm just like everyone else: I'm mediocre, life bores me… Oh, how cruelly you have treated me! I had hallucinations, but who did that harm? I ask: who did it harm?"

     "God knows what you're saying!" Yegor Semyonych sighed. "It's even tedious to listen."

     "Then don't listen."

     The presence of people, especially Yegor Semyonych, now irritated Kovrin; he answered him curtly, coldly, and even rudely, and looked at him only with mockery and hatred, while Yegor Semyonych grew flustered and guiltily cleared his throat, though he felt no guilt in himself. Unable to understand why their warm, good-natured relationship had changed so sharply, Tanya pressed close to her father and anxiously searched his eyes; she wanted to understand and could not, and all that was clear to her was that things were getting worse with each passing day, that her father had aged greatly of late, and that her husband had become irritable, capricious, fault-finding, and dull. She could no longer laugh or sing, she ate nothing at dinner, lay awake whole nights waiting for something terrible, and had worn herself out so completely that one day she lay in a faint from dinnertime until evening. During the vigil it had seemed to her that her father was crying, and now, as the three of them sat on the terrace, she was forcing herself not to think about it.

     "How fortunate were Buddha and Mohammed, or Shakespeare, that their kind relatives and doctors did not treat them for ecstasy and inspiration!" said Kovrin. "If Mohammed had taken potassium bromide for his nerves, worked only two hours a day, and drunk milk, then as little would have remained of that remarkable man as of his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will in the end bring it about that humanity grows dull, mediocrity will be taken for genius, and civilization will perish. If only you knew," said Kovrin with vexation, "how grateful I am to you!"

     He felt a surge of intense irritation and, so as not to say something he would regret, quickly rose and went into the house. It was quiet,and through the open windows the scent of tobacco and jalap drifted in from the garden. In the vast dark hall, moonlight lay in green patches on the floor and on the grand piano. Kovrin recalled the raptures of the previous summer, when jalap had smelled just the same and the moon had shone through the windows. To recapture last year's mood, he went quickly to his study, lit a strong cigar, and told the footman to bring wine. But the cigar left a bitter, unpleasant taste in his mouth, and the wine was not the same as it had been the year before. That's what losing the habit does! The cigar and two sips of wine made his head spin and his heart pound, so that he had to take potassium bromide.

Before going to bed, Tanya said to him:

"Father adores you. You're angry with him about something, and it's killing him. Look at him: he's aging not by the day but by the hour. I beg you, Andryusha, for God's sake, for the sake of your late father, for the sake of my peace of mind, be kind to him!"

"I can't and I don't want to."

"But why?" asked Tanya, beginning to tremble all over. "Explain it to me, why?"

"Because I don't like him, that's all," Kovrin said carelessly, shrugging his shoulders, "but let's not talk about him: he's your father."

"I can't, I can't understand it!" she said, pressing her temples and staring at one point. "Something incomprehensible, something terrible is happening in our house. You've changed, you've become unlike yourself… You, an intelligent, extraordinary man, getting irritated over trifles, getting mixed up in petty squabbles… Such little things upset you that sometimes one is simply astonished and can't believe it: is this really you? Now, now, don't be angry, don't be angry," she went on, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. "You are intelligent, kind, noble. You will be fair to father. He is so kind!"

"He's not kind, he's good-natured. Vaudeville uncles like your father, with their well-fed, good-natured faces, extraordinarily hospitable and eccentric, used to move me and amuse me once — in stories, in vaudeville, and in life — but now they repel me. They are egoists to the marrow. What repels me most is their well-fed contentment and that stomach-deep, purely bovine or porcine optimism."

Tanya sat down on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

"This is torture," she said, and from her voice it was clear that she was already utterly exhausted and that it was hard for her to speak. "Not a single peaceful moment since winter… It's terrible, my God! I am suffering…"

"Yes, of course, I am Herod, and you and your papa are the Egyptian infants. Of course!"

His face seemed to Tanya ugly and unpleasant. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. And even before she had noticed that something was already missing from his face, as though ever since he had cut his hair his face had changed too. She wanted to say something hurtful to him, but immediately caught herself feeling hostile, grew frightened, and left the bedroom.

IX

Kovrin received his own department chair. The inaugural lecture was scheduled for the second of December and a notice to that effect had been posted in the university corridor. But on the appointed day he notified the student inspector by telegram that he would not be giving lectures due to illness.

He was coughing up blood. He spat blood, but it happened two or three times a month that it flowed abundantly, and then he became extremely weak and fell into a drowsy state. This illness did not particularly frighten him, since he knew that his late mother had lived with exactly the same illness for ten years, even longer; and the doctors assured him it was not dangerous, advising him only to avoid agitation, lead a regular life, and talk as little as possible.

In January the lecture again did not take place for the same reason, and by February it was already too late to begin the course. It had to be postponed until the following year.

He was no longer living with Tanya, but with another woman who was two years older than him and looked after him like a child. His mood was peaceful, submissive; he willingly deferred to others, and when Varvara Nikolaevna — that was his companion's name — decided to take him to Crimea, he agreed, though he had a premonition that nothing good would come of the trip.

They arrived in Sevastopol in the evening and checked into a hotel,to rest and drive to Yalta the next day. The journey had worn them both out. Varvara Nikolaevna drank her tea, went to bed, and fell asleep quickly. But Kovrin did not lie down. Back at home, an hour before leaving for the station, he had received a letter from Tanya and had not brought himself to open it; now it lay in his breast pocket, and the thought of it unsettled him in an unpleasant way. Sincerely, in the depths of his soul, he now considered his marriage to Tanya a mistake, was glad that they had parted for good, and the memory of this woman — who had in the end become a walking, living shadow, in whom everything seemed already dead except for her large, intently gazing, intelligent eyes — that memory stirred in him nothing but pity and irritation at himself. The handwriting on the envelope reminded him of how, some two years ago, he had been unjust and cruel, how he had taken out his inner emptiness, boredom, loneliness, and dissatisfaction with life on people who were entirely innocent. He also recalled how he had once torn his dissertation and all the articles written during his illness into tiny scraps and thrown them out the window, and how the scraps, carried by the wind, had caught on the trees and flowers; in every line he had seen strange, groundless pretensions, frivolous swagger, insolence, a mania for greatness, and it had struck him as though he were reading a description of his own vices; but when the last notebook was torn up and flew out the window, he suddenly, for some reason, felt vexed and bitter, went to his wife, and said many unpleasant things to her. My God, how he had tormented her! Once, wishing to cause her pain, he told her that her father had played an unattractive role in their romance, since he had asked him to marry her; Yegor Semyonych accidentally overheard this, ran into the room, and in his despair could not utter a single word — he only shuffled in place and made some strange moaning sound, as though his tongue had been taken from him, while Tanya, looking at her father, cried out in a heartrending voice and fainted. It was disgraceful.

All of this came back to him at the sight of the familiar handwriting. Kovrin went out onto the balcony; the night was calm and warm, and the air smelled of the sea. The wonderful bay reflected the moon and the lights and had a color that was difficult to name. It was a tender, soft blend of blue and green; in places the water resembled blue vitriol in color, and in other places it seemed as though the moonlight had thickened and, instead of water, filled the bay — and altogether, what a harmony of colors, what a peaceful, serene, and lofty mood!

On the floor below, beneath the balcony, the windows were apparently open, because women's voices and laughter could be heard distinctly. It seemed there was a party going on down there.

Kovrin made an effort, opened the letter, and, going back into his room, read:

"My father has just died. I owe this to you, for you killed him. Our garden is perishing, strangers have already taken it over — that is to say, the very thing poor father feared so much is coming to pass. I owe this to you as well. I hate you with all my soul and wish that you would perish soon. Oh, how I suffer! My soul is burning with unbearable pain… Curse you. I took you for an extraordinary man, for a genius, I loved you, but you turned out to be a madman…"

Kovrin could read no further, tore the letter up, and threw it away. A restlessness that resembled fear took hold of him. Behind the screen, Varvara Nikolaevna slept, and her breathing was audible; from the floor below came women's voices and laughter, yet he had the feeling that there was not a single living soul in the entire hotel besides himself. Because the unhappy, grief-stricken Tanya had cursed him in her letter and wished him to perish, he felt a creeping dread, and he glanced fleetingly at the door, as though afraid that the unknown force which had wrought such destruction in his life and in the lives of those close to him over the course of just two years might walk into the room and take hold of him again.

He already knew from experience that when the nerves run wild, the best remedy is work. One must sit down at a table and force oneself, no matter what, to concentrate on a single thought. He took from his red briefcase a notebook in which the outline of a short compiled work had been sketched,he had invented in case Crimea turned out to be dull with nothing to do. He sat down at the table and got to work on the outline, and it seemed to him that his peaceful, submissive, indifferent mood was returning. The notebook with the outline even prompted some reflection on the vanity of worldly things. He thought about how much life demands in exchange for the trivial or altogether ordinary blessings it can offer a person. For instance, in order to obtain a university chair before forty, to be an ordinary professor, to expound ordinary and moreover borrowed ideas in a sluggish, tedious, ponderous style — in a word, in order to attain the position of a mediocre scholar, he, Kovrin, had needed to study for fifteen years, to work day and night, to endure a severe mental illness, to survive an unhappy marriage, and to commit all manner of foolish and unjust acts that one would rather not remember. Kovrin now clearly recognized that he was a mediocrity, and he readily made his peace with this, since in his view every person ought to be content with what he is.

     The outline had nearly calmed him entirely, but the torn letter lay white on the floor and prevented him from concentrating. He rose from the table, gathered the scraps of the letter, and threw them out the window, but a light breeze blew in from the sea and scattered the scraps across the windowsill. Once again he was seized by an unease resembling fear, and it began to seem as though there was not a single soul in the entire hotel besides himself… He went out onto the balcony. The bay gazed at him like a living thing with countless blue, dark blue, turquoise, and fiery eyes, and beckoned to him. Indeed, it was hot and stuffy, and a swim would not have gone amiss.

     Suddenly, on the floor below, beneath the balcony, a violin began to play, and two tender women's voices sang. It was something familiar. The romance being sung below told of some girl, sick with imagination, who heard mysterious sounds in the garden at night and decided that this was a sacred harmony, incomprehensible to us mortals… Kovrin's breath caught, his heart contracted with sadness, and a wonderful, sweet joy he had long since forgotten trembled in his chest.

     A tall black column, resembling a whirlwind or waterspout, appeared on the far shore of the bay. It moved across the bay toward the hotel with terrifying speed, growing smaller and darker, and Kovrin barely had time to step aside to make way… A monk with an uncovered gray head and black eyebrows, barefoot, his arms crossed on his chest, swept past and stopped in the middle of the room.

     — Why did you not believe me? — he asked reproachfully, gazing at Kovrin with tenderness. — If you had believed me then, that you were a genius, you would not have spent these two years so sadly and meagerly.

     Kovrin already believed that he was God's chosen one and a genius, he vividly recalled all his former conversations with the black monk and wanted to speak, but blood was flowing from his throat straight onto his chest, and not knowing what to do, he moved his hands across his chest, and his cuffs grew wet with blood. He wanted to call Varvara Nikolaevna, who was sleeping behind the screen, made an effort, and said:

     — Tanya!

     He fell to the floor and, raising himself on his arms, called again:

     — Tanya!

     He was calling Tanya, calling the large garden with its luxuriant flowers sprinkled with dew, calling the park, the pines with their shaggy roots, the rye field, his wonderful science, his youth, his boldness, his joy, calling the life that had been so beautiful. He saw a large pool of blood on the floor near his face and could no longer utter a single word from weakness, but an inexpressible, boundless happiness filled his entire being. Below the balcony a serenade was being played, and the black monk whispered to him that he was a genius and that he was dying for the sole reason that his frail human body had already lost its equilibrium and could no longer serve as a vessel for genius.

     When Varvara Nikolaevna woke and came out from behind the screen, Kovrin was already dead, and a blissful smile had frozen on his face.

     VOLODYA 

     One summer Sunday, at around five in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old youth, plain-looking, sickly, and timid,sat in the gazebo at the Shumikhin dacha and was bored. His gloomy thoughts ran in three directions. First, the next day, Monday, he had an exam in mathematics; he knew that if he failed to solve the written problem tomorrow, he would be expelled, since he had already spent two years in the sixth grade and had a yearly grade of 2 3/4 in algebra. Second, his stay at the Shumikhin household, wealthy people with pretensions to aristocracy, caused constant pain to his pride. It seemed to him that Madame Shumikhina and her nieces looked at him and his maman as poor relations and hangers-on, that they did not respect maman and laughed at her. Once he accidentally overheard Madame Shumikhina telling her cousin Anna Fyodorovna on the terrace that his maman still tried to look young and made herself up, that she never paid her gambling debts and had a fondness for other people's shoes and other people's tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to visit the Shumikhin place, described to her the humiliating role she played with these people, tried to persuade her, said rude things to her, but she, frivolous and spoiled, having gone through two fortunes in her lifetime — her own and her husband's — always drawn to high society, did not understand him, and Volodya had to escort her to the detested dacha twice a week.

Third, the young man could not for a moment shake off a strange, unpleasant feeling that was entirely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with the Shumikhin cousin and guest, Anna Fyodorovna. She was a lively, loud-voiced, and giggly little lady of about thirty, healthy, sturdy, rosy-cheeked, with round shoulders, a round fleshy chin, and a constant smile on her thin lips. She was neither pretty nor young — Volodya knew this perfectly well — but for some reason he could not help thinking about her, could not help watching her when, playing croquet, she shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back, or when, after long bouts of laughter and running up and down the stairs, she would drop into an armchair and, squinting her eyes and breathing heavily, pretend that her chest felt tight and stifled. She was married. Her husband, a respectable architect, came to the dacha once a week, slept soundly, and returned to the city. Volodya's strange feeling had begun with an inexplicable hatred he developed for this architect, and with the joy he felt every time the man left for the city.

Now, sitting in the gazebo and thinking about tomorrow's exam and about maman being laughed at, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (as the Shumikhin family called Anna Fyodorovna), to hear her laugh, the rustle of her dress... This desire bore no resemblance to the pure, poetic love he knew from novels and dreamed of every night before falling asleep; it was strange, incomprehensible, and he was ashamed of it and afraid of it, as of something very bad and unclean, something hard to admit even to oneself...

"This isn't love," he told himself. "People don't fall in love with thirty-year-old married women... It's simply a little flirtation... Yes, a flirtation..."

Thinking about the flirtation, he recalled his unconquerable shyness, his lack of a mustache, his freckles, his narrow eyes, and in his imagination he placed himself beside Nyuta — and the pair seemed impossible to him; then he hurried to picture himself as handsome, bold, witty, dressed in the very latest fashion...

In the midst of his daydreaming, as he sat hunched over, staring at the ground in a dark corner of the gazebo, light footsteps were heard. Someone was walking unhurriedly along the path. Soon the footsteps fell silent and something white flashed at the entrance.

"Is anyone here?" asked a woman's voice.

Volodya recognized the voice and raised his head in alarm.

"Who's there?" Nyuta asked, stepping into the gazebo. "Ah, it's you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? How can anyone just keep thinking, thinking, thinking... one could go mad that way!"

Volodya stood up and looked at Nyuta in confusion. She had just come back from the bathhouse. A sheet and a fluffy towel hung over her shoulders, and from beneath the white silk kerchief on her head peeked wet strands of hair clinging to her forehead. She gave off the damp, cool scent of the bathhouse and almond soap. She was out of breath from walking quickly.The top button of her blouse was undone, so that yo

Volodya listened, scratching his temple in heavy, strained thought.

     - Only very proud people keep silent and love solitude, - Nyuta continued, pulling his hand away from his temple. - You are proud, Volodya. Why do you look from under your brows? Look me straight in the face! Come on, you seal!

     Volodya decided to speak. Wanting to smile, he began twitching his lower lip, blinking his eyes, and again reached his hand toward his temple.

     - I... I love you! - he said.

     Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed.

     - What do I hear?! - she sang out, the way opera singers do when they hear something terrible. - How? What did you say? Repeat it, repeat it...

     - I... I love you! - Volodya repeated.

     And without any involvement of his own will, understanding and grasping nothing, he took half a step toward Nyuta and took her by the hand above the wrist. His eyes grew dim and tears welled up, the whole world turned into one large, shaggy towel that smelled of the bathhouse.

     - Bravo, bravo! - he heard cheerful laughter. - Why are you silent? I want you to speak! Well?

     Seeing that no one was stopping him from holding her hand, Volodya glanced at Nyuta's laughing face and awkwardly, clumsily took her by the waist with both hands, his wrists meeting at her back. He held her by the waist with both hands, while she, throwing her hands behind her head and showing the dimples at her elbows, adjusted her hair under her kerchief and said in a calm voice:

     - You must be nimble, Volodya, charming, sweet, and one can only become that way under the influence of women's company. But what an unpleasant... unkind face you have. You must talk, laugh... Yes, Volodya, don't be a grouch, you are young and will have plenty of time to philosophize. Now let me go, I'll be on my way. Let go!

     She freed her waist without effort and, humming something, walked out of the gazebo. Volodya was left alone. He smoothed his hair, smiled, and paced from corner to corner three times, then sat down on the bench and smiled once more. He felt unbearably ashamed, so much so that he was even surprised that human shame could reach such sharpness and intensity. From shame he smiled, whispered some incoherent words, and gestured.

     He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and most of all ashamed that he had dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist, when neither by age, nor by his outward qualities, nor by his social standing did he, as it seemed to him, have any right to do so.

     He jumped up, left the gazebo, and without looking back walked deep into the garden, away from the house.

     "Oh, if only I could leave here sooner! - he thought, clutching his head. - God, if only I could!"

     The train on which Volodya was to travel with maman departed at eight forty. There were about three hours left until the train, but he would have gladly gone to the station right now, without waiting for maman.

     At around eight o'clock he was approaching the house. His whole figure expressed resolve: whatever happens, happens! He had decided to enter boldly, look straight ahead, speak loudly, no matter what.

     He passed through the terrace, the large hall, the drawing room, and stopped in the last one to catch his breath. From there he could hear tea being drunk in the adjoining dining room. Mme Shumikhina, maman, and Nyuta were talking and laughing about something.

     Volodya listened.

     - I assure you! - Nyuta was saying. - I couldn't believe my own eyes! When he began declaring his love to me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I didn't recognize him. And you know, he has a certain manner! When he said he was in love with me, there was something wild in his face, like a Circassian.

     - Really! - maman gasped, dissolving into prolonged laughter. - Really! How he reminds me of his father!

     Volodya ran back and burst out into the fresh air. "And how can they speak of this out loud! - he agonized, throwing up his hands and staring at the sky in horror. - They speak of it openly, coolly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?"

     But he had to go back into the house, no matter what. He walked along the path three times, calmed down a little, and went inside."Why don't you come for tea on time?" Mme Shumikhina asked sternly.

"I'm sorry, I... I have to go," he muttered, not raising his eyes. "Maman, it's already eight o'clock!"

"Go on your own, my dear," maman said languidly, "I'm staying the night at Lili's. Goodbye, my friend... Let me make the sign of the cross over you..."

She crossed her son and said in French, turning to Nyuta:

"He looks a little like Lermontov... Don't you think?"

Having said his goodbyes as best he could without looking at anyone's face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later he was already walking down the road to the station, and he was glad of it. He felt neither frightened nor ashamed now, and he breathed easily and freely.

Half a verst from the station he sat down on a stone by the road and began watching the sun, which had sunk more than halfway behind the embankment. Here and there lights were already coming on at the station, a single murky green light flickered, but no train was yet in sight. Volodya enjoyed sitting still, not moving, and listening to the evening gradually closing in. The dim light of the gazebo, the footsteps, the smell of the bathhouse, the laughter and the waist — all of it appeared in his imagination with striking clarity, and none of it seemed as frightening or significant as before...

"It's nothing... She didn't pull her hand away and she laughed when I held her by the waist," he thought, "which means she liked it. If she had found it unpleasant, she would have gotten angry..."

And now Volodya felt annoyed that he hadn't had enough courage back there in the gazebo. He regretted leaving so foolishly, and he was already certain that if the moment were to repeat itself, he would be bolder and take a simpler view of things.

And it wouldn't be hard for the moment to repeat itself. At the Shukmikhins' they take long walks after supper. If Volodya were to walk with Nyuta through the dark garden, there would be his chance!

"I'll go back," he thought, "and leave tomorrow on the morning train... I'll say I missed the train."

And he went back... Mme Shumikhina, maman, Nyuta, and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace playing vint. When Volodya lied to them that he had missed the train, they worried he might miss his exam the next day and advised him to get up early. The whole time they played cards he sat to one side, hungrily watching Nyuta and waiting... He already had a plan in his head: he would approach Nyuta in the dark, take her by the hand, then put his arm around her; there would be no need to say anything, since everything would be clear to both of them without words.

But after supper the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and continued playing cards. They played until one in the morning and then went off to bed.

"How utterly stupid all this is!" Volodya fretted as he lay down in bed. "But never mind, I'll wait until tomorrow... Tomorrow in the gazebo again. It's fine..."

He made no effort to sleep but sat up in bed, arms wrapped around his knees, and thought. The thought of the exam was repugnant to him. He had already decided he would be expelled, and that there was nothing terrible about it. On the contrary, everything was very good, even very good indeed. Tomorrow he would be free as a bird, would put on civilian clothes, smoke openly, come here whenever he liked and court Nyuta; and he would no longer be a schoolboy but a "young man." As for the rest — what people call a career and a future — it was all perfectly clear: Volodya would enlist as a volunteer, become a telegraph operator, or finally go to work at a pharmacy and work his way up to pharmacist... there was no shortage of positions. An hour passed, then another, and still he sat and thought...

At around three in the morning, when it was already growing light, the door creaked softly and maman came into the room.

"You're not asleep?" she asked, yawning. "Sleep, sleep, I'll only be a moment... I just need to get some drops..."

"What for?"

"Poor Lili is having spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow..."

She took a small bottle of something from the cabinet, went to the window, read the label, and left.

"Marya Leontyevna, those are the wrong drops!" Volodya heard a woman's voice a moment later. "That's lily of the valley, but Lili is asking for morphine. Is your son asleep? Ask him to find it..."

It was Nyuta's voice. Volodya went cold. He quickly pulled on his trousers, threw his overcoat over his shoulders, and went to the door.

"You understand? Morphine!" Nyuta was explaining in a whisper. "It should say so in Latin on the label.""Wake Volodya, he'll find it...

     Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was wearing the same blouse she had worn to go swimming. Her hair was uncombed, scattered across her shoulders, her face sleepy, dark in the twilight...

     - So Volodya isn't sleeping... - she said. - Volodya, look in the cabinet for morphine, dear! The trouble with this Lili... She always needs something.

     Maman muttered something, yawned, and left.

     - Well, look then, - said Nyuta. - Why are you just standing there?

     Volodya went to the little cabinet, knelt down, and began sorting through the vials and boxes of medicine. His hands were trembling, and in his chest and stomach there was a sensation as if cold waves were running through all his insides. The smell of ether, carbolic acid, and various herbs - which he kept grabbing at needlessly with his trembling hands, scattering them in the process - made him feel stifled and dizzy.

     "It seems maman has gone, - he thought. - That's good... good..."

     - Will you be long? - Nyuta asked in a drawn-out voice.

     - Just a moment... This looks like morphine... - said Volodya, reading the word "morph..." on one of the labels. - Here you are!

     Nyuta stood in the doorway with one foot in the corridor and the other in his room. She was trying to fix her hair, which was difficult to manage - it was so thick and long! - and she gazed absently at Volodya. In her loose blouse, sleepy, with her hair down, in the dim light that came into the room from the white sky not yet lit by the sun, she seemed to Volodya enchanting, magnificent... Spellbound, trembling all over, and with pleasure recalling how he had embraced that wonderful body in the gazebo, he handed her the drops and said:

     - You are so...

     - What?

     She stepped into the room.

     - What? - she asked, smiling.

     He was silent and looked at her, then, as he had done in the gazebo, took her by the hand... And she looked at him, smiled, and waited: what would happen next?

     - I love you... - he whispered.

     She stopped smiling, thought for a moment, and said:

     - Wait, I think someone's coming. Oh, these schoolboys! - she said in a low voice, walking to the door and peering into the corridor. - No, no one there...

     She came back...

     Then it seemed to Volodya that the room, Nyuta, the dawn, and himself - all merged into one sensation of sharp, extraordinary, unprecedented happiness, for which one could give up one's whole life and endure eternal torment, but half a minute passed, and it all suddenly vanished. Volodya saw only a full, unattractive face distorted by an expression of disgust, and he himself suddenly felt revulsion at what had happened.

     - Well, I must be going, - said Nyuta, looking Volodya over with disdain. - How ugly and pathetic... ugh, ugly duckling!

     How hideous her long hair, her loose blouse, her steps, her voice now seemed to Volodya!..

     "Ugly duckling... - he thought after she had gone. - I really am ugly... Everything is ugly."

     Outside the sun was already rising, birds were singing loudly; one could hear the gardener walking in the garden and the creaking of his wheelbarrow... And a little later came the lowing of cows and the sounds of a shepherd's pipe. The sunlight and the sounds spoke of a life somewhere in this world that was pure, graceful, and poetic. But where was it? Neither maman nor any of the people around him had ever spoken to Volodya of such a life.

     When the footman came to wake him for the morning train, he pretended to be asleep...

     "To hell with it all!" - he thought.

     He got out of bed after ten o'clock. Combing his hair in front of the mirror and looking at his unattractive face, pale from a sleepless night, he thought:

     "Quite right... Ugly duckling."

     When maman saw him and was horrified that he had missed his exam, Volodya said:

     - I overslept, maman... But don't worry, I'll get a doctor's note.

     Madame Shumikhina and Nyuta woke up after noon. Volodya heard how Madame Shumikhina, upon waking, opened her window with a clatter, and how Nyuta answered her coarse voice with a rolling laugh. He saw,as the door opened and a procession of nieces and hangers-on (maman among the latter) streamed from the drawing room toward breakfast, as Nyuta's freshly washed, laughing face flashed by, and beside it the dark eyebrows and beard of the architect who had just arrived.

     Nyuta was dressed in a Little Russian costume that did not suit her at all and made her look awkward; the architect made vulgar, flat jokes; the cutlets served at breakfast had far too much onion in them - or so it seemed to Volodya. It also seemed to him that Nyuta was laughing loudly on purpose and glancing in his direction, to make it clear that the memory of the night troubled her not in the least and that she took no notice of the ugly duckling sitting at the table.

     At four o'clock Volodya was riding with maman to the station. Sordid memories, a sleepless night, the expulsion from the gymnasium that awaited him, pangs of conscience - all of this now stirred in him a heavy, gloomy rage. He looked at maman's thin profile, her little nose, the waterproof coat Nyuta had given her, and muttered:

     - Why do you powder yourself? It's unbecoming at your age! You make yourself up, you don't pay your gambling debts, you smoke other people's tobacco... it's disgusting! I don't love you... I don't love you!

     He was insulting her, and she looked about with frightened eyes, wrung her little hands, and whispered in horror:

     - What's the matter with you, my dear? Good heavens, the coachman will hear! Be quiet, or the coachman will hear! He can hear everything!

     - I don't love you... I don't love you! - he went on, breathless. - You are immoral, heartless... Don't you dare wear that waterproof! Do you hear? Or I'll tear it to shreds...

     - Come to your senses, my child! - maman wept. - The coachman will hear!

     - And where is my father's estate? Where is your money? You've squandered it all! I'm not ashamed of my poverty, but I am ashamed to have such a mother... When my friends ask about you, I always blush.

     The train journey to the city took two stops. The whole time Volodya stood on the platform, trembling all over. He did not want to go into the carriage, where his mother sat - his mother whom he hated. He hated himself, the conductors, the smoke from the locomotive, the cold, which he blamed for his shivering... And the heavier his heart grew, the more keenly he felt that somewhere in this world, among certain people, there existed a life that was pure, noble, warm, graceful, full of love, tenderness, joy, and freedom... He felt this and ached with such intensity that even a fellow passenger, peering closely at his face, asked:

     - You must have a toothache?

     In the city maman and Volodya lived with Marya Petrovna, a gentlewoman who rented a large apartment and sublet it to lodgers. Maman rented two rooms: in one, with windows, where her bed stood and two pictures in gilded frames hung on the walls, she lived herself, while in the other, adjoining it, small and dark, lived Volodya. There was a sofa on which he slept, and besides this sofa there was no other furniture; the whole room was taken up with wicker baskets of clothes, hatboxes, and all manner of odds and ends that maman kept for some reason. Volodya did his lessons in his mother's room or in the "common room" - as the large room was called where all the lodgers gathered at dinner and in the evenings.

     Returning home, he lay down on the sofa and pulled the blanket over himself to stop the shivering. The hatboxes, baskets, and clutter reminded him that he had no room of his own, no refuge where he could hide from maman, from her guests, and from the voices now drifting in from the "common room"; his satchel and books, scattered about the corners, reminded him of the examination he had missed... For no particular reason the thought of Menton came to him, where he had lived with his late father at the age of seven; he recalled Biarritz and two English girls with whom he had run along the sand... He wanted to bring back the color of the sky and the ocean, the height of the waves, and his mood of that time, but he could not manage it; the English girls flickered through his imagination as if alive, while everything else blurred together and dissolved into confusion.

     "No, it's cold here," thought Volodya, got up, put on his overcoat, and went to the "common room."

     In the "common room" they were drinking tea.Three people were sitting at the samovar: maman, the music teacher, an old woman in a tortoiseshell pince-nez, and Augustin Mikhailych, an elderly, very fat Frenchman who worked at a perfume factory.

     - I haven't had dinner today, - maman was saying. - I should send the maid for some bread.

     - Dunyash! - the Frenchman called out.

     It turned out the mistress of the house had sent the maid off somewhere.

     - Oh, that's nothing, - said the Frenchman, smiling broadly. - I'll go for the bread myself right now. Oh, it's nothing!

     He set his thick, foul-smelling cigar down in a prominent place, put on his hat, and went out. After he left, maman began telling the music teacher about her stay at the Shumikhins' and how well they had received her there.

     - After all, Lili Shumikhina is a relative of mine... - she was saying. - Her late husband, General Shukikhin, is a cousin of my husband's. And she herself was born Baroness Kolb...

     - Maman, that's not true! - Volodya said irritably. - Why do you lie?

     He knew perfectly well that maman was telling the truth; there was not a single false word in her story about General Shukikhin and the born Baroness Kolb, and yet he still felt that she was lying. The lie was felt in her manner of speaking, in her facial expression, in her gaze, in everything.

     - You're lying! - Volodya repeated and struck the table with his fist with such force that all the crockery rattled and maman's tea spilled.

     - Why do you tell stories about generals and baronesses? It's all lies!

     The music teacher was flustered and coughed into her handkerchief, pretending she had choked, and maman burst into tears.

     "Where can I go?" thought Volodya.

     He had already been out on the street; it was shameful to go to his friends. Again, at the wrong moment, he recalled the two English girls... He paced back and forth across the "common room" and went into Augustin Mikhailych's room. It smelled strongly of essential oils and glycerin soap. On the table, on the windowsills, and even on the chairs stood a great many flasks, glasses, and small tumblers filled with colored liquids. Volodya picked up a newspaper from the table, unfolded it, and read the title: "Figaro"... The newspaper gave off some kind of strong and pleasant scent. Then he picked up a revolver from the table...

     - Come now, don't pay any attention! - the music teacher was consoling maman in the next room. - He's still so young! At his age young men always allow themselves too much. One has to put up with it.

     - No, Evgenia Andreyevna, he is too spoiled! - maman was saying in a drawn-out tone. - There is no elder over him, and I am weak and can do nothing. No, I am unhappy!

     Volodya put the barrel of the revolver in his mouth, felt for something resembling a trigger or a catch and pressed it with his finger... Then he felt for another protrusion and pressed again. Taking the barrel out of his mouth, he wiped it on the hem of his coat and examined the lock; he had never in his life handled a weapon before...

     - I think this needs to be pulled up... - he reasoned. - Yes, I think so...

     Augustin Mikhailych came into the "common room" and, laughing, began telling something. Volodya put the barrel back in his mouth, clenched it with his teeth, and pressed something with his finger. A shot rang out... Something struck Volodya on the back of the head with terrible force, and he fell onto the table, his face landing right among the tumblers and flasks. Then he saw his late father in a top hat with a wide black band, who had worn mourning in Menton for some lady, suddenly seize him with both arms, and the two of them went flying into some very dark, deep abyss.

     Then everything became confused and disappeared...

Ionych

I

When visitors to the provincial town of S. complained of boredom and the monotony of life there, the locals would say, as if in their own defense, that on the contrary, S. was a very fine place — that S. had a library, a theater, a club, that there were balls, and that, finally, there were clever, interesting, agreeable families with whom one could make acquaintance. And they would point to the Turkin family as the most cultivated and talented of all.

     This family lived on the main street, near the governor's house, in their own home. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a stout, handsome dark-haired man with side-whiskers, organized amateur theatricals for charitable purposes, played old generals himself, and coughed in a very comical way while doing so. He knew a great many anecdotes, riddles, and proverbs, loved to joke and be witty, and always wore an expression that made it impossible to tell whether he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a slender, pretty woman in pince-nez, wrote novellas and novels and gladly read them aloud to her guests. Their daughter, Ekaterina Ivanovna, a young girl, played the piano. In short, every member of the family had some talent of their own. The Turkins received guests warmly and showed off their talents cheerfully, with genuine simplicity. Their large stone house was spacious and cool in summer, half the windows looked out onto an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests were in the house, knives clattered in the kitchen and the smell of fried onions drifted in from the yard — and this always promised a generous and delicious supper.

     And to Dr. Startsev, Dmitri Ionych, when he had just been appointed district physician and had settled in Dyalizh, nine versts from S., people also said that he, as an educated man, simply had to make the acquaintance of the Turkins. One winter he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich on the street; they talked about the weather, the theater, cholera, and an invitation followed. In spring, on a holiday — it was Ascension Day — after seeing his patients, Startsev set off for town to enjoy himself a little and pick up a few things while he was at it. He walked on foot, unhurriedly (he had no horses of his own yet), humming to himself all the while: When I had not yet drunk tears from the cup of life…

In town he had dinner, strolled in the garden, and then somehow the invitation from Ivan Petrovich came back to him of its own accord, and he decided to call on the Turkins and see what sort of people they were.

     "Welcome, welcome," said Ivan Petrovich, meeting him on the porch. "I am very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Come along, I'll introduce you to my better half. I tell him, Verochka," he continued, introducing the doctor to his wife, "I tell him he has absolutely no Roman right to sit there in his hospital — he must give his leisure time to society. Isn't that so, my dear?"

     "Sit here," said Vera Iosifovna, seating the guest beside her. "You may pay court to me. My husband is jealous — he's an Othello — but we shall try to behave in such a way that he notices nothing."

     "Ah, you little rogue, you spoiled thing…" Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. "You've come at just the right moment," he said, turning again to the guest, "my better half has written a majority novel and will be reading it aloud today."

     "Zhanchik," said Vera Iosifovna to her husband, "dites que l'on nous donne du the."

     Startsev was introduced to Ekaterina Ivanovna, an eighteen-year-old girl who looked very much like her mother — the same slender, pretty figure. Her expression was still childlike and her waist was slim and delicate; and her virginal yet already developed bosom, beautiful and healthy, spoke of spring, of real spring. Then they had tea with jam, honey, sweets, and very tasty biscuits that melted in the mouth. As evening came on, guests gradually gathered, and to each of them Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes and said:

     "Welcome, welcome."

     Then everyone sat in the drawing room with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began like this: "The frost was growing sharper…" The windows were thrown wide open, the clatter of knives in the kitchen could be heard, and the smell of fried onions drifted in… It was comfortable in the soft, deep armchairs, the lights flickered so gently in the twilight of the drawing room; and now, on a summer evening,when voices and laughter drifted in from the street and the scent of lilac wafted from the yard, it was hard to imagine how the frost was deepening and how the setting sun lit with its cold rays the snowy plain and the lone traveler making his way along the road; Vera Iosifovna read of how a young, beautiful countess established schools, hospitals, and libraries on her country estate and how she fell in love with a wandering artist, – she read of things that never happen in real life, and yet it was pleasant and comfortable to listen, and such good, peaceful thoughts came to mind – one had no desire to get up.

     "Not bad at all…" Ivan Petrovich said quietly.

     And one of the guests, listening and letting his thoughts drift somewhere very, very far away, said barely audibly:

     "Yes… indeed…"

     An hour passed, then another. The orchestra in the nearby city garden was playing and a choir was singing folk songs. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, they sat in silence for about five minutes listening to "Luchinushka" being sung by the choir, and that song conveyed something that was not in the novel but that does happen in life.

     "Do you publish your work in journals?" Startsev asked Vera Iosifovna.

     "No," she replied, "I don't publish anywhere. I write and then hide it away in my cabinet. What is the point of publishing?" she explained. "After all, we have means."

     And for some reason everyone sighed.

     "Now you, Kotik, play something," Ivan Petrovich said to his daughter.

     The piano lid was raised, the sheet music that had been set out in readiness was opened. Ekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands; then immediately struck them again with full force, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest shuddered, she kept hammering stubbornly at the same spot, and it seemed she would not stop until she had driven the keys deep into the piano. The drawing room filled with thunder; everything thundered: the floor, the ceiling, the furniture… Ekaterina Ivanovna was playing a difficult passage, interesting precisely for its difficulty, long and monotonous, and as Startsev listened he pictured stones tumbling down from a high mountain, tumbling and tumbling endlessly, and he wished they would stop sooner, and at the same time Ekaterina Ivanovna, flushed with effort, strong, energetic, with a lock of hair fallen across her forehead, pleased him very much. After a winter spent in Dyalizh among the sick and the peasants, to sit in a drawing room, to look at this young, graceful, and presumably pure creature and to listen to these loud, tiresome, yet nonetheless cultured sounds – was so pleasant, so new…

     "Well, Kotik, today you played as you have never played before," Ivan Petrovich said with tears in his eyes when his daughter finished and stood up. "Die, Denis, you'll never write anything better."

     Everyone gathered around her, congratulating her, marveling, assuring her that they had not heard such music in a long time, and she listened in silence, smiling faintly, and her whole figure radiated triumph.

     "Wonderful! Magnificent!"

     "Wonderful!" said Startsev too, swept up in the general enthusiasm. "Where did you study music?" he asked Ekaterina Ivanovna. "At the conservatory?"

     "No, I'm only planning to go to the conservatory, for now I studied here, with Madame Zavlovskaya."

     "Did you finish your schooling at the local gymnasium?"

     "Oh no!" Vera Iosifovna answered for her. "We had teachers come to the house; at a gymnasium or institute, you must agree, there could be bad influences; while a girl is growing up she should be under the influence of her mother alone."

     "Even so, I am going to the conservatory," said Ekaterina Ivanovna.

     "No, Kotik loves her mama. Kotik won't upset papa and mama."

     "No, I'm going! I'm going!" said Ekaterina Ivanovna, joking and being willful, and stamped her little foot.

     And then at supper Ivan Petrovich displayed his own talents. Laughing only with his eyes, he told anecdotes, made witty remarks, proposed amusing riddles and solved them himself, and all the while spoke in his peculiar language, honed through long practice in wit and evidently long since become a habit with him: bolshinskiy, not badsome, it has contorted my gratitude to you…

     But that was not all. When the guests, full and content, crowded in the entryway sorting out their coats and walking sticks, the footman Pavlusha bustled about them, or,as he was called here, Pava, a boy of about fourteen, with a crew cut and chubby cheeks.

     – Come on, Pava, do your bit! – Ivan Petrovich said to him.

     Pava struck a pose, raised his hand in the air and declared in a tragic tone:

     – Die, you wretch!

     And everyone burst out laughing.

     "Amusing," thought Startsev, stepping out onto the street.

     He stopped by a restaurant and had a beer, then set off on foot to his place in Dyalizh. He walked the whole way humming: Your voice, so tender and so languid, to me…

     

     Having walked nine versts and then lying down to sleep, he felt not the slightest fatigue, but on the contrary, it seemed to him that he would have gladly walked another twenty versts.

     "Not bad at all…" he recalled as he drifted off, and laughed.

     

II

     

     Startsev kept meaning to visit the Turkins, but there was a great deal of work at the hospital, and he simply could not find a free hour. More than a year passed this way in toil and solitude; but then a letter arrived from the city in a blue envelope…

     Vera Iosifovna had long suffered from migraines, but lately, as Kotik frightened them every day with talk of leaving for the conservatory, the attacks had grown more and more frequent. All the town's doctors had been to the Turkins; at last it was the zemstvo doctor's turn. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter asking him to come and ease her suffering. Startsev came, and after that began visiting the Turkins often, very often… He did in fact help Vera Iosifovna a little, and she was already telling all her guests that he was an extraordinary, remarkable doctor. But he was coming to the Turkins no longer for her migraines…

     A holiday. Yekaterina Ivanovna had finished her long, wearisome exercises at the piano. Then they sat for a long time in the dining room drinking tea, and Ivan Petrovich told some amusing story. But then the doorbell rang; someone had to go to the entrance hall to greet a guest; Startsev seized the moment of confusion and whispered to Yekaterina Ivanovna, greatly agitated:

     – For God's sake, I beg you, don't torment me, let's go into the garden!

     She shrugged, as if puzzled and unable to understand what he wanted from her, but she stood up and went.

     – You play the piano for three, four hours at a time, – he said, following her, – then you sit with your mother, and there's no way to talk to you at all. Give me at least a quarter of an hour, I beg you.

     Autumn was approaching, and the old garden was quiet and melancholy, with dark leaves lying on the paths. It grew dark early now.

     – I haven't seen you for a whole week, – Startsev continued, – and if you only knew what agony that is! Let's sit down. Hear me out.

     They had a favorite spot in the garden: a bench under an old broad maple tree. And now they sat down on that bench.

     – What do you want? – Yekaterina Ivanovna asked drily, in a businesslike tone.

     – I haven't seen you for a whole week, I haven't heard your voice in so long. I desperately want, I crave the sound of your voice. Say something.

     He was enchanted by her freshness, the naive expression of her eyes and cheeks. Even in the way her dress sat on her he saw something extraordinarily lovely, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. And at the same time, despite this naivety, she seemed to him very intelligent and mature beyond her years. With her he could talk about literature, about art, about anything at all, could complain to her about life, about people, though during a serious conversation it sometimes happened that she would suddenly start laughing at the wrong moment or run off into the house. She, like almost all the girls in S., read a great deal (in general, very little was read in S., and at the local library they used to say that if not for the girls and the young Jewish men, they might as well close the place); this pleased Startsev enormously, and each time he eagerly asked her what she had been reading in recent days, and listened, enchanted, as she told him.

     – What have you been reading this week, while we haven't seen each other? – he asked now. – Tell me, please.

     – I've been reading Pisemsky.

     – What exactly?

     – "A Thousand Souls," – answered Kotik. – And what a funny name Pisemsky had: Alexei Feofilaktych!

     – Where are you going? – Startsev cried in dismay, as she suddenly stood up and headed toward the house. – I absolutely must talk to you,"I must explain myself… Stay with me just five minutes! I beg you!

     She stopped as if about to say something, then awkwardly slipped a note into his hand and ran into the house, where she sat down at the piano again.

     "Tonight, at eleven o'clock in the evening," Startsev read, "be at the cemetery by the Demetti monument."

     "Well, that's really not very clever," he thought, coming to his senses. "What does the cemetery have to do with anything? What for?"

     It was clear: Kotik was playing games. Who, after all, would seriously think to arrange a rendezvous at night, far outside town, in a cemetery, when it could easily be done on the street or in the city garden? And was it fitting for him, a zemstvo doctor, an intelligent, respectable man, to sigh, receive little notes, trudge around cemeteries, and do foolish things that even schoolboys now laugh at? Where would this romance lead? What would his colleagues say when they found out? Such were Startsev's thoughts as he wandered around the tables at the club, and then at half past ten he suddenly up and went to the cemetery.

     He already had his own pair of horses and a coachman, Panteleimon, in a velvet waistcoat. The moon was shining. It was quiet and warm, but warm in an autumnal way. In the outskirts, near the slaughterhouses, dogs were howling. Startsev left the horses at the edge of town, in one of the side streets, and walked to the cemetery on foot. "Everyone has their own peculiarities," he thought. "Kotik is peculiar too, and — who knows? — perhaps she isn't joking and will come," — and he gave himself over to this faint, empty hope, and it intoxicated him.

     He walked about half a verst across a field. The cemetery showed as a dark strip in the distance, like a forest or a large garden. A white stone wall appeared, then the gates… In the moonlight one could read above the gates: "The hour cometh in which…" Startsev passed through the wicket gate, and the first thing he saw was white crosses and monuments on both sides of a broad avenue, and the black shadows cast by them and by the poplars; and all around, far and wide, white and black were visible, and the drowsy trees bent their branches over the white. It seemed brighter here than in the field; the maple leaves, shaped like paws, stood out sharply against the yellow sand of the avenues and against the flagstones, and the inscriptions on the monuments were clear. At first Startsev was struck by what he was seeing for the first time in his life and would probably never see again: a world unlike any other — a world where the moonlight was so beautiful and soft, as though this were its cradle, where there is no life, none at all, and yet in every dark poplar, in every grave, one feels the presence of a mystery that promises life — quiet, beautiful, eternal. From the flagstones and the withered flowers, mingled with the autumnal smell of leaves, there breathes forgiveness, sorrow, and peace.

     All around was silence; the stars gazed down from the sky in deep humility, and Startsev's footsteps rang out so sharply and out of place. And only when the church clock began to strike and he imagined himself dead, buried here forever, did it seem to him that someone was watching him, and for a moment he thought that this was not peace and quiet but the muffled anguish of nonexistence, a suppressed despair…

     The Demetti monument is in the form of a chapel, with an angel on top; an Italian opera company had once passed through S., one of the singers had died, she was buried here and this monument was erected. No one in town remembered her anymore, but the oil lamp above the entrance reflected the moonlight and seemed to be burning.

     There was no one. And who would come here at midnight? But Startsev waited, and, as though the moonlight were stoking the passion within him, he waited ardently and painted in his imagination kisses and embraces. He sat by the monument for about half an hour, then walked along the side avenues, hat in hand, waiting and thinking about how many women and girls lay buried here in these graves — women who had been beautiful, enchanting, who had loved, who had burned with passion through the nights, surrendering to tenderness. How cruelly, in essence, Mother Nature plays her joke on humankind, how painful it is to realize this! Startsev thought this, and at the same time he wanted to cry out that he desired, that he was waiting for love at any cost; before him the white shapes were no longer pieces of marble but beautiful bodies, he saw forms that shyly hid in the shadows of the trees, he felt warmth, and this longing grew oppressive…

     And as if a curtain had fallen, the moon slipped behind the clouds,and suddenly everything grew dark all around. Startsev barely found the gate, – it was already as dark as an autumn night, – then wandered for about an hour and a half, searching for the lane where he had left his horses.

     – I'm exhausted, I can barely stand on my feet, – he said to Panteleymon.

     And, settling into the carriage with pleasure, he thought:

     "Oh, I really shouldn't be getting fat!"

     

III

     

     The following evening he went to the Turkins' to make his proposal. But it turned out to be inconvenient, as a hairdresser was doing Yekaterina Ivanovna's hair in her room. She was getting ready to go to the club for a dance evening.

     He had to sit in the dining room for a long time again and drink tea. Ivan Petrovich, seeing that the guest was pensive and bored, took a little note from his waistcoat pocket and read aloud a funny letter from a German steward about how all the locks on the estate had broken and the shyness had collapsed.

     "They'll probably give quite a decent dowry," thought Startsev, listening absently.

     After a sleepless night he was in a dazed state, as if someone had given him something sweet and soporific to drink; his soul felt hazy, yet joyful and warm, and at the same time some cold, heavy little piece of reasoning in his head was saying:

     "Stop while you still can! Is she really a match for you? She's spoiled, capricious, sleeps until two in the afternoon, and you're a deacon's son, a district doctor…"

     "So what?" he thought. "Let it be."

     "Besides, if you marry her," the little piece continued, "her family will make you give up your district post and live in the city."

     "So what?" he thought. "In the city, then in the city. They'll give a dowry, we'll set up a home…"

     At last Yekaterina Ivanovna came in wearing a ball gown, décolleté, pretty and fresh, and Startsev gazed at her with such admiration and fell into such rapture that he could not utter a single word, but only looked at her and smiled.

     She began to say her goodbyes, and he – there was no reason for him to stay any longer – got up, saying it was time for him to go home: patients were waiting.

     – Nothing to be done, – said Ivan Petrovich, – off you go, and you can give Kotik a ride to the club while you're at it.

     Outside it was drizzling, very dark, and only by Panteleymon's hoarse cough could one guess where the horses were. The hood of the carriage was raised.

     – I walk on the carpet, you walk while you fib, – said Ivan Petrovich, helping his daughter into the carriage, – he walks while he fibs… Drive on! Goodbye, please!

     They set off.

     – I was at the cemetery yesterday, – Startsev began. – How unkind and merciless of you…

     – You were at the cemetery?

     – Yes, I was there and waited for you until nearly two o'clock. I suffered…

     – Then go on suffering, if you can't take a joke.

     Yekaterina Ivanovna, pleased that she had played such a clever trick on her admirer and that she was so deeply loved, burst out laughing and then suddenly cried out in fright, for at that very moment the horses turned sharply through the gates of the club and the carriage lurched. Startsev put his arm around Yekaterina Ivanovna's waist; she, frightened, pressed herself against him, and he could not help himself and passionately kissed her on the lips, on the chin, and held her tighter.

     – That's enough, – she said drily.

     And a moment later she was no longer in the carriage, and a policeman near the brightly lit entrance of the club was shouting at Panteleymon in a hideous voice:

     – What are you standing there for, you crow? Move along!

     Startsev drove home, but soon returned. Dressed in someone else's tailcoat and a stiff white cravat that kept sticking up and trying to slip off his collar, he sat at midnight in the club's drawing room and said to Yekaterina Ivanovna with great feeling:

     – Oh, how little those know who have never loved! It seems to me that no one has ever described love accurately, and it is hardly possible to describe this tender, joyful, agonizing feeling, and whoever has experienced it even once will not try to put it into words. What need is there for preambles, for descriptions? What need for unnecessary eloquence? My love is boundless… I ask you, I implore you, – Startsev finally said, – be my wife!

     – Dmitri Ionych, – said Yekaterina Ivanovna with a very serious expression, after a pause. – Dmitri Ionych, I am very grateful to you for the honor, I respect you, but…

She stood up and remained standing, – but, forgive me, I cannot be your wife. Let us speak seriously. Dmitri Ionych, you know, more than anything in life I love art, I am madly in love with it, I adore music, I have devoted my entire life to it. I want to be an artist, I want fame, success, freedom, and you want me to go on living in this city, to continue this empty, useless life that has become unbearable to me. To become a wife – oh no, forgive me! A person must strive toward a higher, brilliant goal, and family life would bind me forever. Dmitri Ionych (she smiled ever so slightly, because in saying "Dmitri Ionych" she was reminded of "Alexei Feofilaktych"), Dmitri Ionych, you are a kind, noble, intelligent man, you are the best of all… – tears welled up in her eyes, – I sympathize with you with all my heart, but… but you will understand…

     And, so as not to cry, she turned away and left the drawing room.

     Startsev's heart stopped its anxious beating. Walking out of the club onto the street, he first of all tore off his stiff necktie and drew a deep breath. He felt a little ashamed and his pride was wounded, – he had not expected a refusal, – and he could not believe that all his dreams, longings, and hopes had led him to such a foolish end, as if in a little play at an amateur performance. And he felt sorry for his feeling, for this love of his, so sorry that it seemed he could have burst into sobs or struck Panteleimon's broad back with all his might with his umbrella.

     For about three days things fell apart in his hands, he neither ate nor slept, but when word reached him that Ekaterina Ivanovna had left for Moscow to enter the conservatory, he calmed down and went on living as before.

     Then, sometimes recalling how he had wandered through the cemetery or how he had driven all over town searching for a tailcoat, he would lazily stretch and say:

     – What a lot of trouble, all the same!

     

IV

     

     Four years passed. In the city Startsev now had a large practice. Every morning he hurriedly received patients at his home in Dyalizh, then drove off to see patients in town, no longer with a pair of horses but with a troika with little bells, and returned home late at night. He had grown stout, put on weight, and was reluctant to walk, as he suffered from shortness of breath. And Panteleimon had also grown stout, and the wider he grew, the more sadly he sighed and complained of his bitter lot: the driving was wearing him out!

     Startsev visited various homes and met many people, but grew close to no one. The townspeople irritated him with their conversations, their outlook on life, and even their appearance. Experience had taught him little by little that while you are playing cards with a townsman or having a snack with him, he is a peaceful, good-natured, and even not unintelligent person, but the moment you start talking to him about something inedible, such as politics or science, he is at a loss or launches into such a dull and spiteful philosophy that there is nothing left to do but wave your hand and walk away. When Startsev tried to talk even with a liberal townsman, for instance about how mankind, thank God, is moving forward and that in time it will do without passports and without capital punishment, the townsman would look at him sideways and mistrustfully and ask: "So then anyone can stab whoever they like in the street?" And when Startsev, in company, at supper or tea, spoke of the need to work, that one cannot live without work, everyone took it as a reproach and began to get angry and argue persistently. For all that, the townspeople did nothing, absolutely nothing, and were interested in nothing, and there was simply no way to think of what to talk to them about. And so Startsev avoided conversation and only had snacks and played vint, and when he happened upon a family celebration at someone's house and was invited to dine, he would sit down and eat in silence, staring at his plate; and everything said at such times was uninteresting, unjust, and foolish, he felt irritated and agitated, but kept silent, and because he always kept a stern silence and stared at his plate, he was nicknamed in the city "the puffed-up Pole," though he had never been a Pole.

     He avoided such entertainments as the theater and concerts, but in compensation he played vint every evening, for about three hours, with great pleasure. He had one other pastime,which he had gradually, imperceptibly slipped into — the habit of taking out in the evenings the banknotes earned through his practice, and sometimes there were yellow and green notes stuffed into every pocket, smelling of perfume and vinegar and incense and fish oil, amounting to as much as seventy rubles; and when several hundred had accumulated, he would take them to the Mutual Credit Society and deposit them in his current account.

In all four years since Ekaterina Ivanovna's departure, he had visited the Turkins only twice, at Vera Iosifovna's invitation, as she was still being treated for migraines. Every summer Ekaterina Ivanovna came to stay with her parents, but he never once saw her; somehow it never worked out.

And so four years passed. One quiet, warm morning a letter was brought to the hospital. Vera Iosifovna wrote to Dmitri Ionych saying she missed him terribly and begged him to be sure to come and ease her suffering, and as it happened, today was also her birthday. At the bottom was a postscript: "I add my voice to mama's request. K."

Startsev thought it over and drove to the Turkins that evening.

"Ah, welcome, welcome!" Ivan Petrovich greeted him, smiling with his eyes alone. "Bonjour-te."

Vera Iosifovna, now much aged, with white hair, shook Startsev's hand, sighed affectedly, and said:

"You, doctor, have no wish to look after me, you never visit us anymore, I am too old for you now. But here a young one has arrived — perhaps she will have better luck."

And Kotik? She had grown thinner, paler, more beautiful and slender; but she was already Ekaterina Ivanovna now, not Kotik; the former freshness and expression of childlike innocence were gone. In her gaze and her manner there was something new — timid and guilty, as though here, in the Turkins' house, she no longer felt at home.

"It's been ages!" she said, offering Startsev her hand, and one could see that her heart was beating anxiously; and gazing intently and curiously into his face, she went on: "How you've filled out! You've gotten a tan, you look more mature, but on the whole you've changed very little."

And now he liked her, liked her very much, but something was already missing in her, or something was in excess — he could not have said exactly what, but something already prevented him from feeling as he once had. He did not like her pallor, her new expression, her faint smile, her voice, and a little later he no longer liked her dress, the armchair she sat in, something in the past when he had nearly married her. He recalled his love, the dreams and hopes that had stirred him four years ago — and he felt ill at ease.

They had tea with sweet pie. Then Vera Iosifovna read aloud from a novel, reading about things that never happen in real life, while Startsev listened, looking at her gray, handsome head and waiting for her to finish.

"It is not the one who cannot write stories who is talentless," he thought, "but the one who writes them and cannot conceal it."

"Not bad at all," said Ivan Petrovich. Then Ekaterina Ivanovna played the piano loudly and at length, and when she finished, she was thanked and admired for a long time.

"It's a good thing I didn't marry her," thought Startsev.

She looked at him and apparently expected him to suggest they go into the garden, but he said nothing.

"Let's talk," she said, coming up to him. "How are you living? What's going on with you? How are things? I've been thinking about you all these days," she went on nervously, "I wanted to send you a letter, I wanted to come to you myself in Dyalizh, and I had already decided to go, but then I changed my mind — God knows how you feel about me now. I have been waiting for you today with such anxiety. For heaven's sake, let's go into the garden." They went into the garden and sat on the bench under the old maple, just as they had four years ago. It was dark.

"So how are you getting on?" asked Ekaterina Ivanovna.

"Oh, fine, getting by," answered Startsev.

And he could think of nothing more to say. They were silent.

"I'm nervous," said Ekaterina Ivanovna and covered her face with her hands, "but please don't mind me. It's so good to be home, I'm so glad to see everyone and I can't quite get used to it. So many memories! I imagined we would talk and talk without stopping, until morning."Now he could see her face up close, her shining eyes, and here in the darkness she seemed younger than she had in the room, and it was almost as if her old childlike expression had returned. Indeed, she looked at him with naive curiosity, as though she wanted to get a closer look at and understand the man who had once loved her so ardently, so tenderly, and so unhappily; her eyes thanked him for that love. And he remembered everything that had been, every smallest detail, how he had wandered through the cemetery, how afterward, toward morning, exhausted, he had made his way home, and suddenly he felt sad and sorry for the past. A small flame kindled in his soul.

     "Do you remember how I walked you to that evening at the club?" he said. "It was raining then, it was dark…"

     The flame kept growing in his soul, and he already wanted to talk, to complain about life…

     "Ah!" he said with a sigh. "You ask how I'm getting on. How are we getting on here? We just are. We're getting older, fatter, letting ourselves go. Day and night, the days slip by, life passes dimly, without impressions, without thoughts… During the day it's all about making money, and in the evening it's the club, a crowd of card players, drunks, and loudmouths I can't stand. What's good about any of it?"

     "But you have your work, a noble purpose in life. You used to love talking about your hospital. I was rather strange back then, imagining myself a great pianist. Now every young lady plays the piano, and I played like all the rest, and there was nothing special about me; I'm as much a pianist as Mama is a writer. And of course I didn't understand you then, but afterward, in Moscow, I thought about you often. You were all I thought about. What happiness it must be to be a district doctor, to help those who suffer, to serve the people. What happiness!" Ekaterina Ivanovna repeated with enthusiasm. "When I thought of you in Moscow, you seemed to me so ideal, so elevated…"

     Startsev remembered the banknotes he used to pull from his pockets each evening with such pleasure, and the flame in his soul went out.

     He stood up to go back to the house. She took his arm.

     "You are the best person I have ever known in my life," she continued. "We will see each other, we will talk, won't we? Promise me. I am not a pianist, I no longer have any illusions about myself, and I won't play for you or talk about music."

     When they went inside and Startsev saw her face in the evening light and her sad, grateful, searching eyes turned toward him, he felt uneasy and thought again:

     "It's a good thing I didn't marry her back then."

     He began to say his goodbyes.

     "You have absolutely no Roman right to leave without supper," said Ivan Petrovich, seeing him out. "That is highly perpendicular of you. Come on now, do your bit!" he said, turning to Pava in the hallway.

     Pava, no longer a boy but a young man with a mustache, struck a pose, raised his hand, and said in a tragic voice:

     "Die, wretched woman!"

     All of this irritated Startsev. Settling into the carriage and looking at the dark house and garden that had once been so dear and precious to him, he recalled everything at once — Vera Iosifovna's novels, Kotik's thunderous playing, Ivan Petrovich's wit, Pava's tragic pose — and thought that if the most talented people in the whole town were so utterly without talent, then what must the town itself be like.

     Three days later Pava brought a letter from Ekaterina Ivanovna.

     "You are not coming to see us. Why?" she wrote. "I am afraid you have changed toward us; I am afraid, and the very thought of it frightens me. Put my mind at ease, come and tell me that everything is all right.

     I must talk with you. Yours, E. T."

     He read the letter, thought it over, and said to Pava:

     "Tell them, my friend, that I can't come today, I'm very busy. I'll come, tell them, in about three days."

     But three days passed, then a week, and still he did not go. Once, driving past the Turkins' house, he remembered that he ought to stop in for at least a minute, but he thought it over… and did not stop.

     And he never visited the Turkins again.

     

V

     

     Several more years passed. Startsev had grown even heavier, had put on fat, breathed with difficulty, and now walked with his head thrown back. When he rode by, puffy and red, on his troika with its bells, and Panteleimon,also plump and red, with a fleshy nape, sits on the box seat with his arms stretched straight out before him, stiff as wood, shouting at oncoming traffic "Keep to the rrrright!" — the sight is imposing, and it seems that what is riding past is not a man but a pagan god. He has an enormous practice in town, no time to catch his breath, and he already owns an estate and two houses in the city, and he is eyeing a third, more profitable one, and when someone at the Mutual Credit Society mentions a house going up for auction, he walks into it without ceremony and, passing through all the rooms without paying any attention to the undressed women and children who stare at him in amazement and fear, he pokes his stick at every door and says:

     — Is this the study? Is this the bedroom? And what's in here?

     And all the while he breathes heavily and wipes the sweat from his brow.

     He has a great deal of trouble on his hands, yet he does not give up his zemstvo post; greed has gotten the better of him, he wants to be everywhere at once. In Dyalizh and in town he is now simply called Ionych. — "Where is Ionych off to?" or: "Shouldn't we invite Ionych to the consultation?"

     Probably because his throat has grown fat, his voice has changed, become thin and sharp. His character has changed too: he has grown difficult and irritable. When seeing patients, he is usually cross, impatiently taps his stick on the floor, and shouts in his unpleasant voice:

     — Kindly answer only the questions asked! No talking!

     He is alone. Life bores him, nothing interests him.

     During all the time he has lived in Dyalizh, his love for Kotik was his only joy and, in all likelihood, his last. In the evenings he plays vint at the club and then sits alone at a large table and has supper. He is waited on by the footman Ivan, the oldest and most respected one, and he is served Lafite No. 17, and by now everyone — the club elders, the cook, and the footman — knows what he likes and what he does not, and they do their utmost to please him, for otherwise, God forbid, he might suddenly lose his temper and start banging his stick on the floor.

     While dining, he occasionally turns around and joins in some conversation:

     — What are you talking about? Eh? Who?

     And when it happens that at a nearby table someone brings up the Turkins, he asks:

     — Which Turkins do you mean? The ones whose daughter plays the piano?

     And that is all there is to say about him.

     And the Turkins? Ivan Petrovich has not aged, has not changed in the least, and still jokes and tells anecdotes as before; Vera Iosifovna still reads her novels to guests willingly, with warm simplicity. And Kotik plays the piano every day, for about four hours at a time. She has visibly aged, is often unwell, and every autumn goes to the Crimea with her mother. Seeing them off at the station, Ivan Petrovich, when the train begins to move, wipes away tears and shouts:

     — Goodbye, please!

     

 

The Upheaval

     

     Mashenka Pavletskaya, a young girl who had only just finished her studies at the institute, returning from a walk to the Pushkins' house where she lived as a governess, found an extraordinary upheaval. The doorman Mikhaylo, who let her in, was agitated and red as a lobster.

     Noise was coming from upstairs.

     "Probably the mistress is having a fit…" thought Mashenka, "or she has quarreled with her husband…"

     In the entrance hall and the corridor she came across the housemaids. One of the maids was crying. Then Mashenka saw the master of the house himself, Nikolai Sergeich, a small, not yet old man with a bloated face and a large bald patch, come running out of her room. He was flushed. He was twitching… Not noticing the governess, he walked past her and, raising his hands in the air, exclaimed:

     — Oh, how dreadful this is! How tactless! How stupid, savage! Disgusting!

     Mashenka went into her room, and there for the first time in her life she had to experience in all its sharpness the feeling so familiar to people who are dependent, defenseless, living on the charity of the wealthy and the well-born. Her room was being searched. The mistress Fedosya Vasilyevna, a stout, broad-shouldered lady with thick black eyebrows, her hair loose and her manner angular, with a barely visible mustache and red hands, her face and ways resembling those of a simple peasant cook, stood at her table putting back into the work bag balls of wool, scraps of fabric, and papers… Evidently the governess's appearance was unexpected to her, for,turning around and seeing her pale, astonished face, she grew slightly flustered and muttered:

     – Pardon,1 I… I accidentally knocked it over… caught it with my sleeve…

     And, saying something else, Madame Kushkina rustled her train and left. Mashenka looked around her room with bewildered eyes and, understanding nothing, not knowing what to think, shrugged her shoulders and went cold with fear… What had Fedosya Vasilyevna been looking for in her bag? If it really was, as she said, an accident — that she had caught it with her sleeve and knocked it over — then why had Nikolai Sergeich rushed out of the room looking so flushed and agitated? Why was one of the desk drawers slightly open? The piggy bank where the governess kept her ten-kopek coins and old stamps had been unlocked. It had been opened, but they hadn't managed to lock it again, though they had scratched up the entire lock. The bookshelf, the surface of the desk, the bed — everything bore fresh signs of a search. The laundry basket too. The laundry had been folded neatly, but not in the order Mashenka had left it when she went out. So it had been a real search, a genuine one — but what for, why? What had happened? Mashenka recalled the doorman's agitation, the commotion that was still going on, the tearful maid; could all of this be connected to the search that had just been carried out in her room? Was she somehow mixed up in some terrible affair? Mashenka turned pale and sank, cold all over, onto the laundry basket.

     The maid came into the room.

     – Liza, do you know why they… searched my room? – the governess asked her.

     – The mistress's brooch went missing — two thousand rubles… – said Liza.

     – Yes, but why search me?

     – They searched everyone, miss… They searched me too, all over… They made us all undress completely and searched us… And I, miss, I swear before God… I never so much as touched their brooch, I never even went near the dressing table. I'll say the same thing to the police.

     – But… why search me? – the governess continued, bewildered.

     – The brooch was stolen, I'm telling you… The mistress went through everything with her own hands. She even searched the doorman Mikhail herself. It's a pure disgrace! Nikolai Sergeich just stands there clucking like a hen. And you, miss, there's no need to tremble like that. They didn't find anything on you. If you didn't take the brooch, then you've got nothing to be afraid of.

     – But Liza, this is… degrading… insulting! – said Mashenka, breathless with indignation. – It's despicable, it's low! What right did she have to suspect me and rummage through my things?

     – You live among strangers, miss, – Liza sighed. – Even if you are a young lady, still… it's almost like being a servant… It's not the same as living with your own mother and father…
