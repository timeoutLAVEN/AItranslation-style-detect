Paragraph 1: Preface to 'Border Town'

　　There is an unspeakable tenderness for farmers and soldiers—this feeling can be seen everywhere in all my works. I never hide this little feeling. I grew up in the small towns described in the work. My grandfather, father, and brothers were all registered in the military: those who died all died in their posts, and those who did not inevitably spent their entire lives in service. From the perspective of the world I have encountered, I will narrate their loves and hates, sorrows and joys, whether the pen is clumsy or not stray too far from the topic. Because they are upright and honest, some aspects of life are extremely great, some are extremely ordinary, some aspects are extremely beautiful, and some aspects are extremely trivial. When I write about them,—— to make them more human and approachable, I naturally write honestly. But because of this, the work inevitably becomes a useless endeavor.

　　According to current trends, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are very likely to develop unpleasant feelings toward such works. The former claims they are "not outdated," telling people that China does not need such works, while the latter is "too worried about falling behind" and currently unwilling to read such works. This is certainly true. What does "falling behind" mean? A somewhat rational person may never understand, but who among most people isn't afraid of being "left behind"? I have something I want to say: "This book is not written for this majority." Those who have read three to five Western-style books on literary theory and criticism, or have also read a large number of classical and modern world masterpieces, often have life experiences that often do not allow them to know a bit about another place in China or something different beyond being "erudite." Therefore, even if a work aligns with a certain literary theory, critics often praise it in various ways, but such criticism inevitably becomes an insult to the author. They neither wish to understand the true loves and hates, sorrows and joys of this people, nor can they explain the merits or faults of this work,—— this book was not written for them. As for art lovers, they are either university students or middle school students, living in densely populated cities in China, and often honestly and innocently devote part of their precious time to reading newly published literary books in China. They work together to create a habit governed by certain theorists, critics, clever publishers, and literary informants accustomed to lying and spreading rumors, whose lives are far removed from the world described in this work. They don't need such works, so the book doesn't want them to have it. Theorists have literary theories from publications from various countries to verify, so they have nothing to say; critics have writers and works they owe a bit of grievances, enough to praise and criticize them for a lifetime. Most readers, regardless of their interests or beliefs, have works to read. Precisely because we care about the general public, aren't there many people, supposedly for the general public, who are always like spinning tops there transforming? The publication of this book, whether or not abandoned by the theorists and critics who lead the majority, nor by the majority of readers who are led, has not completely abandoned it, but the author of this book has long been determined to abandon this "majority."

　　This book is only intended for those who have already left school, or have never been able to approach it, who still know some Chinese characters, and who live in positions that literary theory, criticism, and lies cannot reach, who care deeply about all the advantages and disadvantages of the entire nation in space and time, for reading. They truly know what rural areas are like today, want to know what rural areas were like in the past, and they are certainly willing to learn from this book a little bit about the countryside and soldiers in the world. The world I write about, even if they are completely unfamiliar to them, their tolerance, their passion for seeking comfort and knowledge from a book, will surely allow them to read it with ease. I do not stop there; I also prepare to give them a chance to contrast. In another work, I will mention the civil war of twenty years, causing some of the farmers who bear the brunt to lose their original simplicity, diligence, peace, and integrity, and what new things they have become. They suffered from heavy taxation, opium poisoning, and opium addiction, turning into poverty and laziness! I will simply narrate the worries of some ordinary people amid change as this nation is led by history toward an unknown fate, as well as the ideas and desires of "survival" and "how to survive" arising from malnutrition. My readers should be rational, and this rationality is based on concern for the changes in Chinese society, recognizing the greatness of the past and the current decline of this nation, each quietly engaged in the great cause of national rejuvenation. This work may only give them a touch of nostalgic nostalgia, a bitter smile, or a nightmare, but at the same time, it might still give them courage and confidence!

　　Traveling from Sichuan through Hunan, there is an official road to the east. When this official road neared the border of western Hunan and reached a small mountain town called "Chadong," there was a small stream, by which stood a white pagoda, beneath which lived a solitary household. This family only has an elderly man, a girl, and a yellow dog.

　　The small stream flows downstream, winding around the mountains, and after about three li, joins the great river of Chadong. If one crosses the creek and crosses the small hill, it takes only one li to reach the edge of Chadong City. The stream flows like the back of a bow, the mountain path is like a bowstring, so there is a slight difference in distance. The stream is about twenty zhang wide, and the riverbed is made of large stones. The still water is so deep it cannot reach the bottom of a pole, yet it remains crystal clear, and the fish swimming in the river can be counted in number. Since the creek was a vital passage between Sichuan and Hunan, the water often rose and fell. Due to financial constraints, it was impossible to build a bridge, so a square-head ferry was arranged. This ferry can carry about twenty passengers at a time, with many people crossing the river, and when the number is large, it can go back and forth repeatedly. At the bow of the ferry, a small bamboo pole was erected with a movable iron ring hanging from it. At both ends of the stream, water troughs held abandoned cables. When people crossed, they hung the iron rings on the old cables, and the boatmen would reach out to climb the rope, slowly pulling the boat across to the opposite bank. As the boat neared the shore, the ferry manager shouted, "Slow down, slow down!" and leapt ashore himself, pulling the iron rings. So the people, goods, cattle, and horses all came ashore, crossed the hill, and disappeared. The ferry crossing was state-owned, so the crossover workers did not have to pay for it. Feeling uneasy, someone grabbed a handful of money and threw it onto the boat's deck. The ferry handler picked it up one by one and still stuffed it into the man's palm, looking as serious as when he argued: "I've got the tolerance to talk, three dou of rice, seven hundred coins, that's enough." Who wants this! ”

　　But it didn't work. Everyone wanted to have peace of mind. Who would be embarrassed to give their strength without being paid? No matter what, someone would still give the money. But the boatman couldn't bear it, and for peace of mind, he sent the money to the tea cave to buy tea and tobacco. He hung bundles of the finest tobacco from the village around his belt, generously giving them to anyone who needed them. Sometimes, judging by the distant passerby's attention to the tobacco nearby, he would stick a small bundle of tobacco onto the person's bundle and say, "Aren't you going to smoke this? This is good, this is wonderful, tastes quite good, and it's perfect for gifting!" "The tea leaves are put into large vats, brewed with boiling water in June to quench the thirst of passersby.

Having lived for seventy years, I have been guarding this creek since I was twenty, and over fifty years, I have ferried countless people by boat. Though he was so old, He should have rested, but heaven forbade him; it seemed he could not leave this part of life. He never thought about what his position meant for himself, but simply lived quietly and faithfully there. He replaced the sky, allowing him to feel the power of life when the sun rose, and when the sun set, he did not think that the one who died with the sun was the girl who stayed by his side. His only friends are a ferry and a yellow dog, and his only family is that girl.

　　The girl's mother, the only daughter of the old boatman, secretly had an ambiguous relationship with the same Chadong soldier fifteen years ago behind the backs of her loyal father. After having a child, the garrison soldier wanted to arrange an escape downstream with her. But judging from the escape, one violated the soldier's duty, while the other had to leave his lonely father. After some consideration, seeing that she lacked the courage to leave and he didn't want to ruin her reputation as a soldier, the soldier thought: Since we cannot reunite in life together, and if we die together, no one can stop us. First, I took poison. The woman, however, cared about a piece of flesh in her belly, couldn't bear it, and had no way to assert herself. The matter had already been known to his father, who was a ferryman, but he didn't add a meaningful word, pretending not to have heard of it and continued to live his life peacefully. The daughter, feeling both ashamed and compassionate, stayed by her father's side. After the child was born, she went to the stream and drank a lot of cold water to die. In an almost miraculous moment, this orphan had actually grown into an adult—in the blink of an eye, he was thirteen. To find a place where the two mountains grow thick with bamboo and the overwhelming green hues, the old boatman casually picked up a close name for this poor orphaned chick, calling it "Cuicui."

　　Cuicui has been nurtured in the wind and sun, turning her skin dark, with green mountains and clear waters everywhere, her eyes clear and bright as crystal. Nature raised and educated her, being innocent and lively, always like a little beast. And so well-behaved, like the yellow muntjac on the mountain, never thinking of cruelty, never worrying, never getting angry. Whenever strangers on the ferry noticed her, she would stare at them with bright eyes, acting as if she could step into the deep mountains at any moment. But once she understood that people are indifferent, she would calmly play by the water's edge.

　　The old boatman waits at the bow, rain or shine. When someone was crossing, they would bend slightly and pull the bamboo cable with both hands, steering the boat across the stream. Sometimes, when tired, she would fall asleep lying on a large rock by the stream. When someone waved from across the river, Cuicui would not let her grandfather get up, so she would jump off the boat and nimbly help him cross the stream. Everything was done smoothly and without any mistakes. Sometimes, he would be on the boat with his grandfather's yellow dog, and during the transition, they would fight together. As the boat neared the shore and Grandfather was calling out to the guest, "Slow down, slow down," the yellow dog would grab the rope in its mouth and leap aboard first, clearly knowing how to fulfill its duties, tightly gripping the rope and pulling the boat to shore.

　　On a gentle and gentle day, with no one to cross over, the days were leisurely, so Grandfather and Cuicui would sit on the big rock in front of the door to bask in the sun. Or throw a piece of wood from a height into the water, encouraging the yellow dog nearby to leap down from the rocks and bring the wood back. Or Cuicui and the yellow dog would open their ears, listening to their grandfather tell stories of wars in the city from years ago. Or Grandfather and Cuicui, each playing a vertical flute made of small bamboo, playing a tune for sending off a bride. The transitional man arrived. 　　In Chadong, the city was built by the water and mountains; on the side near the mountain, the city walls stretched like a long snake, climbing along the mountain. On the waterside outside the city, a dock was set up by the river outside the city, where small sailboats could be moored. When the boat goes downstream, tung oil, green salt, and dyed pests are transported. On the upstream route, cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, and miscellaneous goods were transported, along with seafood. Running through all the docks, there was a river street. Most of the houses were landed and half were on the water, and because of limited space, all those houses had stilted houses. The river rises in spring, and as the water gradually enters the street, the families on the street use long ladders—one end set up under the eaves, the other on the city wall. Everyone curses and shouts, carrying bundles, bedding, and rice jars, and enters the city through the ladders. Only when the water recedes does they exit through the city gate. If the water came in especially strong one year, one or two stilted houses along the river would be swept away by the flood, and everyone would stand atop the city walls watching in silence. Those who suffered losses also stared blankly, seemingly speechless about the losses, much like witnessing other irreparable misfortunes under nature's arrangement. When the water rises, one can still see the suddenly widening river surface from the city walls, flowing mightily, accompanied by houses, cattle, sheep, and large trees rising and falling from the upper reaches of the mountains and waters. So, in areas where the water was slower, in front of the customs barge, people often rowed small sampans. Seeing a livestock, a small piece of wood, or an empty boat floating in the middle of the river, or a woman or child crying on board, they would hurriedly row the boat, and downstream they would tie it to the target with a long rope and row toward the shore. These honest and brave people also love profit and are loyal, much like ordinary locals. Regardless of saving lives or objects, he also acts swiftly and bravely in a joyful and adventurous act, earning applause from those who see him.

　　That river is the historically famous You River, now known as the Bai River. After the lower reaches of the Bai River reach Chenzhou and merge with the Yuan River, it becomes somewhat turbid, with the meaning of spring water emerging from the mountains. If you go upstream, the deep pools three to five zhang deep are crystal clear. The deep pool is bathed in the sunlight, with tiny white stones at the riverbed and patterned agate stones—all clearly visible. Fish swim in the water, as if floating in the air. Both banks are surrounded by high mountains, and within the mountains are fine bamboo used for papermaking, which year-round produces a deep green color that captivates the eye. Many families near the water are surrounded by peach and apricot blossoms. In spring, just be careful: wherever there are peach blossoms, there will be homes, and wherever there are homes, wine can be sold. In summer, the purple floral cloth clothes and pants that shine under the sunlight can serve as flags for people's residences. In autumn and winter, houses on cliffs and by the water come into view. The yellow clay walls and jet-black tiles are always perfectly positioned, harmonizing perfectly with the surrounding environment, leaving a very pleasant impression. A traveler with even a slight interest in poetry and painting, curled up on a small boat on this small river, on a thirty-day journey, will surely not feel bored, because miracles are everywhere—natural boldness and ingenuity—each part captivates and captivates.

But within the Hunan border, Chadong was the last water wharf. Although the riverbed was about half a li wide in Chadong, when the water receded in autumn and winter, the riverbed was less than twenty zhang deep, with the rest just a pile of bluestone. After the small boats arrived, they had no way to sail upstream, so all import and export goods from eastern Sichuan had to land and land from this place. On winter daylight, when you go to the city, you see clothes and vegetables drying in front of every household. Sweet potatoes are often hung under the eaves with vines. Sometimes, some men would take up the threshold in front of their own houses, sawing wood or chopping trees with axes, and stack the chopped firewood into open fields like pagodas. Everything is always so silent; every person and every day passes in this pure solitude. A bit of quietness increases one's ability to think about "human affairs" and increases dreams. Those living in this small town must each have their own destined days, harboring expectations about the inevitable likes and dislikes of human affairs. But what are these people thinking? Who knows. Living on higher ground in the city, one stop in front of the door offers views of the opposite river and the scenery within. When the boat arrives, children's imaginations are on the side of those pulling the boat. Although those who come here are talking about business on boats, there are generally certain rules for boats and tow pullers, so there's no need to discuss numbers. Or leaning against the riverside window watching sailors lay out goods and listening to sailors climb masts and sing. Most prostitutes relied on Sichuan merchants for their livelihoods, but the bonds of kindness were mostly formed by sailors. 　　The person at Zhangshui Wharf was named Shunshun, a man who had worked in the military during the Qing dynasty and served as a squad leader in the famous 49th Regiment during the revolution. Among fellow squad leaders, some became great figures or famous figures because of the revolution, some were executed and dismembered. Yet, he brought the pain from his childhood and joyful events, so he returned to his hometown. With the little money he had saved, he bought a six-oared white wooden boat and rented it to a poor boatman, loading cargo between Chatong and Chenzhou on his behalf. With good fortune, nothing bad happened within half a year, so he used the money he earned to marry a young widow with some property and a pale, black-haired young woman. Years later, on this river, he had four boats of various sizes, a shop, and two sons.

　　But this generous and free-spirited man, though his career went smoothly, was so eager to make friends and generous that he could help others in urgent need, so he couldn't become as energetic as the oil merchants. Having lived on a grain farm, he understood the hardships of travelers and the feelings of those in distress. On one hand, he made money from the water, while on the other, he drifted away carefreely. Although this person has a minor foot ailment, he can still swim; His walk was rarely smooth, yet his conduct was so fair and impartial. Only those who use these customs and rules to arrange everything must be a senior and virtuous central figure. One autumn, the original deacon died, and Shunshun became the one who took his place. At that time, he was only fifty years old, a man of understanding and reason, upright and peaceful, and not greedy for wealth, so no one doubted his age.

　　Now, his son is eighteen years old and the youngest is sixteen. Both young men were as sturdy as little bulls, able to sail, swim, and walk long distances. Whatever young people from small towns and towns could do, they did and excelled at every task. The older ones, like their fathers, are bold and open-minded, unconstrained by the trivial details. 　　As the two brothers grew older, they needed to train them in various aspects of life, and as their father, he took turns sending the two children on various journeys. When sailing downstream, he often took his own boat as a companion, sharing hardships and joys with others. When paddling, I picked the heaviest one; when carrying the line, I pulled the head and the second string; I ate dried fish, chili, and stinky pickled vegetables, and slept on the hard gang deck. Ascending along the dry road, they followed the freight of eastern Sichuan, passing Xiushan and Longtan, and conducting business in Youyang. Regardless of the weather, rain, or snow, they always wore straw sandals and hurried along the way. He also carried a short knife, so if he had no choice but to act, he would quickly pull the knife out, stand in an open space, wait for the opponent, and then fight that person hand-to-hand. The gang's culture is that "you must use a knife to deal with enemies, and you must use a knife to connect friends," so when a knife is needed, they never let it miss that small chance. Learning trade, socializing, living in a new place, and learning to use knives to protect both body and reputation—the purpose of education seems to be to teach the two children courage and loyalty. As a result of their education, both became as strong as tigers, yet also friendly and affectionate, neither arrogant nor lazy, not flashy, and not bullying others. Therefore, when the three of them were mentioned on the border of Chadong, everyone showed respect for this name.

　　As a father, when his two sons are very young, he understands that the elder son is similar in every way to himself, yet he shows a bit of indulgence toward the second son. Because of this unconscious selfishness, he named his eldest son Tianbao and his second son Nuosong. This means that heaven blesses people with disagreements in human affairs, but as for what the Nuo god sends, according to local customs, people cannot take it lightly. Nuo Song was very beautiful, but the Chadong boatmen, too awkward to praise it, only knew to give him a nickname, "Yue Yun." Although few people had ever seen Yue Yun in person, their impression was that of the young actor Yue Yun on stage, which gave him a similar bearing. 3

　　At the border between the two provinces, those who have led local military affairs for over a decade have focused on pacification and preservation, handling matters properly, and no incidents have occurred. Water and land commerce was neither interrupted by war nor affected by banditry; everything was in perfect order, and the people lived contentedly. These people, apart from the loss of a cow at home, a capsizing, or other major fatal events that cause them to feel deeply saddened by a tragedy, it seems they will never feel the unfortunate struggles of other parts of China for the people of this border town.

　　The busiest days of the year in the border town are the Dragon Boat Festival, Mid-Autumn Festival, and the New Year. How the three festivals excited the locals thirty or fifty years ago has changed little to now, and they remain the most meaningful days for the local residents.

　　On Dragon Boat Festival, local women and children all wear new clothes, and on their foreheads are drawn with the character for 'king' dipped in realgar and wine. Anyone who comes to this day will surely be able to eat fish and meat. Around eleven in the morning, all the people of Chatong had lunch. After eating, those living in the city all locked their doors and left the city to watch the boats row. If you know someone on He Street, you can go to the entrance of the stilted houses and look around; otherwise, just stand at the customs gate and various docks to look. The river dragon boat starts somewhere in Changtan and ends before customs duties. Engaging in competitions and competitions. Because on this day, officers, tax collectors, and local officials with status all gathered in front of customs to watch the spectacle. Everyone had prepared for rowing several days in advance, dividing into groups and groups to select several strong, agile young men to practice advancing and retreating in the pool. The shape of the boat was very different from ordinary wooden boats; it was long and narrow, with both ends raised high, and the hulls painted with vermilion long lines. During the season, they were often left in dry caves by the river, and when needed, they were towed into the water. Each boat can carry twelve to eighteen rowers: one leader, one drummer, and one gong player. Each rower holds a short oar, slowing down to the beat of the drum to keep the rhythm, rowing the boat forward. 　　Three days later, the boat owner came to discuss bringing Cuicui to stay at her home, but Cuicui wanted to guard her grandfather's grave and was unwilling to enter the city immediately. She only asked the boat master to go through the city government office to say something, and Xu Yangmabing stayed with her for the time being. The boat captain agreed obediently and left.

　　Yang Ma Bing, being over fifty years old, was a storyteller better than Cuicui's grandfather. He was especially attentive in everything, diligent and clean, so living with Cuicui made her feel as if she had lost a grandfather but gained a new uncle. During the transition, someone asked about her poor grandfather, and at dusk, thinking of her grandfather made Cuicui's heart ache and she felt deeply desolate. But as these days of desolation last longer, they gradually faded. Every evening and evening, the two sat by the stream and on the high cliff in front of the door, reminiscing about the old story of her pitiful grandfather lying in the wet soil. There were many things Cuicui had never known before, which only softened her heart. He also spoke of Cuicui's father, a soldier who valued both love and reputation, and how he could win a girl's heart in the uniform of the Green Standard Army at the time. He also spoke of Cuicui's mother, who was so good at singing, and how popular the songs she sang were at the time.

　　Times have changed, and naturally, everything is different. The emperor no longer rules the throne, let alone ordinary people! Yang Mabing recalled when he was young as a groom, leading horses to Bixi Pass to sing to Cuicui's mother, but Cuicui's mother ignored him. Now, he had become the only support and trustee for this orphan, and couldn't help but smile bitterly.

　　Because every evening they would talk about her grandfather and the family's connections, and later they talked about everything before the old boatman died. Through this, Cuicui understood many things her grandfather had never mentioned during his lifetime. The two elders singing, Shunshun's eldest son's death, Shunshun and his son's coldness toward their grandfather, and the people of Zhongzhai using the mill as a dowry to tempt the elders to send them off. The elders both remember their brother's death, and because Cuicui ignores them, they are forced by their families to accept the mill, meaning they still want to ferry. So they act out of spite and go downward. How could the cause of their grandfather's death be related to Cuicui...... Everything Cuicui didn't understand, now she understood completely. After understanding the situation, Cuicui cried all night.

　　After forty-seven, the boat master sent someone to invite Ma Bing into the city, discussing bringing Cuicui to his home as his wife. But since the two elders are in Chenzhou, let's not bring up this matter for now. Instead, move across to He Street and live there. When they return, we'll see what they think. Ma Bing thought Cuicui should be asked about this. On the way back, after explaining Shunshun's intentions to Cuicui, she also made a suggestion for Cuicui, thinking that since her status was unsettled, it would be inappropriate to go to a stranger's home. It was better to wait at Bixi Pass, and wait until the elders returned by boat to see what they thought.

　　After deciding on this plan, the old horse soldier thought the two elders would return soon, so he still entrusted the horses to the camp attendants, keeping Cuicui company at Bixi Pass, living day by day.

　　The White Pagoda at Bixi Ridge is related to the feng shui of Chadong. If the pagoda collapses, it must be rebuilt. Besides the city management office, tax bureau, and merchants donating some money, people from major villages also donated money using booklets. The success of this tower is not for the benefit of just one person; everyone should do their utmost to accumulate virtue and benefit, and everyone has the opportunity to donate. Therefore, a large bamboo tube with joints at both ends was placed on the ferry, with a sawed end in the middle. Everyone could freely throw money in, and the bamboo tube was filled with cavalry and carried to the city's leader, along with another bamboo tube. When the transitional man saw the old boatman was gone and Cuicui had a white thread tied into her braid, he realized the old man had finished his assigned work and quietly lay down in the pit. He looked at Cuicui with sympathy while pulling out money and stuffing it into a bamboo tube. "Heaven bless you. Those who die go to the West, and those who live will always be safe." Cuicui understood what those donors meant, felt a pang in her heart, and hurriedly turned her back to pull the boat.

　　In winter, the collapsed White Pagoda was rebuilt. But the young man who sang under the moon, making Cuicui's soul gently float for the song in her sleep, had not yet returned to Chadong.
