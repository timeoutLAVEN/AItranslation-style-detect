Lady with a Dog

     

I

     

     They said that a new face appeared on the embankment: a lady with a dog. Dmitry Dmitrievich Gurov, who had already lived in Yalta for two weeks and was used to being here, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in Vernet's pavilion, he saw a young lady, a short blonde, in a beret, walk along the embankment: a white spitz ran behind her.

     And then he met her in the city garden and in the square several times a day. She walked alone, still in the same beret, with a white spitz; No one knew who she was, and they called her just like that: the lady with the dog.

     "If she is here without a husband and without acquaintances," Gurov thought, "then it would not be superfluous to get acquainted with her."

     He was not yet forty, but he already had a daughter of twelve years old and two sons who were high school students. He had been married early, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed one and a half times older than him. She was a tall woman, with dark eyebrows, straight, important, solid, and, as she called herself, thinking. She read a lot, did not write in her letters, called her husband not Dmitri, but Dimitri, and he secretly considered her narrow-minded, narrow, ingraceful, afraid of her and did not like to be at home. He began to cheat on her a long time ago, he cheated often, and probably because of this he spoke ill of women almost always, and when they spoke of them in his presence, he called them like this:

     "An inferior race!"

     It seemed to him that he had learned enough from bitter experience to call them what he liked, but still without the "inferior race" he could not have lived even two days. In the company of men he was bored, uncomfortable, with them he was taciturn, cold, but when he was among women he felt free and knew what to talk about with them and how to behave himself; and it was easy for him even to be silent with them. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature, there was something attractive, elusive, which endeared women to him, attracted them; He knew about it. and he himself was drawn to them by some force.

     Repeated experience, bitter experience indeed, had taught him long ago that any rapprochement, which at first so pleasantly diversifies life and seems like a pleasant and easy adventure, inevitably grows into a whole task, extremely difficult, and in the end the situation becomes burdensome. But every time I met an interesting woman again, this experience somehow slipped out of my memory, and I wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and funny.

     And then one evening he was dining in the garden, and a lady in a beret came slowly to take the next table. Her expression, her gait, her dress, her hairstyle told him that she was from decent society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here... There is a great deal of untruth in the stories of the impurity of the local customs, he despised them, and knew that such stories were mostly composed by people who would gladly sin themselves if they could, but when the lady sat down at the next table, three steps away from him, he remembered these stories of easy victories, of trips to the mountains, and the tempting thought of a speedy  a fleeting connection, about an affair with an unknown woman, whom you do not know by name and surname, suddenly took possession of him.

     He gently beckoned the spitz to him and, when it approached, wagged his finger at him. Spitz grumbled. Gurov threatened again.

     The lady looked at him and immediately lowered her eyes.

     "It doesn't bite," she said, and blushed.

     "Can I give him a bone?" And when she nodded her head affirmatively, he asked affably: "Have you been deigned to come to Yalta for a long time?"

     "Five days.

     "And I'm already here for two weeks.

     They were silent for a while.

     "Time passes quickly, and yet there is such boredom here!" she said, without looking at him.

     "It's only customary to say that it's boring here. An inhabitant lives somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra — and he is not bored, but comes here: "Oh, it's boring! Oh, dust!" You'd think he'd come from Grenada.

     She laughed. Then they both continued to eat in silence, as if they were strangers; But after dinner they walked side by side, and there began a joking, light conversation of people who were free and contented, who did not care where they went, what they talked about. They walked and talked about how strangely the sea was illuminated; The water was lilac in color, so soft and warm, and a golden streak ran across it from the moon. They talked about how stuffy it was after a hot day. Gurov said that he was a Muscovite, a philologist by education, but worked in a bank; he once prepared to sing in a private opera, but gave up, he has two houses in Moscow... And from her he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg, but had married in S., where she had been living for two years, and that she would stay in Yalta for another month, and that perhaps her husband, who also wanted to rest, would come for her. She could not explain where her husband served, in the provincial government or in the provincial zemstvo council, and this was ridiculous to her. And Gurov also found out that her name was Anna Sergeevna.

     Then in his room he thought about her, about the fact that tomorrow she would probably meet him. This is how it should be. As he went to bed, he remembered that she had been a student at the institute so recently, that she had studied just as his daughter was now, that there was still so much hesitation and angularity in her laughter, in her conversation with a stranger—it must have been the first time in her life that she had been alone, in such an environment when people followed her, and looked at her, and talked to her only for one secret purpose.  which she cannot help but guess. He remembered her thin, weak neck, beautiful, gray eyes.

     "There's something pitiful about her, after all," he thought, and began to fall asleep.

     

II

     

     A week has passed since we met. It was a holiday. The rooms were stuffy, and the streets were filled with dust and hats. He was thirsty all day, and Gurov often went into the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeevna either water with syrup or ice cream. There was nowhere to go.

     In the evening, when it calmed down a little, they went to the pier to see how the steamer would come. There were many walkers on the pier; We were going to meet someone, holding bouquets. And here two features of the elegant Yalta crowd were clearly striking: the elderly ladies were dressed like young men, and there were many generals.

     On account of the rough sea, the steamer arrived late, when the sun had already set, and turned for a long time before landing at the pier. Anna Sergeyevna looked through her lorgnette at the steamer and at the passengers, as if looking for acquaintances, and when she turned to Gurov, her eyes sparkled. She talked a lot, and her questions were abrupt, and she herself forgot at once what she was asking; Then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

     The well-dressed crowd dispersed, there were no faces to be seen, the wind had died down completely, and Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood as if waiting to see if anyone else would get off the steamer. Anna Sergeevna was already silent and sniffed the flowers, not looking at Gurov.

     "The weather has improved in the evening," he said. "Where are we going now?" Shall we go somewhere?

     She didn't answer.

     Then he looked at her intently, and suddenly embraced her and kissed her on the lips, and the smell and moisture of the flowers washed over him, and at once he looked around fearfully to see if anyone had seen it.

     "Let's go to you," he said quietly.

     And both walked quickly.

     Her room was stuffy, smelling of perfume that she bought in a Japanese store. Gurov, looking at her now, thought: "There are so many meetings in life!" and of those, such as his wife, who loved without sincerity, with superfluous talk, in a mannered manner, with hysteria, with an expression as if it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of two or three very handsome, cold women, who suddenly had a predatory expression on their faces, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it could give, and they were not of the first youth, capricious, unreasoning, domineering, not clever women, and when Gurov grew cold towards them, their beauty aroused hatred in him, and the lace on their linen seemed to him then like scales.

     But there is still the same lack of courage, the angularity of inexperienced youth, an awkward feeling; And there was an impression of confusion, as if someone suddenly knocked on the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the dog," took what had happened in a peculiar way, very seriously, as if she had fallen—so it seemed, and it was strange and inopportune. Her features drooped and withered, and her long hair hung sadly on the sides of her face, and she pondered in a dejected posture, like a sinner in an old picture.

     "Not good," she said. "You're the first to disrespect me now.

     There was a watermelon on the table in the room. Gurov cut off a slice for himself and began to eat slowly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

     Anna Sergeevna was touching, she breathed the purity of a decent, naïve, little-lived woman; A lonely candle burning on the table scarcely illuminated her face, but it was evident that she was not well in her heart.

     "Why should I stop respecting you?" Gurov asked. "You don't know what you're saying.

     "May God forgive me!" she said, and her eyes filled with tears. "It's terrible.

     "You're definitely making excuses.

     "How can I justify myself?" I am a bad, low woman, I despise myself and do not think about justification. I did not deceive my husband, but myself. And not only now, but for a long time now. My husband may be an honest, good man, but he is a lackey! I don't know what he does there, how he serves, but I only know that he's a lackey. When I married him, I was twenty years old, I was curious, I wanted something better; After all, there is, I said to myself, another life. I wanted to live! To live and to live... Curiosity burned me... You don't understand it, but I swear to God I couldn't control myself anymore, something was done to me, I couldn't be restrained, I told my husband that I was sick and came here... And here she walked as if in a frenzy, like a madwoman... And so I became a vulgar, wretched woman, whom anyone can despise.

     Gurov was already bored with listening, he was irritated by the naïve tone, this repentance, so unexpected and inappropriate; If it weren't for the tears in her eyes, one would think that she was joking or playing a role.

     "I don't understand," he said quietly, "what do you want?"

     She hid her face on his chest and clung to him.

     "Believe, believe me, I beg you," she said. "I love an honest, pure life, and sin is disgusting to me, I don't know what I'm doing. Ordinary people say: the unclean has confused. And now I can say of myself that I have been confused by the unclean.

     "Enough, enough," he muttered.

     He looked into her fixed, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke softly and affectionately, and she gradually calmed down, and gaiety returned to her; they both began to laugh.

     Then, when they came out, there was not a soul on the quay, the city with its cypress trees had a completely dead look, but the sea was still noisy and beating against the shore; One longboat was rocking on the waves, and a flashlight flickered sleepily on it.

     We found a cab and drove to Oreanda.

     "I just found out your name downstairs in the hall: it says von Diederitz on the board," said Gurov. "Is your husband German?"

     - No, I think his grandfather was German, but he himself is Orthodox.

     In Oreanda, they sat on a pew, not far from the church, looked down at the sea and were silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning fog, white clouds stood motionless on the tops of the mountains. The leaves did not stir in the trees, the cicadas screamed, and the monotonous, dull sound of the sea, coming from below, spoke of peace, of eternal sleep that awaits us. It was so noisy below, when there was neither Yalta nor Oreanda here, now it is noisy and will be just as indifferent and deaf when we are gone. And in this constancy, in complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, there is perhaps the guarantee of our eternal salvation, the uninterrupted movement of life on earth, uninterrupted perfection. Sitting beside the young woman, who at dawn seemed so beautiful, soothed and fascinated by the sight of this fabulous setting—the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the wide sky—Gurov thought about how, in fact, if you think about it, everything in this world is beautiful, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget the higher goals of existence, our human dignity.

     

     A man came up—a watchman must have been—looked at them and walked away. And this detail seemed so mysterious and also beautiful. It was seen how the steamer came from Feodosia, illuminated by the morning dawn, already without lights.

     "Dew on the grass," said Anna Sergeevna after a silence.

     — Yes. It's time to go home.

     They returned to the city.

     Then every afternoon they met on the embankment, had breakfast together, had lunch, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept badly and that her heart was beating anxiously, and she asked the same questions, agitated now by jealousy, now by fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or garden, when there was no one near them, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Perfect idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with a glance and fear, lest anyone see, the heat, the smell of the sea, and the constant flickering before the eyes of idle, well-dressed, well-fed people seemed to have reborn him; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how seductive she was, he was impatiently passionate, he did not leave her for a single step, and she often thought about her and kept asking him to confess that he did not respect her, did not love her in the least, but only saw in her a vulgar woman. Almost every evening later they went somewhere out of town, to Oreanda or to the waterfalls; And the walk was a success, the impressions were invariably beautiful and majestic every time.

     We were waiting for my husband to come. But a letter came from him, in which he announced that his eyes ached, and begged his wife to return home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeevna hurried up.

     "It's good that I'm going away," she said to Gurov. "It's fate itself.

     She rode on horseback, and he accompanied her. We drove all day. When she got into the carriage of the courier train and when the second bell rang, she said:

     "Let me look at you again..." I'll take a look again. That's it.

     She did not cry, but she was sad as if she were sick, and her face trembled.

     "I'll think of you. remember," she said. "The Lord is with you, stay." Do not mention dashingly. We say goodbye forever, it is so necessary, because we should not have met at all. Well, God be with you.

     The train left quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and in a minute there was no more noise, as if everything had been conspired to put an end to this sweet oblivion, this madness. And, left alone on the platform and looking into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the cry of grasshoppers and the hum of telegraph wires with a feeling as if he had just woken up. And he thought that there had been another adventure in his life, and that it too had already ended, and now there was a memory. He was touched, sad, and slightly repentant; for this young woman, whom he would never see again, was not happy with him; He was affable and cordial to her, but still in his manners, in his tone and caresses, there was a shadow of a slight mockery, a coarse arrogance of a happy man, who was also almost twice her age. All the time she called him kind, extraordinary, sublime; Obviously, he did not seem to her what he really was, which means that he was unwittingly deceiving her...

     Here at the station it already smelled of autumn, the evening was cool.

     "It's time for me to go north," thought Gurov, leaving the platform. "It's time!"

     

III

     

     At home in Moscow, everything was already wintery, stoves were heated, and in the mornings, when the children were going to the gymnasium and drinking tea, it was dark, and the nanny lit the fire for a while. Frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sleigh riding, it is pleasant to see the white earth, white roofs, it breathes softly, gloriously, and at this time I remember my youth. The old lindens and birches, white with frost, have a good-natured expression, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms, and near them you no longer want to think about the mountains and the sea.

     Gurov was a Muscovite, he returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and walked around Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the bells ring, the recent trip and the places he had been to had lost all charm for him. Little by little, he plunged into Moscow life, already avidly read three newspapers a day and said that he did not read Moscow newspapers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties, anniversaries, and he was already flattered that he had famous lawyers and artists and that in the doctor's club he played cards with the professor. He could already eat a whole portion of peasant in a frying pan...

     A month or so would pass, and Anna Sergeyevna, it seemed to him, would be covered in a fog in his memory, and only occasionally would he dream of her with a touching smile, as others had dreamed. But more than a month passed, deep winter came, and everything was clear in his memory, as if he had parted with Anna Sergeevna only yesterday. And the memories flared up more and more. Whether in the silence of the evening the voices of the children preparing their homework could be heard in his study, whether he had heard a romance or an organ in the restaurant, or the howling of a snowstorm in the fireplace, everything suddenly revived in his memory: what had happened on the pier, and the early morning with the fog on the mountains, and the steamer from Feodosia, and the kisses. He walked up and down the room for a long time, remembering and smiling, and then the memories turned into dreams, and what had passed in his imagination mingled with what was to come. Anna Sergeevna did not dream of him, but followed him everywhere like a shadow and followed him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if she were alive, and she seemed prettier, younger, more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed to himself better than he had been then, in Yalta. In the evenings she looked at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the gentle rustle of her clothes. On the street, he followed the women with his eyes, looking for someone like her...

     And there was already a strong desire to share his memories with someone. But at home it was impossible to talk about one's love, and outside the house there was no one to talk to. Not with tenants and not in the bank. And what to talk about? Did he love then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relations with Anna Sergeyevna? And he had to talk vaguely about love, about women, and no one guessed what was the matter, and only the wife moved her dark eyebrows and said:

     "For you, Dimitri, the role of a veil does not suit you at all.

     One night, as he was leaving the doctors' club with his partner, an official, he could not resist saying:

     "If you only knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!

     The official got into the sleigh and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

     "Dmitri Dmitrich!"

     "What?"

     "And you were right just now: the sturgeon has a smell!"

These words, so ordinary, for some reason suddenly outraged Gurov, seemed to him humiliating, unclean. What savage manners, what faces! What senseless nights, what uninteresting, inconspicuous days! Frantic card games, gluttony, drunkenness, constant conversations, all about the same thing. Useless affairs and conversations all about one thing seize the best part of the time, the best strength, and in the end there is a kind of meager, wingless life, some nonsense, and it is impossible to leave and escape, as if you were sitting in an insane asylum or in a company of prisoners!

     Gurov did not sleep all night and was indignant, and then spent the whole day with a headache. And the following nights he slept badly, sat up in bed and thought or walked from corner to corner. He was tired of the children, tired of the bank, he did not want to go anywhere, to talk about anything.

     In December, during the holidays, he got ready for the journey and told his wife that he was going to Petersburg to take care of a young man, and went to S. Why? He himself did not know well. He wanted to see Anna Sergeyevna and talk, arrange a date, if possible.

     He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the whole floor was covered with gray soldier's cloth and there was an inkwell on the table, gray with dust, with a rider on horseback, whose hand with a hat was raised, and his head was broken off. The porter gave him the necessary information: von Diederitz lived in Staro-Goncharnaya Street, in his own house, not far from the hotel, lived well, richly, had his own horses, and was known to everyone in the city. The porter pronounced it like this: Drydyrits.

     Gurov slowly went to Staro-Goncharnaya and found the house. Just opposite the house there was a fence, gray, long, with nails.

     "You can run away from such a fence," thought Gurov, glancing now at the windows, now at the fence.

     He realized: today was an unofficial day, and her husband was probably at home. Anyway, it would be tactless to enter the house and embarrass him. If you send a note, then it will probably fall into the hands of your husband, and then everything can be spoiled. It is best to rely on chance. And he kept walking down the street and waiting for this opportunity near the fence. He had seen a beggar enter the gate and be attacked by dogs, and then, an hour later, he had heard the piano playing, and the sounds were faint and indistinct. Anna Sergeevna must have played. The front door suddenly opened, and an old woman stepped out, followed by a familiar white spitz. Gurov wanted to call the dog, but his heart suddenly began to beat, and he could not remember the name of the spitz from excitement.

     He walked around and hated the grey fence more and more, and already thought with irritation that Anna Sergeyevna had forgotten about it and was perhaps already having fun with someone else, and this was so natural in the position of a young woman who was forced to see this accursed fence from morning to evening. He returned to his room and sat on the sofa for a long time, not knowing what to do, then dined, then slept for a long time.

     "How foolish and restless it all is," he thought, waking up and looking at the dark windows; It was already evening. "So I slept well for some reason. What am I going to do now at night?"

     He sat on a bed covered with a cheap gray blanket like a hospital blanket, and teased himself with annoyance:

     "Here is a lady with a dog... Here is an adventure for you... So sit here."

     In the morning, at the station, a poster with very large letters caught his eye: "Geisha" was playing for the first time. He remembered this and went to the theater.

     "It is very possible that she is at the first performances," he thought.

     The theater was full. And here, as in all provincial theaters in general, there was a fog higher than the chandelier, the gallery was noisily disturbed; in the first row before the start of the performance, local dandies stood with their hands behind them; And here, in the governor's box, in the first place sat the governor's daughter in Boa, and the governor himself modestly hid behind the curtain, and only his hands were visible; The curtain was shaking, the orchestra was tuning for a long time. All the time the audience entered and took their seats, Gurov greedily searched with his eyes.

     Anna Sergeevna also entered. She sat down in the third row, and when Gurov looked at her, his heart sank, and he understood clearly that there was no nearer, dearer, and more important person in the whole world for him now; she, lost in the provincial crowd, this little woman, unremarkable, with a vulgar lorgnette in her hands, now filled his whole life, was his sorrow, his joy, the only happiness he now desired for himself; And to the sounds of a bad orchestra, shoddy philistine violins, he thought about how beautiful she was. I thought and dreamed.

     Together with Anna Sergeyevna, a young man with small whiskers, very tall and stooped, entered and sat down beside him; he shook his head at every step and seemed to bow constantly. Probably it was her husband, whom she had then called a lackey in Yalta, in a fit of bitter feeling. And indeed, there was something modest in his long figure, in his whiskers, in his small bald head, he smiled sweetly, and in his buttonhole glittered some kind of learned badge, like a servant's number.

     In the first intermission, her husband went to smoke, she remained in the chair. Gurov, who was also sitting in the stalls, went up to her and said in a trembling voice, smiling forcibly:

     — Hello.

     She looked at him and turned pale, then looked again with horror, not believing her eyes, and clasped her fan and lorgnette tightly in her hands, evidently struggling to keep herself from fainting. Both were silent. She was sitting, he was standing, frightened by her embarrassment, not daring to sit next to her. Tuned violins and flute began to sing, it suddenly became frightening, it seemed that they were watching from all the boxes. But then she got up and walked quickly to the exit; he followed her, and both walked senselessly, along the corridors, up and down the stairs, going up and down, and before their eyes flashed before their eyes some people in judge's, teacher's, and appanage uniforms, all with badges; Ladies flashed by, fur coats on hangers, a through wind blew, blowing the smell of tobacco cigarette butts. And Gurov, whose heart was beating violently, thought:

     "Oh, my God! And why these people, this orchestra..."

     And at that moment he suddenly remembered how that evening at the station, after seeing Anna Sergeevna off, he had said to himself that it was all over and they would never see each other again. But how far it was from the end!

     On the narrow, gloomy staircase, where "Entrance to the Amphitheater" was written, she stopped.

     "How you frightened me! She said, breathing heavily, still pale and stunned. "Oh, how you frightened me! I'm barely alive. Why did you come? Why?

     "But understand, Anna, understand," he said in a low voice, hurrying. "I beg you, understand...

     She looked at him with fear, with pleading, with love, she looked intently in order to keep his features more firmly in her memory.

     "I'm suffering so much!" She went on, not listening to him. "All the time I thought only of you, I lived with thoughts of you. And I wanted to forget, to forget, but why, why did you come?

     Higher up, on the landing, two schoolboys were smoking and looking down, but Gurov did not care, he drew Anna Sergeevna to him and began to kiss her face, cheeks, and hands.

     "What are you doing, what are you doing!" She said in horror, pushing him away from her. "You and I have gone mad. Leave today, leave now... I adjure you to all the saints, I beg you... They're coming here!

     Someone was walking up the stairs.

     "You must go," Anna Sergeevna went on in a whisper. "Do you hear, Dmitri Dmitrievitch?" I will come to you in Moscow. I have never been happy, I am now unhappy and never, never will I be happy, never! Don't make me suffer any more! I swear, I will come to Moscow. And now let's part! My dear, kind, dear, let's part!

     She shook hands with him, and began to descend quickly, looking back at him, and it was evident from her eyes that she was not really happy. Gurov stood for a while, listened, then, when everything calmed down, he found his coat hanger and left the theater.

     

IV

     

     And Anna Sergeevna began to come to him in Moscow. Once every two or three months she left S. and told her husband that she was going to consult the professor about her female illness, and her husband believed and did not believe it. Arriving in Moscow, she stayed at the Slavianski Bazaar and immediately sent a man in a red hat to Gurov. Gurov went to see her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

     One day he went to see her in this way on a winter morning (the messenger had been with him the night before, and had not found him). His daughter was walking with him, whom he wanted to take to the gymnasium, it was on the way. Coarse wet snow was falling.

     "It's three degrees Celsius now, and meanwhile it's snowing," Gurov said to his daughter. "But this heat is only on the surface of the earth, while in the upper layers of the atmosphere the temperature is completely different.

     "Dad, why is there no thunder in winter?"

     He explained this too. He talked and thought that he was going on a date and not a single living soul knew about it and probably never would know. He had two lives: one was obvious, which was seen and known to everyone who needed it, full of conventional truth and conditional deception, completely similar to the life of his acquaintances and friends, and the other was secret. And by some strange coincidence, perhaps accidental, everything that was important to him, interesting, necessary, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, that constituted the seed of his life, came secretly from others, yet everything that was his lie, his shell in which he hid to hide the truth, such as his service in the bank,  The arguments in the club, his "inferior race," his going to anniversaries with his wife—all this was obvious. And he judged others by himself, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that each person had his real, most interesting life under the cover of mystery, as under the cover of night. Every personal existence is kept secret, and perhaps this is partly why the cultured man is so nervous about respecting personal secrets.

     After seeing his daughter off to the gymnasium, Gurov went to the "Slavianski Bazaar". He took off his fur coat downstairs, went upstairs and knocked softly at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, tired of the journey and waiting, had been waiting for him since the previous evening; she was pale, looking at him and not smiling, and as soon as he came in she was already on his breast. As if they had not seen each other for two years, their kiss was long, long.

     "Well, how do you live there?" he asked. "What's new?"

     "Wait, I'll tell you now..." I can't.

     She could not speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed the handkerchief to her eyes.

     "Well, let him cry, and I'll sit for a while," he thought and sat down in an armchair.

     Then he rang the bell and told him to have tea; And then, when he drank tea, she still stood with her back to the window... She wept with excitement, with the sorrowful realization that their life had turned out so sadly; they are seen only secretly, they hide from people like thieves! Isn't their life broken?

     "Well, stop it!" he said.

     It was obvious to him that this love of theirs would not end soon, no one knew when. Anna Sergeyevna became more and more attached to him, adored him, and it would have been inconceivable to tell her that all this must have an end someday; and she wouldn't believe it.
